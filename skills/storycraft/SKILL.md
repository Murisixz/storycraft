# Storycraft

Stories move people when events cause each other, when someone wants something badly and pays for it, and when the
audience is always hoping, fearing, wondering, or knowing something a character doesn't. Mood without that machinery
(mysterious imagery, sad music, a random twist) feels empty. This skill installs the machinery and adapts it to any
genre and form, without forcing one formula.

## How to work
1. **Identify the mode** from the request (table below). Requests can combine modes; do them in order.
2. **Read the brief:** form and length, genre/tone, audience, what already exists, and hard constraints. If a missing
   answer would change the whole design (e.g., novel vs 60-second video), ask up to 2–3 short questions; otherwise state
   your assumptions in one line and proceed.
3. **Follow the workflow** for the mode. Open reference files only when you need their depth; most requests need one to
   three of them (see "Typical reads" at the end). Don't read everything.
4. **Run the quality gate** before presenting. Fix failures instead of listing them.
5. **Present cleanly:** lead with what the user asked for; put working artifacts (spines, ledgers) in compact tables;
   keep commentary short and specific.

| Mode | Use when the user wants… | Deliver |
|---|---|---|
| **Spark** | ideas, premises, concepts, "what if" options | Distinct premises; for each: protagonist + want, obstacle, stakes, emotional core, the hidden truth or engine of curiosity, and the change it builds to |
| **Architect** | a plot, outline, structure, mystery design, story bible | Story core → ending → hidden story & information plan → causal spine → plants/clues → emotional and pacing passes |
| **Draft** | pages: a scene, chapter, script, short, game beats | Brief scene plan, then the text in the requested form |
| **Doctor** | feedback, "why doesn't this work", fixes, rewrites | Ranked diagnosis with evidence → concrete fixes → revised spine (and revised pages if asked) |

## The story engine
Before writing beats, answer these in a line or two each (template: `assets/templates/story-bible.md`). Every later
choice (which scene, which clue, when to reveal) is made by checking it against these answers.

1. **Who wants what, badly, and why now?** A concrete goal actively pursued; urgency. ("Somebody wants something badly
   and is having difficulty getting it" — Frank Daniel.)
2. **What stands in the way?** Opposition at one or more levels: inner (self), personal (intimates), extra-personal
   (society, nature, institutions). The opposing force should be strong and, ideally, have a will of its own; the
   protagonist should start as the underdog. For serious stories, push the core value past its simple opposite to the
   "negation of the negation" (love → hate → self-hate or hatred posing as love; McKee). See `references/character.md`.
3. **What is at risk?** What do they lose if they fail? If the honest answer is "life goes back to normal," the premise
   is too weak (McKee's risk test). Look for external, internal, and philosophical stakes.
4. **What's wrong inside them?** A false belief, wound, or contradiction that pressure will expose. The best characters
   consciously want one thing and unconsciously need another; their true character shows only in choices under pressure.
5. **What is the dramatic question, and how is it answered?** The main tension of the middle → the culmination that
   answers it → the final tension → the resolution (Howard & Mabley).
6. **What really happened?** The hidden story: true chronology, secrets, and who knows what. Mystery is the gap between
   the told story and the hidden one; you must know the whole iceberg.
7. **Where should the audience end up emotionally, and what will the ending mean?** The theme is an argument the plot
   tests, shown by the final choice, not stated in speeches. State it as a controlling idea: value + cause ("love
   survives when we stop protecting each other from the truth"); see `references/endings.md` §7.

Beat-level engine (McKee): a character takes the action they expect will move them toward the goal, the world reacts
differently or more powerfully than expected, and the gap forces a harder, riskier action. A scene in which
expectation equals result is a dead scene.

## Architect workflow
1. **Core.** Logline + story-engine answers. Choose the form's scale (see `references/forms.md`).
2. **Ending first.** Design the crisis (a true dilemma), the climax (the protagonist's decisive action and an
   irreversible change), the resolution, and a key image. Test: surprising yet inevitable; caused by character, never
   by coincidence or rescue; answers the dramatic question and the theme. Unity sentence: "Because of [inciting
   incident], [climax] had to happen." Depth: `references/endings.md`.
3. **Hidden story and information plan.** Write the true chronology. For each secret, choose when the audience learns it
   relative to the characters: less (mystery → curiosity), same (suspense → anxiety, identification), more (dramatic
   irony → dread, compassion). Every revelation to the audience creates an obligation to stage the character's
   recognition. Depth: `references/information-design.md`; template `assets/templates/information-plan.md`.
4. **Causal spine.** List beats. For each: what happens, what caused it, the link (**therefore** or **but**, never
   **and then**), the value that turns, what the audience learns, what it hopes and fears. Escalate: characters try the
   easiest fix first, then harder, riskier ones, until only an either/or remains. Depth:
   `references/structure-and-scenes.md`; template `assets/templates/causal-spine.md`.
5. **Plants, clues and forward pull.** Plant what the ending needs, early and disguised; pay it off with a new meaning.
   Give every red herring its own true explanation. Add advertising (scheduled events, stated plans) and elements of the
   future (hopes, fears, warnings, promises). For mysteries, fill the clue ledger, run the fair-play check, and walk the
   investigation with the procedure test (each step follows from what the searcher knows and can do). For a whodunit,
   build a master timeline and alibi grid, and make sure the solution eliminates every innocent suspect with
   corroborated evidence, not testimony alone. Depth:
   `references/mystery-and-clues.md`; template `assets/templates/clue-ledger.md`.
6. **Emotional design.** Make the audience care before anything is threatened: a specific want, effort, vulnerability, a
   bond shown in action. Put preparation (including preparation by contrast) and aftermath around the biggest blows.
   Let losses reveal what was loved and come through a character's own choice or flaw. Carry feeling in actions,
   objects and subtext. For heartbreak, use the design sequence in `references/emotion.md` §8.
7. **Pacing pass.** Alternate tension and release, each peak higher; don't let three consecutive scenes turn the same
   way; accelerate toward act climaxes and slow down for the decisive moment; stretch danger, cut dull time; cut any
   scene whose removal wouldn't hurt the main tension, culmination or resolution.
8. **Quality gate** (below), then a **pitch test**: tell the story to yourself in a few sentences per act. Would a
   listener be hooked by the inciting incident, held through the middle, and silenced by the climax (McKee)? Where
   attention would drift, fix the design, not the wording.

## Spark workflow
- Generate more options than you show; keep the ones that are meaningfully different in premise, emotion, setting and
  conflict, not variations of one idea. Never trust the first idea: it is usually the most familiar one. The same goes
  for key scenes in every mode (list several alternatives, choose the truest and least seen; McKee).
- Each premise needs an engine (want + obstacle + stakes) and a hook (an irony, a secret, a contradiction, a ticking
  clock), plus the change it would build to. A cool image is not a premise.
- Note briefly what makes each idea work and what risk it carries. Offer to develop one.

## Draft workflow
1. Make a brief scene plan for each scene (template `assets/templates/scene-card.md`): whose scene it is, their goal,
   the obstacle, the turning point, the value shift, the information shift, the subtext, and the exit beat.
2. Write it. Enter late and leave early. Build beats as action → unexpected reaction → new action. Decide actions
   before dialogue; never write a scene "about what it's about" (put the real issue under a surface task, and let
   characters answer the subtext); let objects and gestures carry emotion; let dialogue do several jobs at once and
   rarely state feelings or theme. The other character in the scene must push too (probe, demand, tempt, resist), not
   just be present. Keep at least one question or fear alive at the end of each scene.
   Last pass: cut any line that explains an image, names the subtext, or states the theme.
3. Format for the medium: prose, screenplay, short-video beat script, game beats (`references/forms.md`). Craft detail:
   `references/structure-and-scenes.md`.

## Doctor workflow
Follow `references/diagnostics.md`:
1. Reconstruct the spine as it stands.
2. Check the failure catalogue: "and then" links, nonevents, passive protagonist, low stakes, weak opposition,
   confusion mistaken for mystery, unplanted twists, false mystery, owed recognitions, coincidence rescues,
   unearned emotion, sagging middle, anticlimax.
3. Rank by impact, prescribe concrete fixes using the author's own material, and show a revised spine. Commit to one
   recommended version of the key scenes; mention alternatives in a line, not as an undecided menu.
4. Say what works, and keep the author's intent and voice.
Template: `assets/templates/doctor-report.md`.

## Quality gate (check before presenting)
- **Causality:** each beat is caused by a prior beat or a character's choice; no "and then" links; no coincidence gets
  anyone out of trouble; coincidence may start trouble, early, and only if it gains meaning.
- **Agency:** the protagonist makes the decisive choices; the climax is their choice under maximum pressure.
- **Stakes:** we know what can be lost at the moment it matters, and we care about the person before they're
  endangered.
- **Forward pull:** at every point the audience has an open question or a hoped/feared outcome; questions get answered
  as new ones open.
- **Fairness:** every reveal was planted (rereading test); every revelation gets its recognition; every red herring has
  an innocent truth; nothing is withheld just to fake suspense; every character acts only on what they know at that
  moment.
- **Ending:** answers the dramatic question, delivers the promised emotion in an unexpected way, and feels inevitable;
  two outcomes stayed plausible until late.
- **Meaning:** the theme emerges from choices and the ending, not from speeches.
- **Scrutiny:** list the objections a sharp reader or viewer will raise (how did they know? why didn't she just...? is
  that physically or mechanically possible?) and make sure the story answers each one, or fix it. For mysteries and
  theory-bait stories, include a short plot-hole check in the deliverable.
- **Form:** fits the length, runtime and medium constraints.
- **Originality:** no recreation of an existing work's characters, plot or text; use principles, not copies.

## Calibration
- Match depth to the request. Ten ideas should be quick and scannable; a novel plot deserves the full Architect
  workflow; a single scene needs a brief scene plan, not a story bible.
- Don't force mystery, twists or tragedy where the user asked for something else. Quiet, low-conflict, or comic
  stories use the same engine at a different temperature (see "Shapes beyond the classic arc" in
  `references/structure-and-scenes.md` and `references/genres.md`).
- Treat templates as thinking tools, not molds; every story is its own prototype.
- **Economy.** Use the fewest devices that do the job: one strong plant beats five, and a story that shows its
  machinery feels engineered. Quiet and short pieces especially need restraint.
- **"No twist" means no secret-driven plot.** When the user asks for a quiet or twist-free story, let the drama come
  from what characters face openly (a choice, a parting, a risk), not from hidden facts revealed late.
- Dark material (death, grief, crime, cruelty) is fine when it serves meaning; avoid gratuitousness; adapt to the
  stated audience age and platform rules.
- When the user wants a story to leave questions open, answer the emotional question and leave only questions with a
  real, designed answer that the clues support.
