# Forms: adapting the story engine to the medium

The engine is the same everywhere (someone wants something badly; obstacles; causality; stakes; managed information).
What changes is the instrument: what can be shown or said, how time is controlled, how long attention lasts, and who
holds the controls.

## 1. What each medium does best
McKee's observation on adaptation: prose excels at **inner conflict** (thought and feeling rendered directly), theatre
at **personal conflict** (dialogue, subtext between people), and film at **extra-personal conflict** (people against
society and the physical world, shown in images). Exploit the medium's strength and compensate for its weakness:
film must externalize inner states into action and image; prose must dramatize, not summarize, the outer world.

## 2. Short story and flash fiction
- **Single effect:** decide the effect first; let every incident and sentence serve it (Poe's 1842 review). Unity of
  impression is the short form's power.
- **Start close to the end** (Vonnegut): open in the pressure, imply the past.
- **One change.** A short story usually turns one value decisively; a flash piece may be a single scene with one gap
  between expectation and result.
- **Iceberg:** know far more than you show; omitted material the writer knows is felt by the reader, while omissions
  born of ignorance leave "hollow places" (Hemingway's theory as quoted in secondary sources).
- **Endings:** a recognition, a decision, or an image that reframes everything; avoid summary epiphanies.
- **Sizing:** under ~1,500 words, 1–2 scenes; 3,000–7,000 words, 3–8 scenes with one dominant line of action.

## 3. Novel
- **Point of view** is the novelist's main information control: close POV gives inner life but limits what the reader
  can know; multiple POVs enable dramatic irony (the reader knows what one character doesn't).
- **Subplots as variations on the theme** (Howard & Mabley): each subplot explores the same human question from another
  angle, with its own conflict and resolution.
- **Chapters as units of tension:** end chapters on an opened question, a turn, or a decision pending.
- **Scene and summary:** dramatize turning points in scene; compress connective time in summary. Use reaction passages
  after big scenes (aftermath) so the character processes the turn and makes the next decision.
- **Interiority is a tool, not a stage:** inner monologue should show contradiction and decision, not repeat what the
  action showed.
- **Long mysteries:** maintain a clue ledger; vary clue types; refresh important plants; check fair play at every act.

## 4. Screenplay (film and TV)
- **Write what can be seen and heard.** Determine the actions first, then activities, then dialogue (Howard & Mabley);
  dialogue alone cannot carry a film. "Pretend the characters can't speak" is a powerful drafting exercise (Mamet's
  memo).
- **Imply the camera through detail** rather than shot directions ("She toys with her wedding ring, then pockets it").
- **Scene length and rhythm:** a feature is roughly 40–60 scenes; vary lengths, accelerate into act climaxes, and slow
  the climactic scene (McKee). One page ≈ one minute.
- **Time control:** ellipsis (cut the boring), elaboration (stretch tension), transitions that link scene tails to scene
  heads through a shared or opposed element (McKee; Howard & Mabley).
- **Preparation and aftermath** are often carried by music and image rather than dialogue.
- **Standard format essentials:** scene heading (INT./EXT. LOCATION — DAY/NIGHT); present-tense action lines in short
  paragraphs; character cue in caps; parentheticals rarely; transitions sparingly.
- **TV:** each episode answers its own question while advancing the series question; acts end on turns; recurring
  dramatic irony (secrets the audience holds) sustains seasons.
- **Short films:** one situation, one turn, few locations; 5–15 pages typical.

## 5. Short-form video (YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Reels)
Constraints: vertical, mobile, often watched without sound or with music only, viewers decide in seconds, and the
video loops. YouTube Shorts can run up to 3 minutes for square or vertical videos (YouTube, Oct 2024).
- **One question, one turn, one reveal.** Compress the engine: want, obstacle, gap, recognition. 3–7 beats with timings.
- **Open in motion:** the first frame should show an effect without its cause (curiosity) or a danger the character
  hasn't seen (suspense). No setup preamble.
- **Story in images and on-screen evidence:** objects, environment, gestures, signs, messages, interface text.
  Environmental storytelling works because viewers piece together "what happened here" (Smith & Worch).
- **Clues that pay off on rewatch:** place 2–4 details that are visible but not emphasized; it must be possible to miss
  some things for finding them to feel meaningful (Smith & Worch).
- **Recognition at the end:** a final image or line that recontextualizes the opening, so the loop rewards a second
  viewing (in a loop the first frame becomes the epilogue).
- **Emotional core in one bond:** two people, a person and an animal, a person and a promise. Show the bond in one
  concrete action early.
- **For game-footage machinima (e.g., Minecraft):** use the game's own systems as evidence: chat logs, death messages,
  item names, signs, statistics, compasses, recognizable mechanics. Viewers who know the rules can verify the clues,
  which makes the mystery fair and debatable.
- **Leave one answerable question** to invite comments; answer the emotional question fully.
- **Design two complete readings.** The first-watch story must satisfy on its own (a clear situation, a turn, an
  emotional ending); the rewatch story adds a deeper or darker truth that the same footage supports. Theory comments
  come from viewers arguing between the readings with evidence.
- **Tier the clues:** *first-watch* clues that carry the surface story; *rewatch* clues that support the deeper truth;
  *pause-only* details (a frame-long name, a background sign, a number on a screen) for the viewers who screenshot.
- **Run a nitpick pass:** list what sharp viewers will object to ("why didn't she just...?", "that's not how the game
  works", "how could he know?") and make sure the footage answers each one, or change the story. Verify every game
  mechanic the story depends on in the exact version you'll film; say which version.
- **Packaging is part of the story:** the title can steer the first reading (an honest genre signal that points
  viewers toward the surface interpretation) but must not lie about what's on screen; keep the description minimal so
  it doesn't explain; in the comments, reward correct theories (likes, hearts) without confirming them, and pin a
  small nudge ("look at 0:04") once theories stall; a strong mystery can set up a part 2 told from the other side.

## 6. Game and interactive narrative
- **Designers are narrative architects** (Jenkins): stories can be evoked (a space calls up a known story),
  enacted (players perform events), embedded (backstory hidden in the mise-en-scène, like a memory palace the player
  deciphers), or emergent (stories arise from systems).
- **Environmental storytelling** (Smith & Worch, GDC 2010): stage spaces with an inherent sense of history that invite
  players to infer events ("What happened here?"); elements should combine into a larger picture yet each carry
  meaning; some things must be missable.
- **Two wants:** the player's want (to explore, master, win) and the character's want. Align them at key moments, or
  create meaningful friction on purpose.
- **Agency vs authorship:** hidden stories (backstory, mystery) survive player freedom best; tie reveals to discovery
  rather than to fixed timing.
- **Choices must cost something** and echo later; otherwise branching is decoration.
- **Pacing:** alternate tension and release across play sessions; peaks should rise over time.

## 7. Comics and graphic novels
- Readers create the action between panels ("closure in the gutter," McCloud's term, via secondary sources). Put the
  most important events in the gutter when the inference is clear; show the before and after.
- **Page turns are reveal points:** put surprises on the first panel of a left-hand (verso) page.
- Panel size controls time: wide or silent panels slow; small, rapid panels speed up.

## 8. Audio drama and podcasts
- Sound is the camera: signature sounds plant and pay off (a squeaking gate, a music box).
- Limit simultaneous voices; identify speakers by name and voice texture; give each scene a distinct acoustic space.
- Silence and offscreen events are powerful; let listeners imagine what they can't see (Henry James's preface to *The
  Turn of the Screw*: make the reader think the evil for themselves).

## 9. Stage
- Unity of place; offstage events reported or implied; the "obligatory scene" the audience foresees must be delivered
  (Archer). Entrances and exits create new "French scenes" within one location (McKee).

## 10. Length and density guide
| Form | Typical scale | Structure density |
|---|---|---|
| Short video | 30 s–3 min | 3–7 beats; 1 question; 1 reveal |
| Flash fiction | < 1,500 words | 1–2 scenes; 1 gap; image ending |
| Short story | 3k–7k words | 3–8 scenes; 1 major turn |
| Short film | 5–15 pages | 1–3 locations; 1 turn; 1 recognition |
| Feature film | 90–120 pages | 40–60 scenes; act climaxes; sequences |
| TV episode | 30–60 pages | 4–6 acts; A/B/C stories |
| Novel | 70k–110k words | 60–100+ scenes; braided subplots |
| Game | hours | levels/sessions as sequences; embedded and emergent story |
