# Horror: dread, the uncanny, and threats with rules

Horror works when the reader foresees something terrible and cannot stop watching it come. It fails when the threat is random, fully explained, or always beaten by a counter-spell. This file gives working methods. Genre values are in `genres.md` ("Horror"); Arabic sentence forms for fear are in `arabic-writing.md` §8; the novels behind the examples are summarized in `novel-studies.md` §12.

## 1. Choose the source of fear
- **The uncanny** (explicable: a person, a creature, a disease), **the supernatural** (a spirit realm, jinn, a curse), or **the super-uncanny** (the reader is kept unsure which). Choose deliberately, and say what the story will never explain (McKee's categories; `genres.md`).
- **Social horror:** violence that the society around it treats as normal. A festival crowd cheering an execution (*Ard Zikola*) frightens through the crowd's joy, not the blade. Keep the celebration and the killing in the same sentence.
- **Personal horror** is about something the protagonist did, wanted or consented to: a pact, a summoning, a guilt. It lasts longer than horror that only happens *to* someone. In *Khawf 1*, every escalation of the hero's fall needs his consent (reading the book, accepting the companion, making the bargain), and the ending turns on his refusal.
- **Give the threat a meaning.** The monster should embody a fear the story is about: greed, forbidden knowledge, a family secret, a faith tested.

## 2. Dread before shock: the familiar made wrong
- **Invert the safest sound or ritual.** The dawn call to prayer heard from *inside* the bedroom, an unseen congregation answering "amen", a brother's double smiling silently in the kitchen (*Khawf 1*). These frighten more than any later battle because they corrupt what the reader trusts.
- **A small sign beats spectacle.** In the middle of a screaming possession, the narrator is disturbed by a mark on the girl's forehead (*Khawf 2*). An empty disc tray, a stopped clock or a missing shoe can carry more fear than an apparition.
- **Absence and silence.** "I could hear only my breathing, my heartbeat and the sound of my own swallowing" (*Khawf 1*, paraphrased). Let a silence follow the loudest moment.
- **Animals and objects acting wrong:** a cat on its hind legs that seems to wave and smile; pigeons arriving by the hundreds at sunset; a camel that speaks and eats embers (*Khawf 2*). Keep the oddity small and concrete.
- **The unseen present through other senses:** a companion who waits outside for three years and is known only as a smell and someone else's laughter (*Khawf 1*).

## 3. The threat's rules: state, predict, test
Dread comes from what the reader can foresee.
- **State a rule, have someone predict from it, then test it.** Include a false negative, and let a sceptic confirm the result. In *Khawf 2*, the narrator predicts that the pigeons will come at sunset; the sceptical brother hears nothing at first, and then says "you see what I see, don't you?". The reader shares the test instead of taking the narrator's word.
- **Let a frightened amateur test the rule before any expert names it.** A youth alternates moonlight and lamplight
  three times; a father shuts the door on the moon and the fever falls (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). The expert's lore then
  confirms what the reader has seen work. It fails where the lore adds rules the tests never touched.
- **Borrow rules the reader already believes** (Ramadan chaining devils, Mecca barred to them, the hour of answered prayer, protective recitation; *Khawf 1*). They need no exposition and carry moral weight. Then honour them: apply them consistently, and test them.
- **Contracts and pacts:** state the terms early, and let the antagonist exploit a clause later ("the sheikh only" → an abdication; *Khawf 2*). Never let the terms blur when the bargain is struck. A scene whose stakes the reader cannot reconstruct loses its horror (*Khawf 1*'s deal with Dajn).
- **Protective systems should fail through the one vulnerability you flagged.** Salt, rice, scripture and a rooster all work as promised in *Khawf 2*. The breach is the victim's own wavering will, which was planted earlier. A new rule invented at the moment of failure reads as cheating.
- **Keep "real or imagined?" fair.** Test the presence by the world's own rules, record each result openly (a test that
  fails; an absence when the rules say it should be absent), and answer the question with a rule planted early
  (*Khawf 3* sustains this for 225 pages).
- **A supernatural rescue before the climax should carry an open price** stated in binding language: a debt, a blood-money, a vow. Then
  make it felt. A price paid offstage is no price. At the climax, the protagonist's choice decides (§8).
- **Don't give the hero a counter-rule for everything.** If every threat has a ready remedy, danger only returns through human lapses, and dread drains away (*Khawf 2*). Let some rules cost the hero, or have no answer.

## 4. Escalation without losing dread
- **Keep the stakes personal** when the story widens into battles, courts or cosmologies. Dread dissolves when the threat stops touching someone we know. Keep a domestic stake in view: the shroud already bearing the hero's name; the brother saying "don't lie to me" (*Khawf 1*). *Khawf 1* drifts from bedroom horror into dark-fantasy intrigue and loses its fear.
- **Make the body the meter of proximity:** tight chest at the door, headache at the threshold, collapse inside (*Khawf 2*). The cost must persist into later scenes.
- **Documents with a blank, then an inventory:** five voice messages, the fourth silent; then the hero walks a shed and
  lists what he finds (*Khawf 3*). Escalation through records lets the reader do the imagining.
- **An object with a rule:** give a mundane object a rule, a use and an echo (a cracked glass that must never be drunk
  from), and let the climax repeat the use at higher stakes.
- **Offstage horror delivered twice:** first as broken bulletins to a helpless listener whose life depends on them, then as a coherent account (*Khawf 1*'s battle reported by a medium while the hero's shroud lies ready).
- **Ration lore.** Taxonomies of demons, statistics of auras and long lectures convert fear into homework (*Khawf 2*'s seventeen-page lecture). Teach rules in action (§3), and keep any explanation to the next line's need.

## 5. Darkness, sound and blocking
- **Block positions before the light fails:** who is where, what is between them, where the door is. Then say what cannot be perceived ("I couldn't see his face as he spoke").
- **Sound before sight:** gears, a creak somewhere, breath, a growl, and only then the fangs in the moonlight (*Wadi
  al-Dhi'ab*).
- **Turn action beats into sound beats** in the dark ("I heard him swallow"). Knocking that rises with the conversation and stops at a renunciation is a scene with no visible monster (*Khawf 2*).
- **The stair timer, the power cut, the candle:** a mechanism that turns the light off on schedule creates a clock.

## 6. Sentence-level horror
These hold in any language. The Arabic forms (the hedge, the escalating «بل», progressive naming with "..") are in `arabic-writing.md` §8.
- **Hedge, plain detail, worst detail last.** "I saw, or thought I saw… a black cat… standing on its hind legs… and I
  think it was smiling" (rendered from *Khawf 2*).
- **Progressive naming:** "a mark… a mark I had seen once in a book… a brand of ownership".
- **Escalate with a correction** ("not a cat — something standing like a man"), then cut to a short sentence of silence
  or absence.
- **A shock is one flat sentence:** subject and verb up front (verb first in Arabic), no ellipsis, no exclamation mark.
- **First-person present for fear in close-up.** A diary page of tiny decisions under a blanket, breath bouncing back
  from a face that may be there (*Khawf 2*'s strongest page).
- **Horror by list:** a run of plural nouns without conjunctions (feet, legs, hands, heads with staring eyes), then
  one flat inference or one number (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*: twenty-nine halves in a pit).
- **Formulae under threat.** A prayer, a protective verse, or a condolence used by the wrong speaker (a devil offering
  "may God increase your reward" at a graveside, *Khawf 1*) turns comfort into menace.

## 7. Horror and belief
- **Respect the reader's faith or culture** while using it. File the supernatural under categories readers know (miracle, jinn, the unseen), and let a sceptic resist it (*Arsis*). Belief is then earned, not imposed.
- **Keep the uncertainty honest.** A narrator who says "you may not believe this" must still give events that could be checked. A frame that calls the story possibly untrue (*Khawf 1* calls itself folklore) works only if the events inside stay concrete.
- **Avoid harm.** Horror built on a faith should not mock it by accident or for cheap effect. If the story criticises belief, give believers their strongest case. Horror that sells cures should not contradict medicine as a matter of fact; *Khawf 2* presents abandoning prescribed treatment as wisdom, which is a real-world harm, not a craft choice. Stereotyped "sources of evil" (a foreign maid as sorceress, a "Westernised" girl as prey) cheapen the fear and the reader's trust.

## 8. Horror endings
- **The threat should be resolved by the protagonist's choice,** at a cost the protagonist keeps paying. A higher power that sweeps every enemy away with no charge to the hero is the most common failure in the novels studied: both *Khawf 1* and *Khawf 2* end this way, and readers feel the gap.
- **When the antagonist must explain, make it a negotiation** in which each answer costs the hero something. A
  question-and-answer with no cost is a lecture (*Khawf 3*).
- **A refusal can be the climax** (refusing the final offer), but the external resolution must flow from that act through an agent established earlier, not one introduced at 97%.
- **An ending can invert the opening's terror** with the same objects (the lamp, the axe, the fangs) and a new rule:
  "it will not harm us unless we harm it" (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). Terror becomes trust.
- **Leave residue:** a mark that stays, a name the reader is asked to remember, a door that might open again. *Khawf 2* extends the story's price to whoever reads the victim's name. That works only because the price was set inside the story first.
- **Give each major death its weight:** a scene; or, if the death itself stays unnarrated, a life fully staged before and an aftermath staged after (novel-studies.md §8). Two principal deaths in one line each, with neither (*Khawf 1*), feel like bookkeeping.

## 9. Failure patterns and repairs
| Pattern | Symptom | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Spectacle without dread | big set pieces, no fear | return to the familiar made wrong (§2); a small sign; silence |
| Lore dump | pages of taxonomy and statistics | teach rules in action; let the next line use the explanation |
| The universal remedy | the hero always has the counter-spell | rules that cost, or have no answer; a flagged vulnerability |
| Dread lost to politics | the cosmology widens, fear thins | a domestic stake in every escalation; the body as meter |
| Blurred bargain | the reader cannot say what was promised | state the terms plainly; withhold motives, not stakes |
| Fainting as a scene cut | the hero blacks out whenever a scene is hard to stage | stage the moment, or cut on an action |
| Deus ex machina | an outside power ends the threat | the hero's choice decides; a cost that stays |
| Disrespect or harm | stereotypes, medical misinformation | an antagonist with a meaning; check real-world claims |

## 10. Checklist
- [ ] The source of fear is chosen, and what will never be explained is decided.
- [ ] At least one familiar sound, ritual or room is made wrong before any spectacle.
- [ ] Each rule the climax depends on is stated, predicted from and tested (with a sceptic where belief is in question). What the story will never explain stays unexplained.
- [ ] Protective systems fail only through a planted vulnerability. Some threats have no easy counter.
- [ ] Every escalation keeps a domestic stake and a bodily cost visible.
- [ ] Lore is rationed to what the next line uses.
- [ ] Shock sentences are flat; dread sentences put the worst detail last.
- [ ] The climax turns on the protagonist's choice, with a lasting cost and some residue.
- [ ] Faith, culture and medicine are used without mockery or misinformation; no stereotyped sources of evil.
