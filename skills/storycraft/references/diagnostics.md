# Diagnostics: finding and fixing what's wrong with a story

Use this in Doctor mode, and as the final quality pass on anything you generate. Diagnose structure before scenes and
scenes before sentences; most "boring" or "flat" stories have a structural cause.

## Contents
1. Doctor procedure
2. Reconstruct the spine (what to extract first)
3. Failure catalogue (symptom → cause → fix)
4. Scene-level questions
5. Report format

---

## 1. Doctor procedure
1. **Read for intent.** What is the author trying to make the audience feel and think? Respect it.
2. **If there is outside feedback, test it first:** split it into claims and check each against the text, with a
   verdict and evidence (revision.md §2). Don't accept or dismiss criticism wholesale.
3. **Reconstruct the spine** (§2) as the draft currently stands, even where it's implicit or missing.
4. **Run the failure catalogue** (§3). Note the evidence (quote, point to the beat, or count).
5. **Rank by impact:** scope and core (want, stakes, causality, ending) → information (leaks, reveals) → clarity
   (orientation, reference, links) → structure (acts, pacing) → staging (scene vs summary) → people → world → prose.
6. **Prescribe specific fixes** using the author's own material: show the changed beat, the new plant, the moved
   reveal, the staged scene. Recommend one; mention the alternative in a line when the choice depends on taste.
7. **Show a revised spine** (beats with causal links and treatment) when the problems are structural.
8. **Protect what works:** say what's strong and why, so revisions don't destroy it.
For the repair passes themselves, follow revision.md.

## 2. Reconstruct the spine
Extract, in one or two lines each:
- Protagonist; conscious want; deeper need or contradiction; what they stand to lose (McKee's risk test: if they fail,
  does life simply go back to normal?).
- Opposition (people, self, world); is it strong enough?
- Inciting incident; main tension (the long middle's question); culmination; resolution.
- The hidden story (if any): what really happened; who knows what when.
- Beat chain, with the link between each pair labeled **therefore**, **but**, or **and then**.
- Value turns per beat (+/−); where the audience hopes and fears.
- Ending: the decisive choice; the change; the final image.
- Unity sentence: "Because of [inciting incident], [climax] had to happen."

## 3. Failure catalogue (symptom → cause → fix)

**Core**
- *"Nothing happens" / flat* → nonevents: scenes where the value at stake doesn't change (McKee) or expectation equals
  result → make each scene turn a value; open a gap between what the character expects and what happens.
- *Episodic, "and then" chain* → events follow in time but don't cause each other (Forster: story vs plot; Parker &
  Stone's therefore/but test) → relink beats so each is caused by a prior choice or event; cut or merge beats that can't
  be linked.
- *Passive protagonist* → no conscious want, or events happen *to* them (others' choices or chance drive the plot) →
  give a concrete, urgent goal; let them try the easiest fix first and escalate; make the climax their choice. (A
  passive-seeming lead works only if they care intensely and active forces press on them, as with Hamlet; Howard &
  Mabley.)
- *Low stakes* → failure costs little; the risk test fails → raise what can be lost; connect it to what they love;
  add a time frame.
- *Hassles, not conflicts* → obstacles don't block an established want (a flat tire with nothing at stake; Howard &
  Mabley) → establish the want and the deadline first.
- *Weak opposition* → antagonism too feeble, so victory is cheap (McKee: a protagonist and story can only be as
  compelling as the forces of antagonism) → strengthen the opposing will; make it attack the protagonist's weakness.
- *Trivial or one-note* → the story only reaches the simple opposite of its core value (love vs hate, truth vs lie) →
  push to the negation of the negation (self-hate, hatred posing as love, self-deception) somewhere in the last act
  (McKee; see character.md §6).
- *Two stories fighting* → the protagonist pursues two unrelated main objectives, breaking unity (Howard & Mabley's
  suspension bridge) → choose one spine; make the other a subplot that varies the theme.

**Scope and staging**
- *Summarized life / "it reads like an outline with beautiful sentences"* → the story's material (decades, several arcs,
  a political world, a long deception) is bigger than its length, so summary carries it → narrow the window, re-time
  with a frame or braided lines, or move to a longer form (revision.md §4; scope-budget template).
- *Reported climaxes* → the most dramatic events (the crossing, the confession, the betrayal) reach the reader only as
  somebody's account → stage them, or make the telling a scene with its own conflict (scene-and-summary.md §6).
- *Decades in a paragraph* → long spans have no anchoring instances → choose a few moments that each change something
  and stage them; keep the summary between them (scene-and-summary.md §5).
- *Revelation without consequences* → after the big reveal, the characters only feel; nobody acts differently → stage
  at least one consequence scene in which someone makes a costly choice because of what they now know.

**Information and reveals**
- *Reveal that only confirms* → the reader guessed long before (leaks from interludes, imagery, symmetry, repeated
  emphasis, a second voice that knows) → run the leak audit (revision.md §6); restore the mystery, convert it to
  deliberate irony, or move the payload to a decision.
- *Mixed strategy* → the same secret is run as a mystery and given away as irony → choose one per stretch; if the
  reader knows, write scenes in which characters act on the false belief.
- *Explanation-heavy reveal* → the revelation scene carries the backstory as a monologue → distribute the backstory
  earlier as unexplained effects; keep in the reveal only the fact that turns the scene; give the explainer a goal in
  the telling (information-design.md §7).
- *Confusing, not intriguing* → the audience doesn't know the situation, the want, or the stakes, or can't tell what
  is unknown → clarify those; name the open question; hide only the specific fact (clarity-and-orientation.md §2, §11).
- *No suspense* → the danger is hidden from the audience → show the bomb; add a clock.
- *Predictable* → only one plausible outcome → strengthen the alternative; keep two outcomes alive.
- *Unearned twist / "where did that come from?"* → reveal not planted, or depends on information withheld unfairly →
  plant earlier, disguise fairly, pass the rereading test.
- *Twist that cancels the story* ("it was a dream", a stranger did it) → make the solution reinterpret, not erase.
- *False mystery* → ordinary exposition withheld to fake suspense; flash-forward teases followed by slow backstory
  (McKee) → give the information and find real dramatic questions.
- *Revelation without recognition* → the audience learns a secret the affected character never confronts → stage the
  recognition (Frank Daniel's obligation).
- *Empty mystery* → intriguing questions with no designed answer → write the hidden story; tie the answer to a
  character's want and fear.
- *Investigation by luck* → clues arrive by chance or the searcher's next step doesn't follow from what they know →
  apply the procedure test ("with what they know and can do, what would they do next?"); fit the crime to the
  evidence the searcher can reach (see mystery-and-clues.md §5).
- *Info dump* → exposition delivered as lecture → cut, postpone, dramatize in conflict, eyedropper not ladle.
- *Courier investigation* → witnesses volunteer the next lead, documents appear intact, and no discovery costs the
  searcher anything → run the discovery audit; give witnesses reasons to withhold; let one key discovery come from
  the searcher's own observation (mystery-and-clues.md §18).
- *Accidental irony* → one character carries three or more suspicion markers before any rival exists, so the reader
  solves the case in the first scenes → commit to irony and cut the false-lead machinery, or spread the markers and
  feed a real rival (mystery-and-clues.md §18).
- *Starved false lead* → a false lead is cleared within a scene of becoming plausible → make the reader believe it, let
  it change an action and cost something before an earned discovery kills it (mystery-and-clues.md §18).

**Plausibility**
- *Coincidence rescue* → chance gets a character out of trouble, or resolves the ending (deus ex machina) → make the
  protagonist's choices decide it; coincidences may start trouble early, never solve it late (McKee: none after the
  midpoint).
- *Broken rules* → the world's established rules are violated for convenience (Howard & Mabley) → honor the rules or
  establish the exception early.
- *Puppet characters* → characters act to serve the plot, not their own wants (a secondary character who knows they're
  secondary) → give every significant character their own objective.
- *Barrier with holes* → the plot needs people unable to talk, meet, leave or learn, but obvious channels (a relative
  abroad, an institution, a go-between) are never closed → run the channel audit; show enforcement at least once
  (world-and-society.md §4).
- *Abstract world* → unnamed war, country and institutions; rules stated, not lived → specify, or commit to fable and
  stop the plot depending on logistics (world-and-society.md §6).
- *Convenient institution* → a court, police force or hospital accepts what the plot needs after one mild question →
  show the habit that makes it normal, the objectors and why they stay quiet, the protagonist's risk, and one forceful
  push-back, or a visible choice not to look (world-and-society.md §7).
- *Undated stakes* → a choice is weighed against a law or policy that depends on a year the story never fixes → date
  the world; show the consequence through a known case (world-and-society.md §7).
- *Mechanism that fails scrutiny* → the plot turns on a linguistic, legal, technical or magical detail that an expert
  would reject, or that stops mattering once others confirm the wrong version → fill the mechanism ledger; find what
  keeps the error alive (mystery-and-clues.md §19).

**People**
- *Function characters* → each person exists to do one plot job (the wise mentor, the loyal friend, the
  comic neighbour) → give them a life beyond the plot, self-interest and one surprising but true action (character.md
  §8, §11).
- *Saintly ally* → a good character who is never petty, jealous, tired or wrong → give a competing want and a moment of
  friction that costs something.
- *Starved secret-keeper* → the character carrying the secret, often the most interesting one, gets no scenes of
  their own → give them pressure, justification and cost on the page (character.md §10).
- *One voice for everyone* → all speakers share the narrator's polished register → voice test; rebuild voices from
  diction, rhythm, what each notices and how each dodges (voice-and-dialogue.md §2).
- *No friction* → nobody argues, refuses or misunderstands; conflict is only with circumstances → let allies want
  different things and say so.
- *Asserted sacrifice* → a character gives up years or safety for others on the strength of a climactic speech → stage
  earlier scenes where the attachment acts and costs; mix the motives; show what the sacrifice damages (character.md §12).
- *Stage-clearing professional* → the lawyer, officer or doctor exists to be impatient or absent so the plot can move
  → give a competence, an interest or a suppressed doubt (character.md §9).

**Emotion**
- *We don't care* → jeopardy before attachment; no empathy point → build the bond first; give the protagonist effort,
  vulnerability, conscience, a specific want (see emotion.md).
- *Told, not felt* → characters announce feelings or theme → externalize through action, object, subtext.
- *Manipulative sadness* → suffering disconnected from choice or meaning → tie it to the want, the choice, the cost.
- *Melodrama* → an event bigger than its motivation → deepen the motivation rather than shrinking the event (McKee).
- *Mood instead of turn* → lyrical atmosphere, sad music or weather where no value changes → find the turn; mood can
  color an emotion but never replace one (McKee).
- *Numbness from repetition* → three or more consecutive scenes turning the same way (three sad, three triumphant) →
  alternate charges, or make the third contrast enough to recolor the others (McKee).
- *Empathy drifts to the wrong character* → a rival or antagonist is more dimensional than the protagonist (McKee's
  *Blade Runner* example) → deepen the protagonist's contradiction or scale back the rival.
- *Preachy* → the story's argument is slanted; the counter-idea gets no real voice; characters state the theme → give
  the opposing side its strongest case and let the climax prove the meaning (McKee; Truby via outline).
- *Monotone* → same intensity throughout → alternate tension and release; preparation by contrast.

**Structure and pacing**
- *Slow start* → long exposition before the inciting incident → start closer to the trouble; deliver background in
  conflict later.
- *Sagging middle* → repetition of the same kind of complication; no escalation → escalate attempts (easiest first,
  then harder), add reversals, deepen (personal progression) or widen (social progression), introduce a midpoint
  revelation.
- *Anticlimax* → the climax is decided offscreen, by someone else, or by chance; or crisis and climax are separated by
  unrelated material → stage the dilemma; cut straight from decision to action.
- *Ending unsatisfying* → doesn't answer the dramatic question, contradicts the emotional promise, or needs a speech
  to explain → see endings.md checklist.
- *Too tidy* → in one scene every thread closes, every relationship reconciles, every motif returns, and the loss turns
  into consolation → proportion test and cost ledger; leave the honest residue open (endings.md §10).
- *Closure pile-up* → individually earned echoes all return in the last two scenes → keep the one that carries the
  meaning; move or drop the rest (endings.md §10).
- *Rupture in a line* → the central relationship breaks in one exchange and a departure → give the break a scene,
  including the response of anyone whose choice was overridden (endings.md §10).

**Prose and visible machinery**
- *"Conspicuously literary"* → high density of comparisons, aphoristic one-line paragraphs, symmetrical phrasing →
  count against the benchmarks; keep the best; make the rest literal (prose.md §8).
- *Machinery shows* → every object returns, counts match, lines echo in other mouths, chapter titles are motifs →
  motif inventory; keep the load-bearing one or two echoes; break symmetries; let most details be merely real
  (prose.md §6).
- *Sounds translated* → de-specified setting, generalizing similes ("the way you…"), calqued idiom → specify;
  transcreate other-language editions (prose.md §10; translation.md).

**Clarity and language**
- *"I understand the words but not what's happening"* → accidental uncertainty: links missing at hinges, background
  never given, pronouns resolving to the wrong person → intended-uncertainty list plus a cold read; repair links,
  background and referents first (clarity-and-orientation.md §1–5, §12).
- *Elegant hinge* → a simile or aphorism stands where the reader needed to learn what changed → make the change
  legible (a plain line, an unmistakable act, a primed reaction); keep the image around it (clarity-and-orientation.md
  §6).
- *Missed decisive event* → a first reader learns of a death, arrest or departure only later (from a burial, a mourning
  dress, a changed name), so the scene's consequences played to someone who did not know → decide when the reader
  should know. Then add the channel that fits the point of view and pace, or label the doubt if it is designed
  (clarity-and-orientation.md §6). The fix is a channel, not an explanation of feelings.
- *Translated edition unfollowable* → the translation kept the source's information order and its typographic cues
  (italics, capitals, explicit pronouns, tense) without replacing them → run the translation procedure: source
  analysis, transfer plan, re-anchored drafting, verification passes, cold read (translation.md).
- *Unnatural Arabic in an Arabic draft* → English syntax and attribution habits (tags after the line, long participle
  chains, metaphors calqued word by word), dialect used without a plan, names that read as ordinary words →
  arabic-writing.md.
- *Genre promise broken or clichéd* → an obligatory scene is missing, or delivered in its most familiar form → list the
  genre's conventions; for each key scene list several alternatives and pick the truest, least-seen one (McKee; see
  genres.md).
- *Subplots wandering* → subplots don't vary the theme or affect the main line → connect or cut.

## 4. Scene-level questions
Condensed from Howard & Mabley's rewrite questions and McKee's scene analysis:
1. Whose scene is it (whose want drives it)? What do they want here, and what's in the way?
2. What value is at stake, and does it change from beginning to end? Where is the turning point?
3. Does the audience know what might go right or wrong, and when do they learn it?
4. Is anything revealed? Is a recognition owed?
5. Where were the characters before, and where are they going after?
6. Is there a better time or place for this scene (one that adds pressure or irony)?
7. Are the actions motivated and true to each character's through-line?
8. Is there dramatic irony or subtext? Is dialogue doing several jobs at once?
9. Is there a plant, a payoff, advertising, or an element of the future?
10. Does it contrast with the scenes around it? Is preparation or aftermath needed?
11. If you cut it, would the main tension, culmination or resolution suffer? If not, cut or merge. (Before cutting,
    check that it isn't the only place a character's life beyond the plot is shown.)
12. What does each *other* person in the scene want, and do they push for it in their own voice?
13. Is the key event staged here, or merely reported? If reported, does the telling have its own conflict?
14. Could anything in this scene let a sharp reader guess a later secret too early?

## 5. Report format
Use `assets/templates/doctor-report.md`:
- **What works** (2–4 specifics).
- **Feedback verdicts** (when responding to notes): claim | evidence | verdict | cause | fix.
- **The core problem** (one sentence) and its evidence.
- **Ranked issues** (impact, evidence, fix).
- **Revised spine** (beats with therefore/but links, value turns, reveals).
- **Optional next steps** (what to draft or test next).
Keep the tone collegial and specific; avoid generic advice ("raise the stakes") without a concrete proposal.
