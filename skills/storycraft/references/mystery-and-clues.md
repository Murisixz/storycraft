# Mystery and clues: hidden stories, investigations, fair play, satisfying solutions

A mystery is a gap the audience wants closed. It satisfies when the gap has a real, designed answer that was fairly
available, and when finding it changes what we feel about the people involved. It feels empty when it is only atmosphere
(questions with no designed answers) or when the answer arrives from nowhere.

## 1. Two stories: the hidden story and the told story
- Every mystery contains two stories: one told roughly in order (the investigation, the search, the present), and one
  told radically out of sequence (the events that caused it) (Jenkins on detective fiction). The audience reconstructs
  the second from traces in the first.
- Forster: a plot with a mystery in it **suspends the time-sequence** ("The queen died, no one knew why, until...").
  The writer knows the full chronology; the reader experiences effects before causes. McKee calls this two-way
  curiosity: show the discovery or consequence, withhold the act or cause.
- The told story still needs its own engine: someone wants something (to find the truth, to clear a name, to protect
  someone, to escape), faces obstacles, takes risks, and changes. A mystery whose investigator has nothing at stake is
  a puzzle, not a story. Pure whodunits are "cool entertainments for the mind" (McKee): the detective is admired, not
  feared for. To add heart, put the searcher in real jeopardy, or make the answer cost them something personal.
- **Keep suspense running beside the mystery.** Mystery alone engages curiosity but not feeling; suspense (what will
  happen next, to someone we care about) carries emotion (Howard & Mabley). In a whodunit the final piece of the puzzle
  can wait for the climax, but the last act should still run on suspense: a dangerous culprit cornered, a second victim
  at risk, the searcher about to lose something. In other genres, solve the main factual mystery by the end of the
  second act and let the final act run on suspense. A **mystery of meaning** (what did her last word mean? why did he
  keep the sled?) can last to the final image when its answer lands as an emotional metaphor (Rosebud).

## 2. Kinds of mystery
- **Closed mystery (whodunit):** the crime happens unseen; at least three plausible suspects; red herrings; the true
  culprit withheld until the climax (McKee's convention). Satisfies through deduction and reversal.
- **Open mystery (howcatchem):** the audience sees the crime and knows the culprit; clues replace suspects. The crime
  looks perfect but has one fatal flaw of logic that the investigator finds (McKee's "Columbo" form). The audience "runs
  from behind": we want the detective to be masterful.
- **Howdunit / whydunit:** the who is known or obvious; the mystery is the method or the motive. The why is the richest
  vein for heartbreak.
- **Hidden-opponent thriller:** the threat is known but the opposition is not. Build a hierarchy of opponents with the
  most dangerous one hidden at the top, hide each one's true agenda, and reveal the structure piece by piece at an
  increasing pace; start the protagonist against an obvious opponent and let attacks from the hidden one surface
  (Truby, via Fraim's outline). In detective stories the mystery itself stands in for the absent opponent.
- **Personal mystery:** no detective. A family secret, a vanished person, an unexplained object, a past that doesn't add
  up. The searcher's relationship to the answer is the emotional engine.
- **Environmental mystery:** the evidence is a place ("What happened here?"). Common in games and short video.
- **Mixed forms:** a story can start closed and turn open (Chinatown becomes "how will he stop him?" once the culprit is
  exposed at the act-two climax; McKee), or parody the form (*The Usual Suspects* turns "who did it?" into "nobody did
  it").

## 3. Writing the hidden story first
Before plotting clues, write the **true chronology** as plain cause and effect:
1. The precipitating situation (relationships, pressures, secrets) long before the story starts.
2. The decisive events, in order: who did what, when, where, how, and **why** (motive rooted in desire and fear).
3. What each participant knew, believed, hid, and lied about afterward.
4. Physical and testimonial traces each event left (objects moved, marks, absences, timings, records, behavior).
5. What changed afterward that the present-day story will stumble into.
6. **For a whodunit, a master timeline** at the resolution the solution needs (often minute by minute across the
   crime window), giving every character's location, action and knowledge at each point. Build an **alibi grid** from
   it (suspect × time slot: where, with whom, verified by what). Most fatal mystery holes are timeline holes.
Rules:
- Make the hidden story itself a strong story (desire, conflict, a choice, a cost). The answer should be as dramatic as
  the question. The deepest negative is often a hidden backstory fact (McKee on Chinatown), so design the antagonism
  and the reveals together (see character.md on the negation of the negation).
- **Fit the crime to the evidence your investigator can reach** (Beinhart, via a summary): a village schoolteacher and a
  forensic unit find different traces. Decide what kind of searcher you have, then design traces they can plausibly
  find, misread and finally understand.
- Iceberg principle: know far more than you will show; omission of things you know reads as depth, omission of things
  you don't know reads as emptiness (Hemingway's theory, as quoted in secondary sources).
- The answer must be **inevitable in hindsight** (Chandler's rule, as reported: "The solution, once revealed, must seem
  to have been inevitable").

## 4. Suspects, victims, motives and secrets
- Give each suspect: a want in the present story, a plausible **motive**, **means** and **opportunity**, and a
  **secret** that makes them behave suspiciously for reasons unrelated to the crime. That secret is the raw material of
  a red herring and of character depth.
- Every character believes they are the protagonist of their own life and pursues their own goals (Howard & Mabley);
  suspects who exist only to be suspicious are flat.
- Introduce the true culprit early and give them real screen time (Knox's first rule: the criminal must be mentioned
  early), without giving them away.
- Make the culprit's motive human. A killer driven by love, grief, shame, protection or desperation produces the most
  moving solutions (see §12). Give the culprit a strong but wrong justification (Truby, via outline).
- **Define the victim deliberately**: who the victim was sets the emotional chord of the whole mystery (Beinhart, via a
  summary). A victim with their own small drive, a want or a secret project interrupted by death, makes the loss felt
  and supplies the hidden story's motive. The "body" should be a person the audience comes to miss.

## 5. Running the investigation (the told story's engine)
- **Procedure test** (Beinhart, via a summary): at every step, ask "with what the searcher knows and can do, what would
  they plausibly do next?" Each step follows from the last clue, not from authorial convenience. This makes an
  investigation causal: *therefore* and *but*, never *and then*.
- **Scene shape for investigation scenes:** state the problem → move toward a resolution that turns out false →
  twist to the real situation → end on a new, more urgent problem (Beinhart's misdirection pattern, via a summary). This
  is McKee's gap between expectation and result applied to every scene.
- **Escalating, accelerating reveals:** each discovery forces a decision that bends the searcher's plan; each is bigger
  than the last; they come faster toward the end (Truby, via outline). Order revelations from least to most important;
  secrets come last, extracted under pressure (McKee).
- **Nested mysteries** (Howard & Mabley's analysis of Chinatown): set the main tension once the full scope of the
  mystery is established; run a long middle in which exposition arrives as clues and twists, each solution opening a
  new, deeper mystery (Russian dolls); keep the final act short. The true backstory can be simple; the complexity
  belongs to the telling.
- **The first theory fails.** The searcher's confident wrong solution is the investigation's apparent defeat; a new
  revelation reopens the path.
- **Clarity about the situation is non-negotiable** ("Clarity is God," Beinhart, via a summary): the audience must
  always know who is where, who wants what, and what is at stake. Withhold the specific secret, never the basic
  situation. Confusion is not mystery.
- **Knowledge gaps between characters** generate suspense inside a mystery: a searcher who knows less than the culprit
  is in danger; let the audience sometimes know more than the searcher (an audience revelation; see
  information-design.md) and owe them the recognition scene.
- **The searcher's flaw bends the investigation:** pride, grief, prejudice or love makes them misread a clue. When the
  flaw is corrected, the clue reads true, so the solution and the inner change land together.

## 6. Clues: types, double meanings, placement
**Types:**
- **Physical:** objects, marks, wounds, items out of place, missing items.
- **Testimonial:** what people say, including lies (a lie is a clue once caught).
- **Behavioral:** what people do, react to, avoid, or notice. A reaction whose cause the audience misreads is a strong
  clue: in *The Empire Strikes Back* the mentors' worry reads as fear for Luke's safety and is later understood as fear
  for his soul (McKee).
- **Temporal/spatial:** timings, distances, sequences, what couldn't have been seen from where.
- **Negative clues:** something that should have happened and didn't (the dog that didn't bark in the night, from
  Conan Doyle's "Silver Blaze").
- **Pattern clues:** individually innocent facts that become meaningful together.
- **Character clues:** a trait, skill or habit established early that makes the solution possible. Plant every
  capability the climax needs (Howard & Mabley note that Hamlet's swordsmanship is never prepared, a missed
  opportunity).
- **Self-betraying knowledge:** a character corrects someone with a detail they could only know if they had seen more
  than they admit (in *Rashomon*, the woodcutter's "it was a sword, not a dagger"; Howard & Mabley).

**Double meanings (the heart of fair misdirection):** the best setups carry an innocent first meaning and a truer
second one. In Chinatown, asked what he argued about with his son-in-law, Noah Cross says "My daughter." We assume
Evelyn; he means the child he fathered with her. The line is true, fair and misleading (McKee). Setups can be layered
several levels deep, and each payoff can become a new setup.

**Placement rules:**
- Plant solution-critical clues **early**, separate them from their payoff in time, and refresh important ones so the
  audience can recall them (Howard & Mabley on planting and payoff).
- **Calibrate firmness:** memorable on recall, not telegraphed. The classic failure is to overprepare the obvious and
  underprepare the unusual; less story-literate audiences need firmer plants (McKee).
- **Decide per clue: noticed or overlooked?** For a fair-play puzzle, the clue should be seen but misread (§7). For
  suspense or irony, emphasize it when planted (a held shot of the earring in *sex, lies, and videotape*) so its later
  discovery needs no explanation (Howard & Mabley).
- **Distribute** clues so that each act adds both evidence and complication.
- **Pair** each true clue with a plausible misreading (see §7).
- Prefer clues the audience can **see or hear** directly over clues reported later.
- Logic is retroactive: you may invent the startling solution first, then go back and plant its evidence (McKee).

## 7. Hiding clues in plain sight
Craft heuristics for making a clue visible but unremarkable (synthesized from common mystery practice):
1. **Bury it in a list** of similar details.
2. **Place it just before a distraction:** a dramatic event, a joke, a reveal about something else.
3. **Give it an innocent explanation** at the time (a character explains it away, plausibly).
4. **Filter it through a character who misreads it**, so the audience adopts the wrong interpretation.
5. **Make it characterization:** present the clue as color (a habit, a hobby, a joke) rather than evidence.
6. **Delay its significance:** it only matters once a second, later fact is known.
7. **Split it:** show half in one scene, the other half much later.
8. **Let a red herring stand beside it** and draw the attention.
9. **Make it an absence** (what's missing is harder to notice than what's present).
10. **Put it in a true line with a misread referent** ("My daughter").
If the audience starts hunting for the payoff the moment a clue appears, the plant is too emphatic (Howard & Mabley).

## 8. Red herrings and misdirection
- A red herring is a false trail laid so the audience believes or suspects false facts while the real ones stay hidden
  (McKee). Every red herring must have its **own true explanation**, revealed later; otherwise it's a cheat.
- **Best misdirection uses the audience's assumptions:** about genre, about who is "nice," about timelines, about what
  "everyone knows."
- **Character misdirection:** a suspect behaves guiltily because of an unrelated secret (an affair, a debt, shame,
  protecting someone else). The culprit can also misdirect deliberately with true statements.
- **The fake ally:** a character who seems to help but works for the opposition, ideally torn because they come to want
  the protagonist to win (Truby, via outline). Exposing them is a natural mid-story reveal.
- **Structural misdirection:** emphasize the wrong question (who? when the real mystery is why?), or let the
  investigator's flaw (pride, grief, bias) bend the investigation.
- **Never lie in the narration** about objective facts. Characters may lie; the storyteller may not (see fair play).

## 9. Fair play and coincidence
- The audience should have **equal opportunity** with the investigator: every solution-critical clue must be shown or
  stated before the solution (Van Dine's first rule, 1928). The investigator may not rely on clues hidden from the
  audience (Knox).
- **No accidents or unaccountable intuitions** that solve the case (Knox); no culprit introduced late; no supernatural
  solution unless the world's rules established it from the start (Knox's rule against preternatural agencies applies
  to realistic mysteries; in fantasy, set the rules early and keep them; Howard & Mabley on obeying the rules of a
  suspended disbelief).
- **Coincidence:** coincidences that get characters *into* trouble are fine; coincidences that get them *out* are
  cheating (Emma Coats's Pixar rule). McKee goes further: bring coincidence in early and let it gain meaning (ideally
  becoming a force of antagonism); as a rule of thumb none after the midpoint; never use one to turn the ending.
- **Eliminate, don't just accuse.** A fair solution rules out every other suspect with fair evidence, not only points
  at the culprit. Testimony, confessions and mutual alibis are weak eliminators (either party may lie); corroborate
  each elimination with physical, temporal or systemic evidence (a log, a mark, a mechanism, a distance).
- **Knowledge-state audit:** check every character's action against what they knew at that moment (use the
  information ledger). A character who fears what they know to be false, or volunteers evidence that convicts them
  without a reason, is a hole sharp readers will catch.
- **Rereading test:** after the solution, could a careful reader point to where each step was shown? Write the
  solution's logic as a numbered chain and check each link against a planted moment.
- **Honor the subgenre contract:** a cozy plays scrupulously fair with clues; a hard-boiled story promises grit,
  moral compromise and a searcher who pays physically (Beinhart, via a summary); a psychological mystery promises the
  truth will cost the searcher something inside.
- Historical rule lists (Van Dine, Knox) include dated and even offensive items; keep the principle (fairness, no
  cheating), not the letter.
- **No false mystery:** don't withhold ordinary exposition just to fake suspense; no deceptive cutaways (McKee).
- **Logic holes:** close them if you can; check whether the audience can notice at that speed; or have a character name
  the oddity and dismiss it so the audience feels the story knows (McKee).

## 10. Many versions of one truth (the *Rashomon* design)
For stories where witnesses, diaries, recordings or unreliable narrators give conflicting accounts (from Howard &
Mabley's analysis of *Rashomon*):
- **Distort each account by the teller's inner need**, usually to come out honorable, guiltless or loved. Each account
  is internally consistent and contains details only that teller could know.
- **Anchor every version to one fixed time and place and one recurring physical clue** (the dagger) whose role changes
  from version to version and which returns in the frame.
- **Open each account with its claim** ("I killed him") so curiosity shifts from *what* to *how*, *why* and *who is
  lying*. Basic facts can be withheld at first.
- **Let one teller expose themselves** by correcting another version with self-betraying knowledge.
- **Put the apparently neutral account last**, after we have empathy with the participants, and still give it a
  hidden motive.
- **If the factual question stays open, resolve the frame emotionally or morally** (in *Rashomon* the woodcutter's
  choice to take in the abandoned baby) so the audience leaves with feeling, not just doubt.

## 11. The solution and the reveal
- The solution should **reinterpret** what we have seen (true surprise with a rush of insight), not merely add a new
  fact (McKee's true vs cheap surprise). Turning points work as surprise → curiosity ("why?") → insight (the audience
  rushes back through the story) → new direction (McKee). Plant what they will rush back to.
- Build the reveal on **recognition + reversal**: the truth changes the situation and demands a choice (confront,
  protect, forgive, expose, flee).
- **Show, don't lecture.** Use a key object or image to crystallize the solution; limit the explanatory scene to what
  can't be shown, and keep it in conflict (someone resists the explanation; something is at stake while it's told).
  Let characters answer the subtext of a confession rather than its words (Gittes's "We'll have to get you out of town"
  answers Evelyn's real plea; McKee).
- **Ordering:** let the investigator (and audience) reach a confident wrong solution first, then let one planted detail
  overturn it (the gap between expectation and result).
- **A final reversal of understanding** (the audience re-sees the whole story) is the most powerful reveal, but only if
  it passes the rereading test and changes meaning, not just facts (Truby, via outline, cites *The Sixth Sense* and *The
  Usual Suspects*).
- **Aftermath:** give space for what the truth costs the people who learn it.

## 12. Mysteries that break hearts
- **The why matters more than the who.** Ground the culprit's act in love, grief, loyalty, shame, protection of
  someone vulnerable, or a long injustice.
- **Ground any tragic misbelief.** If the motive rests on a mistaken belief (she thought he had killed her daughter),
  show why it was believable at the time and why the character never checked it. Otherwise the heartbreak feels
  engineered.
- **Personal stakes for the searcher:** the answer implicates someone they love, or themselves.
- **The answer reframes a relationship:** the "betrayal" was protection; the "abandonment" was sacrifice; the
  "accident" was a choice.
- **Use dramatic irony late:** once the audience knows, let them watch a character who doesn't yet (the recognition
  scene is the emotional climax). Compound it by giving each party a different partial truth while the audience holds
  the whole, and place the person with most to lose in the scene unaware (Howard & Mabley on *Diner*).
- **Known outcome, suspense on how:** if the ending is announced (a death, a confession, a verdict), the question becomes
  how and why; this is often more painful than not knowing (Beinhart, via a summary; McKee on dramatic irony).
- **The Center of Good shifts:** let the audience's sympathy move to someone unexpected as the truth emerges.
- **Leave the right question open:** the factual case can close while a moral question stays alive (was it right?).

## 13. Small-scale mysteries (short video, flash, games)
- One hidden story, one dominant question, 2–4 clues, one recognition.
- Show effects first (an empty chair set for two; a message never sent), then let traces accumulate.
- Make clues **legible on rewatch:** high-contrast objects, readable text, a distinct sound.
- In games and machinima, use **systemic evidence** the audience already understands (inventory, logs, mechanics) so
  theories can be tested against rules.
- The ending answers the emotional question; it may leave one factual question open if the clues support a real answer.
- Tier the clues (first-watch, rewatch, pause-only), design two complete readings, and run a nitpick pass (see
  forms.md §5).

## 14. Clue-ledger procedure and checklist
Use `assets/templates/clue-ledger.md`:
1. Write the hidden story and the solution chain (numbered inferences).
2. For each inference, list the clue(s) that support it: where it appears, how it's disguised, what misreading is
   offered, and when its significance can first be grasped.
3. List red herrings with their own true explanations and when those are revealed.
4. List suspects with motive/means/opportunity/secret.
5. Mark the moment of solution; verify every supporting clue appears earlier.
6. Walk the investigation with the procedure test: does each step follow from what the searcher knows?
Checklist:
- [ ] Is the hidden story complete and dramatic in its own right?
- [ ] Does the investigator/searcher want something personal and risk something?
- [ ] At least three plausible suspects (for a whodunit), each with a secret?
- [ ] Is every solution step supported by an earlier fair clue (rereading test)?
- [ ] Is there a master timeline and alibi grid, with no contradictions?
- [ ] Is every innocent suspect eliminated by corroborated evidence, not testimony alone?
- [ ] Does every character act only on what they know at that moment?
- [ ] Does every red herring have an innocent explanation?
- [ ] Is the culprit present early and given real presence?
- [ ] Does each investigation step follow from the last (procedure test), with no rescuing coincidence?
- [ ] Is the basic situation always clear even while the secret is hidden?
- [ ] Is the solution inevitable in hindsight and does it change what the story means?
- [ ] Is the reveal staged in action/image, with aftermath?
