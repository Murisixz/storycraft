# Translation: carrying a story into another language (English → Arabic in detail)

A literary translation succeeds when a native reader of the target language gets the same story, mysteries, emotions and voices, and can follow it as easily as the source reader did. Matching sentences is not the test. This file gives the procedure, with English → Arabic worked in detail. A translation still needs the shared craft: the story's information plan, voices, emotion and clarity are designed with the same references as an original. Arabic conventions (voice, register, dialect, attribution, reference, names, punctuation) live in the Arabic language layer, `arabic-writing.md`; this file gives the transfer decisions and points there. The language-general clarity method is in `clarity-and-orientation.md`.

## 1. What must survive, and what must be rebuilt
**Must survive exactly:**
- the plot facts, the clues and their placement;
- the information plan: what the reader knows when, which secrets are withheld, and where they are revealed;
- the voices: who is formal, who is blunt, who jokes;
- the emotional beats;
- the subtext;
- the strategic ambiguities the author designed.

**Must be rebuilt in the target language:**
- syntax, word order and sentence boundaries;
- cohesion devices (connectives, reference, tense and aspect);
- idiom, imagery and collocation;
- dialogue layout and punctuation;
- every cue the source carried through typography or grammar that the target lacks. In English → Arabic: capital letters marking names, italics marking mentioned words, explicit subject pronouns plus topic continuity, the past perfect, and dash interruptions.

**The trap.** A translation can match its source paragraph for paragraph, read naturally, and still be unfollowable. The source's ellipses were bridged by cues the target cannot carry, which shows up as:
- unbuilt links;
- pronouns that resolve to the wrong person;
- English metalanguage;
- background never stated.

Calques are rarely the main cause.

Short pieces (a scene, a poem-length text) can compress stages 1–3 into a few lines. Never skip the inference list or the cold read.

## 3. Stage 1: source analysis
1. **Write the source's intended-uncertainty list** (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §2): what the reader must not yet know, may suspect, should infer, and must have clear, stage by stage.
2. **List every inference the source leaves to the reader.** For each one, decide:
   - *intended* (mystery, subtext, irony) or *accidental* (the source was simply terse);
   - which source cue makes it possible. Examples: an italicized word marks a quotation; a capital letter marks a name; "he" follows the subject of the previous sentence; the past perfect marks a memory.
3. **Mark the cues that won't survive** and plan a replacement for each (§6).
4. **Inventory language-dependent mechanisms.** List any plot that turns on a word, a letter, a form of address, a pun or a misheard sentence. Re-describe it in the target's own grammar and script before drafting (§6.4; the mechanism ledger is in `mystery-and-clues.md` §19).
5. **Flag source defects.** Contradictions, hinges written as ellipsis, and logic that only seems to work in the source language. Fix them with the author's agreement, or record them. Do not silently "improve" the plot.

## 4. Stage 2: glossaries
- **Names.** For each name, record:
  - the target spelling;
  - whether it can read as an ordinary word (سعيد "happy", أمل "hope", كريم "generous", نور "light", منصور "victorious");
  - how it will be marked (`arabic-writing.md` §7, including the accusative trap).
- **Clues, echoes and refrains.** Choose **one** target word or phrase for each plot-bearing word, and use it at every occurrence. For example, if "the blue scarf" recurs as evidence, the Arabic must say «الوشاح الأزرق» each time, not «الشال» once and «الوشاح» later. Verbal echoes that the ending relies on must stay identical.
- **Terms.** For each institution, rank, office, currency, measure and foreign word, decide whether to domesticate it (القاضي، رئيس المحكمة) or keep it foreign, and whether it needs a gloss under the gloss policy (§5).

## 5. Stage 3: the transfer plan
- **Narration register.** For Arabic, standard Arabic (فصحى) at a chosen level: plain modern literary, or elevated. Keep it consistent (`arabic-writing.md` §2).
- **Dialogue register, character by character.**
  - Choose among فصحى dialogue made to sound spoken, light colloquial (a few dialect markers), and full dialect.
  - Decide by the readership and by what each voice must carry (`arabic-writing.md` §2 compares the strategies seen in the novels studied).
  - If readers may come from outside the dialect's region, they must be able to follow the plot from the narration and the فصحى lines.
- **Gloss policy.**
  - Gloss a dialect or foreign word on first use only when the plot or the line's meaning depends on it and the readership is unlikely to know it.
  - Prefer letting the verb or the reply carry the meaning.
  - No footnotes.
- **Dialogue layout.** Choose one convention and apply it throughout. The Arabic novels studied name the speaker *before* the line, with a tag line or a speaker label. Tags after the line, English style, are almost absent (`arabic-writing.md` §4).
- **Punctuation** (`arabic-writing.md` §9):
  - Arabic comma «،», semicolon «؛» and question mark «؟»;
  - one quotation-mark system;
  - a policy on ".." (common in popular fiction, rare in literary fiction);
  - Arabic-Indic or Western digits, chosen once.
- **Readership.** Say what they can be assumed to know: geography, institutions, the source culture. Background the source left to cultural decoding (a stamp, a street, a coin) must be stated in the target if the plot needs it.

## 6. Stage 4: drafting rules, English → Arabic
Draft paragraph by paragraph with the intended-uncertainty list open. After each paragraph, check the six orientation questions (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §1).

### 6.1 Logic and links
- **Carry the logic, not just the sentences.** Where English juxtaposes two sentences and lets the reader infer the link, decide what the link is: cause, contrast, sequence or concession. Mark it if a native reader would not infer it: ف، ثمّ، لكنّ، غير أنّ، إذ، لذلك، ومع ذلك.
- **Don't over-chain.** Connective density in the Arabic novels studied varies widely, so connectives are not the main risk. The real risk is a missing *premise* at a hinge.
- **State the premise behind a comparison or a reason** if the source compressed it (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §5).
- **At hinges,** make sure the Arabic literally says what changed, even where the English relies on a simile or an "as though" clause.

### 6.2 Reference: who is "he"?
- **The nearest-person rule** (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §4). An attached pronoun or an omitted subject tends to resolve to the most recent, most salient person of matching gender and number. English "he" resolves more readily to the previous sentence's subject.
- **A typical failure.** "Sami met your brother at the station. He was carrying a parcel." English readers give the parcel to Sami, the subject. In «التقى سامي أخاك في المحطة. كان يحمل طردًا», the Arabic reader gives it to the brother.
- **Repairs** (`arabic-writing.md` §3):
  - fold the description into the subject;
  - re-name at paragraph starts and after any interposed man or woman;
  - use a distinctive epithet when a name would repeat too often;
  - choose subject-first order deliberately when the subject must stay the topic.

### 6.3 Time and aspect
- **Memory markers** in and out: `arabic-writing.md` §6.
- **Don't calque the English past perfect** once a memory is framed. After «تذكّرت تلك الليلة:», continue in the plain past or with circumstantial clauses, not «كان قد».
- **Future seen from the past:** in narration, «كان سـ + المضارع» («كانت ستسافر في الصباح»). In free indirect thought, a bare «سـ / سوف» («ستسافر في الصباح، ولن تودّع أحدًا»).
- **Free indirect discourse.** English signals it with tense and deixis. Arabic needs a clearer frame: a verb of thinking once at the start (قالت في نفسها، فكّرت), or a paragraph shaped as interior monologue with a clear end.

### 6.4 Mentioned words, letters and language mechanisms
- **Never translate English metalanguage word for word.** "She wrote *we*" becomes «كتبت: نحن», which an Arabic reader takes as her words ("[it was] us").
  - Quote the actual form: «كتبتها بصيغة الجمع: "خرجنا"، لا "خرجتُ"».
  - Or name the form: «بضمير الجماعة» / «بصيغة المتكلّم».
- **Marking mentioned words without italics:** `arabic-writing.md` §9.
- **Re-explain the mechanism in Arabic terms.** If the plot turns on a form that exists differently in Arabic, explain it as an Arabic reader would see it. Cut explanations aimed at English readers ("the *hamza*, a mark that…") where the Arabic reader can see the mark for themselves.
- **Keep perception verbs literal:** سمعتُ (heard) is not فهمتُ (understood), and كتبتُ (wrote) is not صحّحتُ (corrected).
- **Deceptive narration.** A narrator's false statement about the basic situation ("I went to his house" when a double went) is a fair-play risk in any language (`mystery-and-clues.md` §8, §16; `viewpoint-and-narration.md` §6, §9). Keep the source's in-scene signal (a glitch, a sceptic's doubt). Where the Arabic needs more, render the claim as perception («خُيِّل إليّ أنّي…»).

### 6.5 Dialogue
- **Attribution frequency** follows `clarity-and-orientation.md` §7. The Arabic layout (a tag or label before the line) is in `arabic-writing.md` §4.
- **A tag before the line** in multi-party scenes: «قال رئيس المحكمة:» / «- …».
- **Don't split a line around an English-style tag** («غدًا»، قالت. «في الصباح؟»). Use the action beat as the introduction: «قالت وهي تطفئ المصباح:» / «- غدًا. في الصباح؟».
- **Speech inside speech:** use a colon and inner quotation marks, and close them visibly.
- **Keep each character's register as planned.** Don't let the narrator's standard-Arabic gloss leak into a dialect speaker's mouth.

### 6.6 Idiom, imagery and collocation
- **Translate the function of an image, not its words.**
  - "Her heart was in her mouth" → «كاد قلبها يقفز من صدرها»; the calque «كان قلبها في فمها» does not work.
  - "The kettle sang" → «صفرت الغلّاية», not «غنّت الغلّاية».
- **Prefer an active verb with a real subject to «تمّ + مصدر» calques.** "The decision was taken to close the road" → «قرّروا إغلاق الطريق».
- **Collocation check.** For each image, ask: would an Arabic writer pair this verb with this noun? Arabic metaphors need Arabic verbs.

### 6.7 Names, homographs and selective vowelling
Apply `arabic-writing.md` §7 to every name in the glossary and every plot word. The accusative trap (a name that is also an adjective, in the indefinite accusative) is the one translations hit most. Vowel only where a misreading is likely.

### 6.8 Foreign words, places and institutions
- **French, English or dialect phrases in Arabic script** («سوري», «مرسي») need their meaning carried in the sentence, usually by the verb: «تمتم بالإنجليزيّة معتذرًا: "سوري، سوري"». Check them as homographs too: «سوري» also reads «سوريّ», "Syrian".
- **Places known only by a transliterated name** need a role noun once: «سجن…», «حيّ…», «مقهى…».
- **Institutions:** give the target's full term at first mention («رئيس المحكمة», «قاضي التحقيق», «كاتب الجلسة»). After that, «الرئيس» is natural inside a courtroom scene; out of context it suggests a head of state.

### 6.9 Narration scripts (documentary voiceover)
A narration script is translated for the ear and for the picture, not for the page (`documentary.md` §10–11; `arabic-writing.md` §12).
- **Keep the cue points.** Every line that lands on a picture, a pause or a sound must still land there: keep the order of the cue points, and rebuild the order of words inside each row. Re-time the Arabic by reading it aloud at the narrator's pace, and mark where a line now runs long or short against its picture. Then re-cut the picture, or, if it is locked, shorten the line.
- **Rebuild sentences for listening:** event sentences verb-first, construct chains split, one relative clause at a time, and the news at the end of the sentence where the pause falls.
- **Carry the status of every claim.** The source's attribution verbs and hedges («بحسب»، «يقول»، «يُرجَّح»، «لا نعرف») must survive, and so must every marker that separates the record from invention in fiction told as documentary.
- **Write numbers out and vowel names** for the Arabic narrator, as §6.7 and `arabic-writing.md` §12 describe.
- **On-screen text** (names, dates, captions over the picture) is translated with the script and checked against the narration, so the two never disagree.

## 7. Stage 5: verification passes (in this order)
This follows the canonical order in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §12, with translation-specific passes added.
1. **Links at hinges.** Every turn states what changed. Long-distance inferences the plot depends on are echoed, and acknowledged once only where readers miss them.
2. **Background at first need.** Roles, kinship, places, dates and institutions the plot needs are stated where they first matter (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §9).
3. **Orientation.** Scene openings and memory entries and exits are anchored.
4. **Reference and attribution.** For every pronoun and omitted subject, ask "who?". Every line's speaker must be recoverable.
5. **Mechanisms and mentioned words** are expressed in Arabic grammar and script.
6. **Names and homographs.** Check them against the glossary, and vowel only where needed.
7. **Clues and echoes.** Check every occurrence against the glossary.
8. **Dialect and foreign words** follow the gloss policy. The plot survives the readership's limits.
9. **Collocation and idiom,** one sentence at a time.
10. **Punctuation and layout.** One dialogue convention, no false section breaks, nested speech marked.

## 8. The cold read for translations
Follow `clarity-and-orientation.md` §12, with three additions:
- **The checker never sees the source.** Their value is reading as the target reader will.
- **Classify each stumble against the source:**
  - **S (source):** the source is unclear in the same place. Flag it to the author, and fix it in both editions if agreed.
  - **T (translation):** the target introduced it.
  - **S→T (uncompensated):** the source was terse but bridged by a cue the target lacks.

  Fix T and S→T. Expect many serious stumbles to be S→T.
- **Check the repairs against the intended-uncertainty list.** A repair should add orientation, not explain the mystery.

## 9. Failure catalogue with before and after
Short invented examples. "Literal" means a faithful but unfollowable rendering.

| Problem | English | Literal Arabic | Repaired Arabic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Description attaches to the wrong man | The doctor nodded to the porter. He was a small, anxious man. | أومأ الطبيب إلى الحمّال. كان رجلًا ضئيلًا قلقًا. | كان الطبيب رجلًا ضئيلًا قلقًا. أومأ إلى الحمّال… |
| Omitted subject drifts | Nadim came back from the bank with Sami's father. He had not slept. | عاد نديم من المصرف مع والد سامي. لم يكن قد نام. | عاد نديم من المصرف مع والد سامي، ولم يكن نديم قد نام ليلتها. |
| English metalanguage | The witness wrote *we*, not *I*. | كتب الشاهد: نحن، لا: أنا. | كتب الشاهد الفعل بصيغة الجمع: «رأيناه»، لا «رأيتُه». |
| Memory without markers | The room had smelled of paraffin. Her father had leaned over the map. | كانت الغرفة تفوح برائحة الكيروسين. انحنى أبوها على الخريطة. | تذكّرت تلك الليلة: كانت الغرفة تفوح برائحة الكيروسين، وأبوها منحنٍ على الخريطة. |
| Post-posed tags in a three-person scene | "Not tonight," said Rana. "Then when?" said her brother. | Name read as an adjective | Everyone saw Saeed at the wedding. | رأى الجميع سعيدًا في العرس. | كان سعيد في العرس، ورآه الجميع. |
| Homograph near a plot word | She drew the angel from memory. | رسمت الملك من ذاكرتها. | رسمت الملاك من ذاكرتها. |
| Missing premise | Better to lose the farm this year than next. | خسارة المزرعة هذا العام خيرٌ من خسارتها في العام المقبل. | خسارة المزرعة هذا العام خيرٌ من خسارتها في العام المقبل، فالدَّين يتضاعف كلّ شتاء. |
| Calqued image | Her heart was in her mouth. | كان قلبها في فمها. | كاد قلبها يقفز من صدرها. |

## 10. Arabic → English, briefly
- **Reduce chains:** many «و» and «ف» links become separate sentences or subordinate clauses.
- **Dialogue punctuation:** ".." pauses become ellipses only where they mark real hesitation.
- **Speaker labels** ("(أمي):") become normal tags.
- **Names:** transliterate consistently, and gloss honorifics once.
- **Formulae** (الحمد لله، إن شاء الله) are kept where they carry character, and translated where they carry meaning.
- **Restore** what English readers need and Arabic readers infer: the institutions and the calendar.

## 11. What not to do
- **Don't simplify vocabulary** to make a text "clear." Clarity comes from the information plan, reference and links, not from easy words. Mature Arabic prose stays mature.
- **Don't explain the subtext** or the mystery to compensate for a lost cue. Replace the *cue* (a role noun, an echo, a tag, a time word), not the inference.
- **Don't vowel everything,** add footnotes, or flatten dialect into standard Arabic unless the plan says so.
- **Don't let fluency hide logic breaks.** A paragraph can read beautifully and still attach "he" to the wrong man.

## 12. Checklist
- [ ] Source analysis done: intended-uncertainty list, inference list (intended or accidental), cues that won't survive, mechanisms, defects.
- [ ] Glossaries: names (word-readings, markers), clues and echoes (one word each), terms.
- [ ] Transfer plan: registers, gloss policy, one dialogue convention, punctuation, readership.
- [ ] Draft re-anchors referents, marks memories, states hinge changes, and re-describes mechanisms in target terms.
- [ ] Verification passes 1–10 done in order.
- [ ] Cold read logged; stumbles classified S / T / S→T; T and S→T fixed; the repairs spoil nothing.
