# Premise: turning ordinary material into story possibilities

A premise is a situation that forces particular people to act: someone, somewhere, under a pressure that won't let
them stay as they are, facing a question the reader wants answered. It is not a theme ("grief"), a mood ("eerie") or
an image ("a lantern across a lake"). An image becomes a premise once we know whose want it serves, what opposes them
and what it will cost.

## 1. What a premise has to contain
- **A person with a want** that's concrete enough to pursue (to keep the house, to find who wrote the book, to stay
  with someone, to survive the weekend).
- **A pressure** that makes acting necessary *now*: an arrival, a death, a letter, a discovery, a deadline, a closing
  door.
- **An opposition** that pushes back with a will of its own: a person, an institution, a rule, the past, the self.
- **A hidden engine:** a secret, an irony or a contradiction that the story will open. It gives the premise depth
  beyond its surface events.
- **An implied change:** what the situation will do to the person if it runs its course.
- **An implied length:** how much story the situation contains (§7).

## 2. Where premises come from: transforming ordinary material
Most strong premises start from something ordinary and familiar to many readers:
- a school, a house, a marriage, a job;
- a holiday with relatives;
- a museum visit, a book found in a shop, a crime in a suburb.

The premise is made by **one transforming move** that turns the ordinary thing into a source of pressure, mystery or
irony. The ordinary surface keeps the story believable and gives it texture; the move gives it its engine. The studied
novels show many such moves; novel-studies.md describes what each text does, without claiming to know how its author
arrived there.

**Working method:**
1. **List ordinary material** you know well or can research deeply: workplaces, rituals, family structures,
   institutions, places with rules.
2. **For each item, ask** what would make it dangerous, secret, unbearable or ironic for someone inside it.
3. **Apply the moves in §3,** generate several variants, and test them (§5).

## 3. The transforming moves
These are ways to find pressure in ordinary material. They are not plots to borrow; any of them can produce countless
unrelated stories.

| Move | Question to ask | What it tends to generate |
|---|---|---|
| **Hidden purpose** | What if this institution (school, hospital, archive, club) exists for a purpose its members aren't told? | mystery through euphemism and ritual; dramatic irony; moral horror in the ordinary |
| **The governing absence** | Who is missing (dead, gone, never spoken of) yet still controls the present? | comparison, jealousy, testimony, objects that carry a person; a reveal about who the absent one really was |
| **Consequential misreading** | Who misreads something they see, and acts on it? | causality driven by error; guilt; dramatic irony; atonement plots |
| **Displaced narrator** | Who is the least expected person to tell this, and what can they see that others can't? | fresh irony and grief; a narrator who knows more, or less, than a normal one |
| **Closed system with a rule** | Who is trapped together, and what rule or pattern governs what happens to them? | elimination, suspicion, escalating fear; fair-play puzzles |
| **The object with a past** | What object (a book, a letter, a photograph, a key) could lead someone into another person's hidden life? | quest structure; doubled lives; testimony from many witnesses |
| **Private wound × public structure** | Which family grief, love or guilt is trapped inside a war, regime, border or institution? | barriers that are social and internal at once; history pressing on choices |
| **Encoded message** | What if a death, a document or a place carried a message only one person could read? | puzzle chains; pursuit; urgency |
| **Inverted convention** | Which genre convention could run backwards (the detective is the suspect; the ghost is the mourner)? | surprise of form; irony |
| **The test of a virtue** | Which good quality (loyalty, patience, faithfulness) would destroy someone if kept too long? | tragedy through virtue; moral complexity |

## 4. Generating options
- **Generate more than you show.** Produce many variations, then keep the ones that differ in *kind*: different
  people, pressure, setting and emotional destination, not the same idea with the names changed. Never trust the
  first idea; it is usually the most familiar (McKee).
- **Vary one axis at a time:**
  - who (protagonist, narrator, secret-keeper);
  - when (period, season, the hour);
  - where (institution, landscape);
  - which secret;
  - which irony;
  - which opposition;
  - the charge of the inciting event (best to worst, worst to best; McKee).
- **Swap the protagonist.** Ask whose story it is. The most interesting character in a premise is often not the
  obvious lead but the one who carries the secret, the one who pays, or the one who watches.
- **Ask what the situation forces.** List five things the situation would make a person do that they'd never otherwise
  do. If the list is short, the pressure is weak.

## 5. Premise tests
| Test | Pass if… |
|---|---|
| **Engine** | want + opposition + stakes are clear, and the risk test passes (failure doesn't just return life to normal) |
| **Hidden engine** | there's a secret, irony or contradiction that deepens on contact; the hidden story is dramatic in itself |
| **Choice-forcing** | the situation produces dilemmas, not just events; someone will have to choose between goods or evils |
| **Scene-generativity** | you can list at least eight to ten scenes the premise *makes* happen (for a short story, three to five); if you can only list summaries, the premise is a synopsis |
| **People** | at least two characters besides the protagonist have their own stake in the situation |
| **World** | the setting presses on choices, and any barrier the premise relies on survives the channel audit (world-and-society.md §4) |
| **Meaning** | a controlling idea is in reach (value + cause); the ending can say something without a speech |
| **Freshness** | it isn't a recreation of a known work; the move, the setting and the people differ in at least two respects from the nearest famous example (§8) |
| **Scope** | the material fits the intended length (§7) |

## 6. From premise to people
- **A premise is lived through particular people.** Before plotting, write who is inside the situation, what each
  wants from it, and what each was doing before it began: the life the premise interrupts (character.md §8).
- **Give the secret-keeper a stake.** If the premise contains a secret, the person who keeps it has motives, costs and
  a point of view. Premises often get richer when that person's side becomes half the story.
- **Avoid premises that need saints or idiots.** If the situation only works because someone is endlessly patient or
  never asks the obvious question, fix the premise rather than the character.

## 7. Scope: does the material fit the length?
The commonest silent failure is novel-sized material in a short-story budget. The draft then summarizes its decades,
gives each character a line, and explains its climax instead of staging it.

**Count the material:**
- the time span;
- the number of viewpoint characters;
- the characters who need an arc;
- the major reveals;
- the world rules to establish;
- the relationships that must be *shown* to matter.

Then list the must-stage moments (scene-and-summary.md §2) and estimate the words each needs.

**Rules of thumb:**
| Length | What it comfortably holds |
|---|---|
| Flash (< 1.5k words) | one moment, one turn; the past only implied |
| Short story (3k–7.5k) | one main line; a narrow window (hours to days) or a few stations; 1–2 characters with arcs; one major reveal; the past entering through the present |
| Novelette (7.5k–17.5k) | one main line plus one developed secondary life; a longer window or 3–5 staged stations |
| Novella (17.5k–40k) | one main line, a small cast, perhaps one subplot; long spans only as chosen staged stations; room for a secret-keeper's side |
| Novel (70k+) | several lines, braided viewpoints, decades if needed, multiple reveals, a full world |

(The length bands follow SFWA's Nebula categories for novelette and novella.) The books in novel-studies.md show how
full-length novels distribute time and scenes.

**If it doesn't fit, choose one:**
- **Narrow** to the window where the pressure peaks, and let the past enter through what people say, hide and do.
- **Re-time** with a frame, braided timelines or retrospective selection (long-form.md §4).
- **Expand** to the length the material needs.

Don't keep the large material and the small length by summarizing (scope-budget template).

## 8. Freshness without imitation
- **Name the nearest famous example** of your premise honestly, then change at least two of these:
  - the move;
  - the setting and period;
  - the protagonist's position;
  - the nature of the secret;
  - the emotional destination.
  If the premise only works with that example's specific plot points, characters or signature images, it is a copy.
- **Borrow principles, not furniture.** "A governing absence" is a principle. A particular dead first wife, a
  particular housekeeper and a particular burning house are another book's furniture.
- **Let the material you know generate the specifics:** a profession's real procedures, a region's real customs, a
  family structure you understand. Specific knowledge is the best defence against derivative work.

## 9. Premise failure patterns
| Symptom | Cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| A striking image but no story | no want, opposition or cost attached | ask whose want the image serves and what blocks it |
| A synopsis, not a premise | the premise lists events rather than a situation that forces choices | rewrite as person + pressure + question |
| Requires a saint or a fool | the situation needs someone never to act or ask | change the situation, or make the not-acting a choice the story examines |
| Barrier with holes | contact, escape or help is too easy in reality | channel audit; change the world or the barrier |
| The premise is the twist | all the power is in one late reveal; the rest is waiting | build a want and a pressure that work before the reveal; let the reveal change a decision |
| Scope mismatch | decades, many arcs and a world in a short form | §7: narrow, re-time or expand |
| Derivative | the premise depends on another work's plot or characters | §8: change two axes; use your own material |

## 10. Premise checklist
- [ ] Person + want + pressure + opposition + hidden engine + implied change, all stated?
- [ ] At least eight to ten scenes the premise forces (three to five for a short story)?
- [ ] At least two other people with their own stake?
- [ ] Barrier and world plausible (channel audit)?
- [ ] Controlling idea in reach?
- [ ] Nearest famous example named, and the premise different in at least two respects?
- [ ] Material fits the intended length, or the length has been changed?
