# Prose: register, specificity, rhythm, and keeping the machinery out of sight

Prose has one job in a story: to make the reader experience the scene, the person and the change. It fails in two
opposite ways: flatness (nothing is seen, felt or heard) and display (the sentences call attention to the author). The
second failure is the one a craft-heavy design invites. When every image is a symbol, every object returns and every
number recurs, the reader starts reading the pattern instead of the people.

## 1. What prose is for
- **Serve the scene.** A sentence earns its place when it delivers one of these:
  - what the viewpoint character perceives;
  - what they want or fear;
  - what changes;
  - what the reader needs in order to feel the next beat.
- **Perception is characterization.** What a narrator notices, names and skips tells us who they are. Write the world
  through someone's attention, not through a neutral camera, unless neutrality is the effect you want.
- **Prose can't stand in for scenes.** Lyrical summary of events that should have been staged (a courtship, a decade, a
  confrontation) gives the reader beautiful sentences in place of an experience. Fix the scene list first
  (scene-and-summary.md), then the sentences.

## 2. Register: plain, heightened and the switch between them
- **Choose a home register** that fits the narrator and genre:
  - conversational retrospective;
  - formal period voice;
  - clipped thriller;
  - ornate gothic;
  - restrained literary.
  Readers adjust to any consistent register. What they notice is a register that doesn't match the speaker, or that
  never varies.
- **Heighten rarely and on purpose.** Lift the language at the peaks: dream, dread, revelation, ecstasy, the image the
  book will be remembered by. Heighten *around* a revelation, but let the revelation itself be legible: a plain clause, or an act or
  reaction the reader cannot mistake (clarity-and-orientation.md §6). Elsewhere, plain prose builds the credit the peaks spend. A book that is heightened in
  every paragraph has no peaks.
- **Plain is not flat.** Plain prose still has exact nouns, live verbs and controlled rhythm. A plain narrator can be
  devastating precisely because they don't dramatize; the reader supplies the feeling the narrator declines to name.
- **Register can carry character and information.** A narrator's euphemisms, evasions or borrowed official vocabulary
  can show what they can't face or have been taught not to see (viewpoint-and-narration.md §5).

## 3. Specificity and the merely real
- **Prefer the particular** to the general (a brand, a price, a street, a dish, a job's real procedure). Specific detail
  makes a world believable and a loss feel real.
- **Not every detail is a symbol.** Most details in a living world just *are*: the soup was too salty, the bus was
  late. Let most details stay merely real. When the reader learns that every object will return with meaning, they
  start predicting, and the story becomes a puzzle about the author.
- **Load the detail with pressure, not significance.** A detail with "push" implies action or strain (a packed bag by
  the door, an unpaid bill). It is stronger than a detail that announces a theme.
- **Avoid de-specified settings by default.** Unnamed countries, "the capital", "the war" and "a committee" read as
  fable or allegory. Use them only when that is the intended mode (world-and-society.md §6).

## 4. Comparisons: when a simile earns its place
- **Earn it.** A comparison earns its place when it:
  - shows something no literal phrase could;
  - comes from the viewpoint character's own world (a sailor's comparisons differ from a dressmaker's);
  - is fresh rather than stock.
- **Strip the decorative ones.** A comparison that only decorates or tells the reader how to feel can usually go.
- **Watch the generalizing simile** ("the way you know a friend's laugh across a room", "the way people do"). It steps
  outside the scene, addresses the reader and universalizes. Once in a while it creates intimacy; repeated, it becomes
  the author's signature and turns every moment into a little lesson. In the published novels measured below it is
  close to absent, about 0.0–0.1 per 1,000 words.
- **Vary the mechanism.** If the same frame ("as though…", "like a…", "the way…") keeps carrying the comparisons, the
  construction becomes audible. Convert some into direct metaphor, some into a plain statement of the physical fact, and
  cut the rest.
- **Keep similes out of the climax.** At the highest-pressure moment, literal action and sensation usually hit harder
  than comparison.

## 5. Rhythm and syntax
- **Vary sentence length by function:**
  - long, accumulating sentences for perception, memory and complex thought;
  - short ones for impact, decision and shock.
  A uniform rhythm (all short, all long, or all the same shape) dulls.
- **Use one-line paragraphs sparingly.** "He did not wait." "The door stayed shut." Each works once or twice. A dozen in a
  short story become a drumbeat that announces "this is significant".
- **Repetition is a tool with a short half-life.** Anaphora ("She thought: … She thought: …"), refrains and triads work
  when rare and when each repetition changes something. A refrain repeated unchanged becomes wallpaper.
- **Watch for symmetrical sentences.** Balanced antitheses, zeugma ("he took his hat and his leave") and
  chiasmus are ornaments. Two in a scene can delight; a steady supply makes the voice sound composed and literary, not
  lived.
- **Dialogue has its own rhythm.** People interrupt, trail off, repeat themselves and answer the question before last
  (voice-and-dialogue.md §3).
- **Sentences at hinges and shocks:**
  - when a plain line carries the decisive fact, give it its own short clause, never subordinated to a simile. When
    an act or a ritual carries it, keep that act concrete and unmistakable (clarity-and-orientation.md §6);
  - in a sentence of dread, the worst detail can come last: hedge, plain detail, worst detail (horror.md §6);
  - a shock is one flat sentence, with no ellipsis and no exclamation mark;
  - in a physical crisis, give one bodily change per clause, in physiological order, and no adjective of emotion is
    needed (*Ard Zikola*'s collapse).
- **Hand off between paragraphs.** Start the next paragraph on the person or object the last one ended with, or on a
  time word.

## 6. Motifs, symbols, echoes and numbers: the budget
- **Budget by length.** A short story can carry one or two load-bearing echoes, an image or line that returns with a
  changed meaning. A novel can carry several braided motifs, but each needs room and variation. More echoes don't add
  meaning; they show the design.
- **Load-bearing versus decorative.** An echo is load-bearing when its return changes what the reader understands or
  feels. Keep those. Cut, vary or let fall the rest.
- **Break the symmetries.** Life is asymmetrical. If counts match (seven days here and seven days there), the same scar
  appears on two people, and the same line returns in another mouth, keep the one that
  matters and break the others by changing:
  - the number;
  - the timing;
  - the agent;
  - the outcome.
- **Numbers as motif.** Exact numbers give authority ("forty-two stairs"). They also give the author away when every
  chapter is titled with a number and every span is counted. Count only what a character would count, and only when the
  count matters to them.
- **Plant without pointing.** A plant the reader notices as a plant is announcing its payoff (information-design.md
  §10).
- **One device per job.** If the ending already lands through an action, it doesn't also need a returning line, a
  re-staged ritual and a symbolic object. Choose the strongest and trust it.

## 7. Exposition and backstory in prose
- **Prose can summarize, and should, for connective tissue.** The skill is in *which* summaries carry life: summary
  anchored by concrete instances, summary under pressure from a present scene, and summary in a voice with attitude
  (scene-and-summary.md §4).
- **Free indirect style** lets exposition arrive coloured by a character's judgment, which turns information into
  characterization.
- **Distribute backstory.** Spread it across the story, attached to present pressures, rather than delivering it in one
  block, above all not inside the revelation scene (information-design.md §7).
- **Documents** (letters, manuscripts, reports, notebooks) can carry backstory in a different voice. Give them their own
  voice, purpose and gaps, and budget their length: a long embedded document stops the present story.

## 8. Measuring the prose (benchmarks from published novels)
Rough counts make habits visible. They are made with simple pattern matching on extracted text, so they are good for
comparison only, not as standards. The texts are those studied for this skill (see novel-studies.md and sources.md).

| Text | Comparisons per 1k words ("like a/the…", "as if/though", "the way…") | Generalizing "the way you/one/people…" per 1k | Number words per 1k | Dialogue share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atonement | 1.5 | 0.01 | 11.5 | 8% |
| The Lovely Bones | 1.7 | 0.00 | 10.4 | 13% |
| Never Let Me Go | 1.7 | 0.07 | 11.0 | 22% |
| Rebecca | 2.1 | 0.01 | 8.6 | 37% |
| The Shadow of the Wind (tr.) | 2.5 | 0.04 | 11.1 | 32% |
| And Then There Were None | 1.2 | 0.00 | 12.2 | 45% |
| The Da Vinci Code | 1.4 | 0.01 | 11.4 | 31% |
| The Secret History (opening only) | 2.8 | 0.00 | 14.7 | 18% |
| The Thirteenth Tale (opening only) | 1.5 | 0.13 | 11.3 | 2% |

Drafts that editors call "conspicuously literary" often run at two to three times these rates.

How to read it:
- **Ornate isn't the same as tic-ridden.** Even the most ornamented novel in the set ran about 2.5 explicit comparisons
  per 1,000 words. A draft running at 4–5 is probably decorating summary.
- **One construction dominating is the clearer warning sign.** That matters more than overall density.
- **Number words** sat around 9–15 per 1,000 words in every novel. Three times that means numbers have become a motif
  system.
- **Dialogue share varies hugely by design.** The low-dialogue novels (Atonement, The Lovely Bones) carry their scenes
  through dense perception and interiority. A low-dialogue draft that also lacks dramatized interiority is summary.
- **To run your own counts,** search the draft for the markers, divide by words/1,000 and compare. Then read every
  instance of the most frequent construction and keep only the best.

## 9. Distance and voice
- **Narrative distance** runs from far (summary, the historian's view) to close (the moment, inside a mind). Close in
  for decisions, perceptions and blows; pull back for transitions, years and irony. Abrupt jumps in distance can be
  powerful, but drifting without control blurs the scene (viewpoint-and-narration.md §3).
- **Voice is choice plus consistency:**
  - the vocabulary the narrator owns;
  - what they find funny;
  - what they refuse to say;
  - how they move between past and present.
  A narrator with a voice doesn't need ornament to be distinctive.

## 10. Translationese and editions in other languages
- **What it is.** Translationese is syntax and idiom carried across languages: calqued similes, foreign word order, and
  idioms that don't exist in the target language. It makes a text sound translated even when the story is good.
- **It is rarely the main reason a translation can't be followed.** Foreign phrasing usually causes only minor
  stumbles. What breaks comprehension is the information plan:
  - links left to the reader;
  - pronouns that resolved to the wrong person;
  - English metalanguage ("she wrote *we*") laid over Arabic grammar;
  - background facts never given.
- **Transcreate.** Rebuild the sentences in the target language's idiom and rhythm, and re-plan where each fact and
  link appears. The full procedure is in `translation.md`; for Arabic, see also `arabic-writing.md`.
- **Check the original too.** A de-specified fable register in the original ("the capital", "a committee", proverbial
  epigraphs, generalizing similes) reads as translated in *any* language. Fix that at the source.

## 11. Prose failure patterns and repairs
| Symptom | Likely cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| "Beautiful sentences, but I'm not moved" | prose doing a scene's job | stage the moment; cut the lyric summary to a bridge |
| "Conspicuously literary" | rising density of comparisons, aphorisms, symmetries | count (§8); keep the best third; make the rest literal |
| "I could see the twist coming from the imagery" | decorative echoes pointing at the secret | motif inventory; cut pointing echoes; plant through action and character instead |
| "Sounds translated" | de-specified setting, generalizing similes, calqued idiom | specify; cut the "the way you…" frames; transcreate other editions (§10) |
| "Every character sounds the same" | the narrator's register leaks into dialogue | voice-and-dialogue.md §2 voice test |
| "Purple" | heightened register everywhere | choose the peaks; flatten the rest |
| "Flat" | generic nouns, filtered perception ("she saw", "she felt"), no attitude | exact nouns, live verbs, perception through a character with an opinion |
| "Too neat" | every object returns; numbers match | break symmetries; let details be merely real (§3, §6) |
| "Beautiful, but what just happened?" | an image or aphorism stands where a fact or decision belongs | make the change legible with a plain line, an act or a reaction the reader can read; keep the image around it (clarity-and-orientation.md §6) |

## 12. Prose checklist
- [ ] Is the home register chosen, and are the heightened passages reserved for peaks?
- [ ] Is the world specific (names, procedures, objects), unless fable is a deliberate mode?
- [ ] Comparisons per 1,000 words in a normal range, with no single construction dominating?
- [ ] Number words counted only where characters would count?
- [ ] Recurring motifs inventoried, with only the load-bearing ones kept?
- [ ] Symmetries broken except the one that matters?
- [ ] Few one-line aphoristic paragraphs, each placed where it lands?
- [ ] Backstory distributed, not dumped, and never parked inside the revelation?
- [ ] At every hinge (clarity-and-orientation.md §6), does a first-time reader know what changed (what was learned, decided, done) by the point the design intends, before any image is asked to carry it?
- [ ] Other-language editions transcreated, not calqued, with the information plan re-checked (translation.md)?
