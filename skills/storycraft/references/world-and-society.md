# World and society: setting, institutions, history and barriers that hold

A setting is not a backdrop. It is a set of pressures and possibilities that generates events and limits choices:
- who may speak to whom;
- what things cost;
- which rules are enforced and by whom;
- what everyone takes for granted.

"There are no portable stories" (McKee): a specific world produces specific events. A setting the reader doesn't
believe makes every choice inside it feel arbitrary.

## 1. The world as cause
- Ask what this world *makes happen* that another world wouldn't. A closed school, an inherited house, an island cut
  off by weather, a city under a dictatorship, a museum at night: each forces specific encounters, secrets and limits.
- **Let the world oppose.** Extra-personal antagonism (institutions, laws, class, weather, war) is strongest when it
  acts through people with their own interests: the official who enjoys his power, the servant who guards the old
  order, the neighbour who informs.
- **Let the world tempt.** Settings also offer bad solutions: money, escape, a convenient lie the society rewards.

## 2. Fixing the world: period, place, duration, conflict, daily life
- **Fix four dimensions** (McKee):
  - **period:** when;
  - **duration:** how much time the story spans;
  - **location:** where, physically;
  - **level of conflict:** which layers of society and self are engaged.
- **Daily life** is what makes a world felt:
  - what people eat;
  - how they earn and spend;
  - how they travel and how long it takes;
  - how news arrives;
  - what is cheap and what is scarce;
  - what people joke about;
  - what they fear saying aloud.
  Two or three exact details of daily life do more than a page of history.
- **Keep the world small enough to know completely.** Depth in a few rooms, streets or institutions beats a survey of a
  nation.

## 3. Social context: class, family, law, money, faith, gender
- **Know the rules everyone obeys without noticing:**
  - who inherits;
  - who may marry whom;
  - who may be alone with whom;
  - who is believed;
  - who can be punished without consequence.
  These generate the most natural plot pressure, because characters don't question them and readers feel them.
- **Show the rule in action on a person** rather than describing it. A servant corrected for using the wrong door
  teaches the class system faster than an essay.
- **Give the powerless their tactics:** silence, deference used as armour, gossip, small sabotage, alliances below
  stairs. Agency under constraint is more interesting than either freedom or helplessness.
- **Let social position shape testimony.** Whose account is trusted, ignored or punished is itself a story engine,
  especially in mysteries (mystery-and-clues.md §4).

## 4. Barriers and the channel audit
*Examples:* novel-studies.md §9 (how the studied novels justify their barriers).
When a story depends on characters *not* being able to do something (talk, meet, leave, learn the truth, get help),
the reader will test the barrier. "Why didn't she just…?" is the question that breaks belief.

**Channel audit (procedure):**
1. State what the barrier prevents (e.g., "Nadia and Elias cannot communicate for 31 years").
2. List every channel a resourceful person would try:
   - **direct:** a visit, a call, a letter;
   - **indirect:** a relative abroad, a third country, an institution (the Red Cross, a church, a union), a lawyer, a
     merchant, a smuggler, a released prisoner, a radio broadcast, a newspaper notice;
   - **informal:** rumour, an intermediary, a bribe;
   - **later-era options:** amnesties, opened archives, reunification programmes.
3. Close each channel with a concrete, story-specific reason the reader can see or infer:
   - it was tried and failed (on the page, or in a remembered instance);
   - it is forbidden *and enforced* (show an enforcement);
   - it is too costly or dangerous (show the cost on someone);
   - it is psychologically closed (pride, shame or fear stops the character, and the story shows it);
   - it was blocked by someone with a motive (a parent, a rival, a censor, a secret-keeper).
4. Leave open no channel that would end the story at once. If one must stay open, make *not using it* a choice the story
   examines.

**Rules of thumb:**
- **Enforcement must be visible at least once.** A rule that is never enforced reads as a convenience. Show the arrest,
  the confiscation or the punishment, or its trace on a person.
- **Bigger barriers need more texture.** A border that lasts decades needs the life around it:
  - workarounds;
  - black markets;
  - informers;
  - the jokes people tell;
  - the officials' routines;
  - what happens to people who tried.
- **Barriers should cut both ways.** What stops one side also stops the other, unless the asymmetry is the point and is
  explained.
- **The best barriers are partly internal.** External prohibition plus a character's own reason for not pushing (fear,
  pride, a vow, loyalty, guilt) is more believable and more dramatic than prohibition alone.

## 5. History and politics in fiction
- **The public enters through the private.** Wars, regimes and depressions matter in fiction through what they do to
  particular households, jobs, bodies and loyalties. Choose the few historical facts that press on your characters'
  choices, and make those facts concrete.
- **Power is a person.** Political menace is most convincing through someone who wields it:
  - an inspector;
  - a commissar;
  - a landlord;
  - a magistrate.
  Give them appetites and a personal stake.
- **Research for events, not for information.** Use research to find what *happens* in this world (raids, rationing,
  queues, censorship, show trials, amnesties) and turn those into scenes, complications and twists (McKee: research
  should generate events, not dumps).
- **Avoid anachronism in attitude as well as objects.** Characters can't know what the era didn't know, and they
  shouldn't think in the reader's moral vocabulary. Give them their period's assumptions, and let the reader feel the
  distance.
- **Dates and duration** need to agree with history and with each other (revision.md §12).
- **Real atrocities** demand care. Specificity, restraint and attention to victims as people, not props.

## 6. Fable or specific? Choosing the level of abstraction
| Mode | What it gives | What it costs | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specific** (named places, dates, institutions) | belief; texture; the reader can test plausibility and finds it holds | research burden; risk of error; less allegorical reach | most realist, historical, crime and literary fiction |
| **Invented but concrete** (an invented country or town with full institutions, money, language and rules) | freedom plus belief | you must build what history would have given you | when real history would distort the story or be appropriative |
| **Fable** (unnamed "the war", "the capital", "a committee") | universality; a legend-like tone | plausibility gaps feel arbitrary; the prose tends to sound translated or allegorical; readers question workarounds the story never addresses | short parables and legends where plausibility isn't the reader's question |

If you choose fable, keep the abstraction consistent. Make up for it with physical and sensory specificity, and don't
pair it with a plot that depends on the logistics of a real-world barrier, because readers will audit it.

## 7. Places and institutions as story engines
- **Houses and estates** hold the past in rooms, objects and servants. A house can embody an absent person, a family's
  power or a secret, but give it routines, staff with their own loyalties, and rooms that are used, not just symbolic.
- **Closed communities** (schools, islands, ships, villages, religious orders) concentrate pressure: no exit,
  surveillance, shared rules, hierarchies. Decide how people get in and out, who watches, and what happens to those who
  break the rules.
- **Institutions with hidden purposes** (a school that is more than a school, a society behind a society) produce
  mystery through euphemism, ritual and the gap between what members are told and what they sense.
- **Cities under pressure** (war, occupation, dictatorship) produce encounters across class, informers, black markets,
  disappearances and characters who know which streets to avoid.
- Whatever the place, give it **its own time**: daily rhythm, seasons, change across the story. A place that never
  changes is a stage set.

## 8. Research without dumps
- **Use only what the scene needs** and what the viewpoint character would notice. A local doesn't explain the tram
  system to herself.
- **Put the expertise in a character's hands.** Someone who knows the procedure performs it, argues about it or exploits
  it. Expertise shown in action convinces; expertise lectured from one character to another stalls the scene.
- **Watch the explainer-character pattern.** When characters exist mainly to deliver research, give them stakes in
  being believed and let them be wrong or self-serving.

## 9. Rules, probability and the world's laws
- **Every world sets its own causal laws, and once set they can't be broken** (McKee). In fantasy, allow one leap of
  premise, then strict consistency.
- **Establish exceptions early.** If the climax needs an exception to a rule (a secret door, a gap in the patrols, a
  forged pass), plant it before it's needed and give it a cost.
- **Probability over possibility.** Readers accept improbable premises and resist improbable conveniences. Spend
  improbability at the start, never at the resolution.

## 10. World checklist
- [ ] Do I know period, duration, place and the level of conflict, plus a few exact details of daily life?
- [ ] Are the social rules that press on choices shown acting on a person?
- [ ] Have all contact, escape and help channels been audited and closed with visible reasons?
- [ ] Is each enforced rule shown being enforced at least once?
- [ ] Is power embodied in people with their own stakes?
- [ ] Is the level of abstraction (specific, invented, fable) chosen deliberately and kept consistent?
- [ ] Do places have routines, staff, seasons and change?
- [ ] Is research turned into events and character expertise, not lectures?
