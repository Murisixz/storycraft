# Structure and scenes: cause and effect at every scale

Structure is the selection and ordering of events to produce a specific emotional and intellectual experience. It is
not a template. Every story is its own prototype (Howard & Mabley); the principles below explain why shapes recur and
how to build one that fits.

## 1. Units and the engine of change
- **Story event:** a meaningful change in a character's situation, expressed as a value turning (life/death,
  love/hate, truth/lie, freedom/slavery, hope/despair), achieved through conflict (McKee). No value change = no event.
  Change by chance is not an event: rain that happens to end a drought means nothing; rain brought by someone's
  struggle does (McKee's *The Rainmaker* example).
- **Beat:** an exchange of action and reaction; a new beat begins only when behavior changes. Each beat should top the
  previous one.
- **Scene:** action through conflict in continuous time and place that turns at least one value with perceptible
  significance. **Sequence:** a series of scenes (often 2–5) that culminates in a bigger turn. **Act:** a series of
  sequences that peaks in a major reversal. **Story:** a series of acts that builds to a climax of absolute,
  irreversible change. Lower-level changes can be undone; the final one cannot (McKee).
- **The gap** (McKee): a character takes the minimal, conservative action they expect will move them toward their goal;
  the world (their own self, intimates, or society/nature) reacts differently or more powerfully than expected; the
  gap forces a harder, riskier action. A beat in which expectation equals result is dead ("knock, door opens").
- **Causality test:** between beats write "therefore" or "but," never "and then" (Parker & Stone). Plot is causality;
  story is mere sequence (Forster).

## 2. The macro-design
Five parts (McKee): **inciting incident** (the primary cause of everything that follows) → **progressive
complications** → **crisis** (the decisive dilemma) → **climax** (the decisive action and irreversible change) →
**resolution**. Howard & Mabley's lens on the same thing: **main tension** (the middle's governing question) →
**culmination** (answers it and creates a final tension) → **resolution** (the will to struggle disappears).
- **Unity test:** "Because of [inciting incident], [climax] had to happen" must be logical (McKee).
- **One spine:** one main objective per story; a protagonist with two unrelated aims breaks the story in half
  (Howard & Mabley's suspension bridge). Combine aims by making one the means of the other (Truby, via Fraim's outline:
  "through the love of a good woman, a man defeats his brother").
- **Design backward:** settle the ending (see endings.md), then build causes toward it.

## 3. The inciting incident
- It must **radically upset the balance** of the protagonist's life, positively or negatively, felt at once (McKee). A
  mood, a move or a vague decision is not an inciting incident.
- **Its seven jobs** (McKee): upset the balance; arouse a desire to restore it; give a conscious object of desire;
  (for complex protagonists) stir a contradictory unconscious desire; launch the quest; raise the **major dramatic
  question** ("Will X or Y?"); and project the **obligatory scene** (the eventual face-to-face with the opposition at
  full force). If it does these, even a tiny event works (French toast scraped into the disposal in *Kramer vs.
  Kramer*).
- **Timing:** "as soon as possible ... but not until the moment is ripe," i.e., once the audience knows enough to react
  fully; usually within the first quarter (McKee). If it comes late, hold attention with a setup subplot or
  dramatized exposition.
- **Running start, not slow start** (Truby, via outline): open with a wound or ghost from the past, a world that
  imprisons the protagonist, weaknesses, and an immediate problem. Strong inciting events often look like a solution
  but drop the protagonist into worse trouble ("from the frying pan into the fire").
- **Generate options** by reversing charge: worst→best and best→worst versions of the inciting event, and a further
  twist (McKee).

## 4. Progressive complications and escalation
- **Points of no return:** each action must exceed the previous ones in magnitude or quality of will and risk; never
  retreat to a smaller action (McKee).
- **Each solution creates the next problem** (Howard & Mabley on *Witness*), each misstep follows logically from the
  last (*The 400 Blows*), and in a trap story every escape attempt tightens the trap (*North by Northwest*).
- **Easiest solutions first** (Howard & Mabley): deny the problem → ask an authority → reason or bargain → confront →
  only the dreaded option remains (either/or). This generates a natural middle.
- **The first plan fails** (Truby, via outline): the opposition is too strong at first; the protagonist must dig deeper.
  Give the opponent a hidden plan and counterattacks; each counterattack is a reveal.
- **Complexity, not mere complication** (McKee): a relatively small cast and few locations, with conflict on the inner,
  personal and extra-personal levels at once. Key scenes can turn several values at the same time.
- **Move conflict between levels** when one goes quiet (inner → personal → society) rather than letting it stall.
- **Under pressure, characters reveal and sometimes worsen:** a protagonist losing the battle may cross moral lines,
  an ally may confront them (Truby's "attack by ally"), and that second front deepens the story.
- **One devastating apparent defeat** around two-thirds to three-quarters through (Truby, via outline): the
  protagonist believes all is lost; a new revelation then makes victory conceivable and turns desire into obsession.

## 5. Reveals and turns across the whole story
- Plan a **revelation sequence** separately and check it as a unit: logical (in the order the protagonist would most
  likely learn things), building in intensity, and arriving at an increasing pace (Truby, via outline, suggests a
  feature-length story carries something like 7–10 significant reveals).
- Each strong reveal forces a **decision** that bends the protagonist's desire or motive in a new direction.
- **Audience revelation** (Truby, via outline): at least once, let the audience learn something important before the
  protagonist (often that an apparent friend is an opponent). It lifts a slow middle and creates dramatic irony.
- **Backstory as ammunition for turns:** seed the past with significant events and spend them as turning points,
  putting the biggest revelations at act climaxes (McKee).
- **The most powerful reveal is a reversal of understanding**: the audience re-sees everything in a new light. Plant it
  so the rereading test passes (see information-design.md, mystery-and-clues.md).

## 6. Act design, subplots and the middle
- **Number of acts scales with length** (McKee): short fiction and short film usually one major reversal; sitcom and
  novella two; full-length film and novels at least three. More acts aren't better if they become repetitive.
- **Proportions are approximations:** first act about a quarter; last act shortest and fastest.
- **The last two act climaxes should have opposite charges** (if the ending is positive, the penultimate act climax
  is negative, and vice versa). For an ironic ending, lean it one way and set the penultimate against the lean (McKee).
- **The penultimate climax should intensify the dramatic question** ("Now what?").
- **Sagging middle** = Act One-sized actions recycled. Fix: invent far more scene options, add subplot reversals,
  insert a mid-act climax, deepen (personal progression) or widen (social progression) (McKee).
- **Subplots must earn their place** (McKee): contradict the controlling idea, resonate with it as a variation (Howard &
  Mabley: subplots as variations on the theme), set up a later central-plot element, or (best) complicate the
  protagonist's life. If you cross-cut two lines, pay off their implied convergence.
- **Dinner-party test** (McKee): if two characters would react identically to the same event, merge or cut one.

## 7. Scene design
- **Every scene turns a value.** Name the value, its charge at the start and at the end; if they match, the scene is a
  nonevent: cut it or redesign it (McKee).
- **Whose scene is it?** Ask whose want makes the scene happen; it isn't always the protagonist's (Howard & Mabley,
  after Frank Daniel). Give that character a scene objective, an obstacle, and tactics that change as they fail.
- **Turning points** come by **action** or by **revelation**. A good turn works in four steps (McKee): **surprise →
  curiosity ("Why?") → insight** (the audience rushes back through what it saw) **→ new direction**. Plant what the
  audience will rush back to.
- **Setups with two meanings:** an innocent first reading and a truer second one ("my daughter"). Calibrate firmness:
  memorable on recall, not telegraphed; don't overprepare the obvious and underprepare the unusual. **Each payoff can
  become a new setup.** Logic is retroactive: you may invent the wild turn first and plant its logic afterward (McKee).
- **A scene shouldn't resolve the larger conflict** (Howard & Mabley); it leaves the protagonist closer to or farther
  from the goal and propels forward.
- **Enter late, leave early;** open by framing what the scene is about and funnel to a single point, with the most
  important line or image last (Truby, via outline).
- **Plans:** a character can pursue a scene goal directly (say what they want: conflict rises) or indirectly (pretend to
  want something else: conflict hides, then erupts when the deception surfaces) (Truby, via outline).
- **Preparation and aftermath** scenes don't turn plot but steer feeling; McKee still wants even quiet scenes to turn
  some value, so give "breathing" scenes a small decision.
- **Draft actions before dialogue:** if the scene works silently, dialogue will enrich it; if it only works through
  talk, redesign it (Howard & Mabley; Truby; Mamet's memo).
- **Five-step scene analysis** for diagnosis (McKee): (1) define the conflict: what each character wants (as opposed
  infinitives, "to get"/"to stop"); (2) note the opening value charge; (3) break into beats, naming each action and
  reaction; (4) note the closing value; (5) survey the beats to find the turning point. Fix dead scenes by changing
  behavior or desire, not by rewording.

## 8. Choice and dilemma
- A choice between good and evil is no choice; the audience can predict it. Real choice is a **dilemma**: between
  irreconcilable goods, or the lesser of two evils, or both, roughly equal in weight from the character's point of view
  (McKee; Truby makes the climactic moral choice "between two positives, as close to equal as possible").
- Structure dilemmas as a **triangle** (the character torn between two things, which may be people, ideals or selves),
  not as back-and-forth vacillation, and end them with an **irreversible sacrifice**.
- Suffering is not drama; give victims decisions.

## 9. Subtext
- **Never write a scene "about what it's about"** (McKee). Put the real action under a surface task: a love scene played
  as changing a tire, a confession played as an argument about a bill.
- Let characters answer the subtext rather than the text (replies that seem illogical but make perfect sense).
- Even confessions, diaries and narrators conceal; no one fully knows or tells the truth.
- Externalize inner life through action, objects and the juxtaposition of word and deed (Howard & Mabley: the knife
  behind the back during a declaration of love).
- Mood specifies an emotion and can foreshadow; it never replaces a turn (McKee).

## 10. Exposition and backstory
- Exposition is ammunition: characters use facts as weapons or tools to get what they want in the scene; conflict
  first, information second; cut "as you know" lines (McKee).
- **Withhold by default:** cut what the audience can infer; give only what prevents confusion; plant the question
  "Why?" before supplying the answer.
- **Order from least to most important,** secrets last, forced out under pressure.
- **Rules of thumb** (Howard & Mabley): eliminate the non-essential; deliver necessary exposition in conflict (or humor);
  postpone it to its moment of maximum impact; use an eyedropper, not a ladle; make the audience work for it.
- **Flashbacks** only once the audience hungers for them, and each must be a mini-drama with its own turn (McKee).
- **Narration test:** remove it; keep it only if it adds counterpoint rather than explanation (McKee).
- **Open on a consequence and withhold its cause** to pull curiosity backward while the present pulls forward.

## 11. Dialogue essentials
- Dialogue is not real talk; it's compressed, directed and more articulate than life, while sounding natural (Howard
  & Mabley; Truby).
- Each line should do several jobs: characterize, reveal mood or motive (or hide it), reflect relationship, connect to
  what came before, advance the action, carry exposition, foreshadow (Howard & Mabley).
- Put the strongest word or image last; prefer concrete images to abstractions; keep memorable lines short.
- Cut lines that repeat what the action shows. Give each character a distinct voice. Keep theme and moral argument
  mostly in structure; when values must be argued aloud, let an ally criticize the hero, let hero and opponent clash,
  and give the opponent a strong but wrong justification (Truby, via outline).

## 12. Composition and pacing
- **Unity and variety:** within one causal spine, vary tone and texture: the tragic in the comic, the political in the
  personal (McKee).
- **Tension and release:** relentless tension exhausts. Build to each act climax, lower the pressure as the next act
  opens, then build higher than before (McKee).
- **Alternate emotional charges:** avoid three consecutive scenes turning the same way (three sad scenes in a row play
  as numbness or even comedy) unless the third contrasts enough to recolor the others (McKee).
- **Rhythm and tempo:** rhythm = scene length; tempo = activity within the scene. Vary lengths; shorten scenes and raise
  tempo approaching an act climax, then slow down for the climactic scene ("earn the pause") (McKee).
- **French scenes:** within one location, each major entrance or exit creates a new scene and dynamic (McKee).
- **Expressing progression:** widen (consequences spread into society), deepen (into intimate secrets), symbolic
  ascension (images grow from particular to archetypal, unannounced), ironic ascension (life reverses a confident plan)
  (McKee).
- **Transitions:** link the tail of one scene to the head of the next through a shared or opposed trait, action, object,
  word, light, sound or idea (McKee); bridge time jumps with verbal, visual or prop transitions (Howard & Mabley).
- **Time:** cut dull real time (ellipsis); stretch tense time (elaboration); use a time frame or clock to store tension
  (Howard & Mabley).

## 13. Shapes beyond the classic arc
The classic design (McKee's **archplot**: an active protagonist pursuing a goal against mostly external forces, through
causal, linear time, in a consistent reality, to a closed, irreversible ending) is the default for suspense and
mystery, but not the only good shape:
- **Miniplot** (McKee): restraint and minimalism — more inner conflict, open endings, multiple protagonists, quieter or
  passive-seeming leads. The emotional question still gets answered.
- **Antiplot** (McKee): deliberate breaks with causality, linear time or consistent reality, producing meanings like
  "life is absurd." Use only with intent.
- McKee's **story triangle** places every story among these three along seven contrasts: closed vs open ending;
  external vs internal conflict; single vs multiple protagonists; active vs passive protagonist; linear vs nonlinear
  time; causality vs coincidence; consistent vs inconsistent reality. Below them lies **nonplot** (portraits with no
  real change), which he calls narrative rather than story. Each form implies a worldview; choose the one you believe,
  knowing the audience narrows as you move away from archplot.
- **Multiprotagonist / ensemble:** several characters with separate desires make a portrait of a group or place; unity
  comes from place, time, situation or theme (Howard & Mabley's analysis of *Diner*). A **plural protagonist** shares
  one desire and one fate (McKee).
- **Two protagonists, two complete stories:** in *The Godfather*, Vito's and Michael's stories are each complete,
  each protagonist important in the other's, linked by a transfer of power, unified by a group whose goals never
  change, and bookended by parallel events (wedding and baptism) (Howard & Mabley).
- **One event, many perspectives:** unity of a single event retold by conflicting witnesses (Howard & Mabley's
  *Rashomon* analysis); ideal for mysteries about truth and self-deception.
- **Reverse or fractured chronology:** turns the story into dramatic irony (Pinter's *Betrayal*); curiosity becomes
  "how and why".
- **Low-conflict and contrast structures:** story is change, and change can come from relating, finding, losing,
  bearing, discovering or parting, not only fighting (Le Guin, via an excerpt). Four-part *kishōtenketsu*
  (introduction, development, a twist that juxtaposes something unexpected, reconciliation) builds meaning through
  contrast rather than opposition (via secondary sources). Keep a want, a change and a turn; lower the temperature.
- **Frame and narrator stories:** make the act of telling dramatic. Give the teller a reason to tell, let the telling
  change them, and consider closing the frame before the end (Truby, via outline).
Whatever the shape, keep these: a reason to keep watching (a question or a fear), meaningful change, and an ending
that answers the emotional question.

## 14. Scaling to length
| Length | Major reversals | Beats/scenes | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 s–3 min video | 1 | 3–7 beats | one question, one reveal; open in motion |
| Flash fiction (<1.5k words) | 1 | 1–2 scenes | single effect; image ending |
| Short story (3–7.5k words) | 1–2 | 3–8 scenes | one dominant line of action; a narrow time window |
| Novelette (7.5–17.5k) | 1–2 | 6–15 scenes | one secondary life can be developed |
| Novella (17.5–40k) | 2 | 15–40 scenes | small cast; one subplot at most; long spans only as staged stations |
| Short film (5–15 min) | 1–2 | 6–15 scenes | few locations; one recognition |
| Feature film | 3+ | 40–60 scenes | sequences with their own questions |
| Novel | 3+ | 60–120+ scenes | subplots as theme variations |

Before choosing a length, check that the story's material fits it: time span, arcs, reveals and world
(premise.md §7; `assets/templates/scope-budget.md`). Material too large for its length produces summary where scenes
should be.
