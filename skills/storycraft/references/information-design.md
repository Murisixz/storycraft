# Information design: suspense, curiosity, dramatic irony, surprise, reveals

The audience's experience is built from what it knows, when it learns it, and how that compares with what the
characters know. Managing this is the storyteller's main lever on attention and emotion. Without it, a story is "just
so many events in a sequence" (Howard & Mabley).

## 1. The three audience positions
For every sequence, choose deliberately where the audience stands relative to the characters (McKee):

| Position | Audience knows… | Main pull | Feeling produced | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mystery** | less than (some) characters | curiosity about a hidden fact | intrigue, puzzle-solving, "what really happened?" | whodunits, secrets in the backstory, an opening hook |
| **Suspense** | the same as the characters | curiosity about the *outcome* + concern | anxiety, identification, "how will this turn out?" | most stories; most scenes |
| **Dramatic irony** | more than (some) characters | concern | dread, compassion, tenderness; "how and why?" | tragedy, sad stories, betrayals, confrontations |

- **A note on terms.** Writers use "suspense" in two senses. McKee's positional sense (audience knows the same as the
  character) is used in the table above. Hitchcock's famous "suspense" (the bomb under the table that the audience knows
  about and the characters don't) is, positionally, dramatic irony. In this skill, positions are always defined by
  **who knows what**; "suspense" as a feeling (anxious anticipation of an outcome) can come from either shared knowledge
  or irony, and the strongest dread usually comes from irony.
- Most strong stories run in **suspense** overall and switch locally: a **mystery** sequence to sharpen curiosity
  about a fact, an **irony** sequence to touch the heart (Casablanca's Paris flashback: we already know the love ends,
  so we watch with tenderness; its last act hides Rick's decision from us, turning to mystery so the answer lands as a
  jolt).
- **Mystery hooks; suspense moves.** Mystery works in the past and present tenses (what is or was happening?) and is
  excellent for engagement; suspense is "pure future" (what will happen?), and only the future can hold hope against
  fear. Weave the two, alternating for much of the story, then solve the major factual mystery by the end of the second
  act and run the last act on nearly pure suspense. Unduly sustained mystery "can pique our curiosity, but it can't
  touch our feelings" (Howard & Mabley's analysis of *North by Northwest*). Exception: a **mystery of meaning** can
  last to the final image if its answer pays off as an emotional metaphor (Rosebud in *Citizen Kane*).
- Pure mystery mostly satisfies the murder-mystery audience; the detective is admired, not identified with, because
  they're rarely in real jeopardy. If you want heartbreak, don't rely on mystery alone: give a character the audience
  fears for, and use irony.
- Same climax, three ways (McKee's corridor): **mystery** = every door closed, the threat strikes from where no one
  looked; **suspense** = both sides know the other is there, no one knows who wins; **irony** = we see the killer
  behind the door she's approaching. Pick the one that serves the emotion you want.

## 2. Hope vs fear: how anticipation works
- Participation comes from **uncertainty between a hoped-for and a feared outcome** (Howard & Mabley). It needs:
  (1) at least some empathy for a pivotal character; (2) knowledge of what *might* happen; (3) belief that either
  outcome is genuinely possible.
- **Anticipation is informed, not ignorant.** Hiding the danger kills tension; showing it (the hole in the floor
  beside Chaplin's blindfolded skating) creates it. Beginners withhold information to avoid predictability; experts
  share the stakes and keep **two equally plausible outcomes** alive.
- **Inevitable ≠ predictable.** Predictable = the audience can guess what happens next. Inevitable = looking back,
  it could not have happened otherwise. Aim for both unpredictability (two plausible outcomes at each step) and
  inevitability (each step probable, no visible hand of the author).
- Curiosity and concern are the two engines of interest (McKee): questions the mind wants closed, and positive values
  (love, justice, survival, truth) the heart wants protected. Use both.

## 3. Building suspense
1. **Make us care first** (see emotion.md). Suspense is apprehension about something bad threatening someone we're
   attached to, which seems likely but not certain (Zillmann's disposition account, as summarized in later research).
2. **Show the bomb.** Tell the audience about the threat before the characters face it: fifteen seconds of surprise vs
   fifteen minutes of suspense (Hitchcock). This is dramatic irony used for suspense: the audience knows more, so
   anxiety becomes dread. Exception: a surprise that is the highlight of the story.
3. **Ask, then delay** (Lee Child): pose or imply a clear question early ("will she get out before he wakes?") and
   withhold the answer, while delivering smaller answers so progress is felt.
4. **Give it a clock.** A time frame stores emotional energy: deadlines, appointments, a fuse, a departing train, a
   rising tide (Howard & Mabley).
5. **Advertise the future.** *Advertising* = a character's stated plan or scheduled event (wedding, meeting,
   operation, test). *Elements of the future* = hopes, fears, warnings, predictions, promises, omens. Both make the
   audience lean forward and imagine outcomes (Howard & Mabley).
6. **Stretch the moment of danger** (elaboration): slow down when the outcome is about to be decided; cut away from
   the boring (ellipsis).
7. **Escalate.** Each attempt costs more and risks more; each gap between expectation and result raises the stakes
   (McKee).
8. **Use the obligatory scene.** Once you promise a confrontation the audience foresees and desires, deliver it on
   screen (Archer; McKee calls the crisis the story's obligatory scene).

## 4. Building curiosity
- Curiosity is a felt **gap in knowledge** that the person believes can be closed (Loewenstein's information-gap
  account). So: show enough to make the shape of the missing piece visible. Total ignorance produces no curiosity;
  confusion produces irritation.
- **Suspend the time-sequence:** "The queen died, no one knew why, until it was discovered that it was through grief
  at the death of the king" — a plot with a mystery in it (Forster). Show the effect, withhold the cause.
- **Open questions faster than you close them**, but close some regularly. Every turning point should answer one
  question and raise a sharper one.
- **Curiosity is weaker than interest.** Archer distinguished mere curiosity ("the interest of ignorance") from
  dramatic interest, which survives when curiosity is dead (we rewatch tragedies whose endings we know). Build stories
  that still work once the secret is known: the emotion must come from character and stakes, not only from the
  withheld fact. Studies of spoilers are mixed (some found spoiled stories enjoyed as much or more; replications found
  losses in suspense and "movingness"), so design for both first and second viewing.

## 5. Dramatic irony and the recognition you owe
- Irony = the audience knows something a character doesn't: the deaf man on the tracks with a train behind him; Juliet
  only seems dead; Salieri "helps" the dying Mozart in order to steal his Requiem (Howard & Mabley).
- **Revelation → recognition (Frank Daniel):** when you reveal something to the audience that a character doesn't
  know, you create an obligation to stage the moment the character finds out. Withholding that recognition feels like
  betrayal; delivering it is often the emotional peak.
- Irony converts anxiety into **dread** (of the moment of discovery) and **compassion** (for someone walking toward
  disaster). It is the native mode of tragedy and of heartbreaking stories.
- Opening with the ending (a corpse floating in a pool; a death notice; a chat log already saying who died) turns the
  whole story into irony and refocuses curiosity on **how and why** (McKee). Reverse-chronology tellings push this
  further (Pinter's *Betrayal*: we end on the hopeful first meeting already knowing the pain to come).
- Irony enables subtext: when we know a character is lying, every line plays double (Howard & Mabley).

## 6. Surprise and twists
- **True surprise** = the gap between expectation and result springs open *and* releases a rush of insight into a truth
  hidden under the surface (McKee). It reinterprets what we have seen.
- **Cheap surprise** = jolting the audience by breaking the flow (a fake-out dream, a hand on the shoulder that turns
  out to be a friend). A convention in horror, fantasy and thrillers; a cheat elsewhere.
- **False mystery** = withholding exposition that should be given, just to create suspense over dull material (a crash
  in the opening, then "six months earlier..."). Avoid it (McKee).
- A good twist passes three checks:
  1. **Planted:** the evidence for the true reading was on screen or page, fairly (rereading test).
  2. **Motivated:** it follows from what characters wanted and did, not from author convenience.
  3. **Meaningful:** it changes what the story means or what a character must decide, not just what we know.
- Prefer suspense over surprise for sustained effect; reserve surprise for one or two turns that reframe the story,
  ideally a **turning point inside the climax** (McKee) or the final recognition.

## 7. Designing a reveal
*Examples:* novel-studies.md §7 (explanation versus consequence in the studied novels).
- **Recognition + reversal together** is the strongest form: the change from ignorance to knowledge flips the
  situation at the same moment (Aristotle's Oedipus).
- **Stage it through action and image**, not explanation. The best reveals are implied or shown (Chinatown replaced a
  long expository speech with one question and a silent denial; McKee).
- **Prepare it.** Plant clues and objects that will change meaning (Howard & Mabley's planting and payoff); separate
  plant and payoff in time; distract when they must be close.
- **Choose its position.** Where should the audience learn it relative to the character? Before (irony and dread), with
  them (shared shock), after (mystery, and the character's behavior is reinterpreted in hindsight)?
- **Give it aftermath and consequence.** Let characters and audience absorb the blow, then show what someone does
  because of it (endings.md §9–10).
- **Keep the explanation load light.** The revelation scene should carry the one fact that turns it, not the whole
  backstory. Plan the explanation across the story:
  - **Before:** spread the backstory as unexplained effects and partial accounts (an odd habit, a gap in a record, a
    reaction that doesn't fit), so the reveal *connects* rather than *informs*.
  - **During:** give the explainer a goal in the telling (to be forgiven, to wound, to keep one last thing back) and
    let the listener resist, interrupt or refuse. A confession is a scene, not a lecture.
  - **After:** leave room for questions the characters ask later and for consequences.
  - **Long embedded accounts** (a manuscript, a letter, a confession) stop the present story. Budget their length and
    give them their own voice and gaps.
- **A large reveal can widen step by step and end in a human witness:** one cell, then the count, then the clean rooms,
  then a cart of bodies, then a captive who explains in short, flat answers (*Amarita*). Each step raises the scale;
  the witness supplies the meaning.
- **For endings that leave a question:** answer the emotional question fully; leave open only a question the story's
  clues make answerable (a real answer exists, and attentive viewers can argue for it).

## 8. Exposition is information design
- Exposition serves the audience, not the characters. Use it like a spice (Howard & Mabley): cut what will become clear
  anyway; deliver the rest in scenes of conflict (or with humor); postpone it to its moment of greatest impact; dose it
  with an eyedropper, not a ladle.
- Make the audience work only for what the information plan withholds, through hints, partial revelations,
  contradictions and small mysteries. Effort on the designed questions creates participation (and closure; see Smith
  & Worch's environmental storytelling). Effort on basic facts creates confusion: who someone is to the protagonist,
  what a place is and when we are come in a clause at first need (clarity-and-orientation.md §9).
- McKee's principle (detailed in structure-and-scenes.md): show, don't tell, and convert exposition into ammunition that a
  character uses to get what they want in the scene.
- **Techniques that keep lore inside scenes:**
  - **Place lore between two lines of dialogue,** and let the next line use it. Or let action cut the answer off
    ("What's the difference?", and the light changes) (*Khawf* 2).
  - **Explain lore after the reader has seen its effect,** not in advance. But establish any rule the climax depends on
    before it is needed (clarity-and-orientation.md §8).
  - **Make every prop a contract:** explain it, then use it (salt, rice, scripture, a rooster, each used in turn).
  - **Introduce a device by its parts** in short parallel clauses, then show each part working, using the same nouns.
  - **Teach a strange world through the newcomer's mistakes** (world-and-society.md §9).
  - **A reader-proxy** (a sceptical brother, a pupil) asks the reader's questions. A proxy does not make a long lecture
    short.
  - **A reluctant functionary answering short questions** explains a hidden institution better than a speech: each
    flat answer adds one fact (*Amarita*).
  - **Avoid invented statistics and taxonomies** delivered as speech. They give lore false authority and stall the
    scene.

## 9. The information ledger (procedure)
Use `assets/templates/information-plan.md`. For each major secret or question:
1. **Truth:** state the fact (from the hidden story).
2. **Holders:** who knows it at the start, and who learns it when.
3. **Audience entry point:** when and how the audience learns it (before/with/after the affected character).
4. **Mode it creates:** mystery, suspense or irony in each stretch.
5. **Clues and plants:** where the truth leaks before the reveal (see mystery-and-clues.md).
6. **Recognition owed:** if the audience learns first, where is the character's recognition scene?
7. **Payoff:** what the reveal changes (decision, relationship, meaning).
Then run down the beat list and confirm that the audience always has at least one live question, and that one is
answered roughly as another opens.

## 10. Inference control: leaks, rival explanations and the reader's speed
*Examples:* novel-studies.md §4 (framing, conceding, sealing, rotating).
Fairness guards against under-planting. The opposite failure is just as common and harder to see from the inside:
the author knows the answer, so every hint feels subtle, while the reader assembles it chapters early and the reveal
lands as confirmation.
- **Track inference, not just disclosure.** For each secret, record when the audience is *told* and also when a sharp
  reader could *infer* it with confidence. The gap between the inference point and the reveal point decides the
  effect:
  - **Inference just before the reveal:** the pleasure of being right.
  - **Inference at the reveal:** shock with insight.
  - **Inference long before:** the reveal is a formality, unless the design has turned the secret into irony.
  Procedure: revision.md §6. Template: `assets/templates/information-plan.md`.
- **Know where leaks come from:**
  - **A second voice that knows:** an interlude, a prologue in the secret-keeper's voice, a document, or the narrator's
    hindsight remarks. Each hint is small; together they give the answer away.
  - **Details that only make sense if the secret is true,** like a stranger who knows the family's pet name, or a
    scar that matches.
  - **Emphasis:** a detail lingered on, repeated or given its own paragraph.
  - **Symmetry:** matched numbers, mirrored events, the same line in two mouths. Patterns point.
  - **Packaging:** chapter titles, epigraphs, jacket copy, a "there was one thing she refused to think about" line.
- **Keep a rival explanation alive.** A mystery holds when the reader can believe more than one answer. Give the false
  answer real support:
  - a motive;
  - a piece of evidence;
  - a character who argues for it;
  - the viewpoint character's own assumption (often the strongest, because the reader shares it).

  Every rival explanation needs its own innocent truth (mystery-and-clues.md §8).
- **Control speed with the narrator.** A narrator who misreads, who is too young or too invested to understand, or who
  uses euphemisms she was taught, lets the reader sense more than she says and so supports irony. A narrator who
  withholds by choice must have a reason the reader accepts, and the withholding must be part of her character
  (viewpoint-and-narration.md §5).
- **One strategy per secret per stretch.** Choose mystery, suspense or irony for each secret in each stretch. A second
  voice that gives the reader the answer converts a mystery into irony, whether intended or not. Then write the
  scenes irony needs: characters acting on the false belief while the reader winces.
- **Decide what the reveal is for:**
  - **Recontextualizing:** earlier scenes change meaning.
  - **Confirming:** a dreaded guess comes true, which works for tragedy if the reader was meant to dread it.
  - **Forcing a decision:** the fact matters for what someone must now do.

  Mystery-led stories need the first or the third. A known fact can still devastate when its *consequences* are the
  reveal.
- **A fair flash-forward can mislead.** End a section on a future moment described so that it fits two people (a
  dawn visitor known only by her dress and dialect), and pay it with a detail only the true one fits (a nickname only
  she uses; *Amarita*). The reader's own inference becomes the trap. Pay it before the setup is forgotten.
- **Plants that don't point.** A plant is fair when it's visible and fairly readable in hindsight, and it hides when
  it is also *doing another job* (characterization, comedy, conflict) at the moment it appears. A detail that has no
  job except being a clue points at itself.

## 11. Failure modes
- **Confusion mistaken for mystery:** the audience doesn't know what's at stake, who wants what, or where/when it is.
  Fix: clarify the situation; hide only the specific fact. Keep an intended-uncertainty list and run a cold read
  (clarity-and-orientation.md §2, §12).
- **Withheld danger:** no suspense because the audience can't foresee what might go wrong. Fix: show the bomb.
- **One plausible outcome:** predictability. Fix: strengthen the opposing possibility.
- **Revelation without recognition:** the audience is told a secret that the affected character never confronts.
- **Twist that cancels the story** ("it was all a dream"; the killer is a stranger introduced at the end). Fix: plant,
  motivate, make it meaningful.
- **Explaining the reveal:** a long speech after the image already landed. Fix: trust implication, cut the speech.
- **Mystery box without contents:** intriguing questions with no designed answer. Fix: write the hidden story before
  plotting the clues.
- **Leaky secret:** the reader infers the answer long before the reveal. Fix: leak audit; cut or soften the pointing
  details; support a rival explanation; or convert to deliberate irony (§10).
- **Mixed strategy:** a secret run as a mystery in the main line while a second voice gives it away. Fix: choose one
  strategy per stretch (§10).
- **Explanation-heavy reveal:** the backstory delivered as a monologue inside the revelation scene. Fix: distribute it
  earlier as effects; keep only the turning fact; give the teller a goal and the listener resistance (§7).

## 12. Quick recipes by form
- **Short video (≤3 min):** open on an effect without its cause (curiosity) or on a visible danger (suspense); keep one
  dominant question; place 2–4 visual clues that are legible on rewatch; end with a recognition that recontextualizes
  the first shot.
- **Short story:** one main question; irony is cheap to set up in prose (the reader is told what the character can't
  see); land a single turn that changes meaning.
- **Novel:** braid questions of different lengths (scene, chapter, act, book). Track promises and payoffs; revisit the
  information ledger at every act break.
- **Film/TV:** use visual irony (the audience sees what's behind the door); sequences can each carry their own
  question; episodes should close one question while widening the main one.
- **Games:** the player's knowledge can lag or lead the character; environmental storytelling lets players pull
  information at their own pace (see forms.md).
