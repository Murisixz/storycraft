# Character: desire, need, contradiction, opposition, cast

Characters are not people; they are designed to be understood, felt for, and revealed under pressure. In story,
character and structure are one thing seen from two sides: events test characters, and characters' choices cause
events.

## 1. Character is revealed by choice under pressure
- **Characterization** is the observable surface: age, job, looks, habits, speech, possessions. **True character** is
  what a person chooses when the stakes are high, and only choice under pressure reveals it (McKee). The greater the
  pressure, the truer the revelation.
- A character's traits matter less than **how they pursue their objective** (Howard & Mabley).
- The most interesting revelations **contrast with the surface**: the timid clerk who refuses the order, the charming
  host who would kill to keep a secret.
- **What characters say about themselves (and what others say) is a clue**, which action will confirm or contradict
  (McKee). A character who misjudges themselves gives the audience dramatic irony.
- Build key scenes as **dilemmas**, not trait displays (see structure-and-scenes.md §8). Give the wrong choice a real,
  tempting rationalization, and make each choice cost more than the last: a ladder of ever-harder decisions (McKee).
- **McKee's five steps of a character arc:** (1) establish the characterization; (2) a choice under pressure reveals the
  true character beneath it; (3) that true character contrasts with or contradicts the surface; (4) escalating
  dilemmas press the deep character harder and harder; (5) by the climax the character's inner nature has changed (or
  been revealed as unable to change). Fit the surface traits to make the final action credible.

## 2. Desire: what drives the story
- "Somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it" (Frank Daniel, via Howard & Mabley).
- **Ask what the character wants now, soon and overall, consciously and unconsciously** (McKee).
- **One main objective.** It must arouse opposition, and its nature sets the audience's attitude to the character
  (Howard & Mabley). The protagonist is the one whose **decisions** create the story, not necessarily the title
  character or the one with the most screen time: John Book, not the boy, in *Witness*; Elliott, not E.T.; Kane, not
  the reporter Thompson (Howard & Mabley).
- **Intensity matters:** the audience's concern rises with how badly the character wants it (Howard & Mabley).
- **Negative wants count:** not wanting, escaping, keeping something hidden, restoring the old order.
- **Conflict vs hassle:** an obstacle matters only if it blocks an established want with something at stake (Howard &
  Mabley).
- **Obstacles should be difficult but possible.** When the odds are truly impossible, the character's belief that they
  can win keeps hope alive (Howard & Mabley).
- **Start the want modest and raise it.** Escalate the intensity and stakes of the same desire instead of replacing it
  with a new one (Truby, via Fraim's outline). A desire that starts at maximum leaves nowhere to build.

## 3. Need, flaw and the inner story
- **Desire vs need** (Truby, via outline): desire is the surface goal the audience tracks; need is the inner change
  required for a better life, usually overcoming a weakness. The character should not know the need at the start.
  Give them a **moral** need (they are hurting someone) as well as a psychological one.
- **Conscious vs unconscious desire** (McKee): a complex protagonist has a conscious object and a contradictory
  unconscious desire. The unconscious desire is the spine, so conscious goals can shift. A simple protagonist's goal
  never changes.
- **Weakness × action = changed person** (Truby, via outline): find the one action that best forces the character to
  face their weakness; the end state is the opposite of the opening weakness (or its tragic deepening).
- **Link inner and outer conflict:** the external antagonist makes the inner struggle visible (Howard & Mabley). The
  ideal opponent attacks exactly the protagonist's great weakness (Truby, via outline).
- **Internal conflict between two goods** (duty vs happiness, loyalty vs truth) is the richest inner conflict (Maass,
  via a summary; consistent with McKee's dilemma).
- **Prove inner change through a choice under pressure, never through a speech.** The self-revelation should be sudden,
  shattering and shown in a decision (Truby, via outline; McKee).

## 4. Dimension is contradiction
- **Dimension means contradiction**, either within deep character (guilt-ridden ambition) or between surface and depth
  (a charming thief) (McKee). Piling up traits and quirks is not dimension: it is "a cluster of traits stuck on a name."
- **The contradiction must be consistent:** a tension that recurs across situations, not a one-off inconsistency.
- **The protagonist should be the most dimensional character.** Otherwise empathy drifts to whoever is more
  contradictory (McKee: in *Blade Runner*, the replicant Roy Batty out-dimensions the hero).
- **Every added dimension is a promise:** a minor character with a striking contradiction implies they'll matter later.
  Pay it off or flatten it (McKee).
- **Walls and leaks** (Beinhart, via a summary): characters guard what they care about behind a façade; pressure cracks
  it, and what leaks out reveals them.

## 5. Empathy and the Center of Good
Covered in emotion.md §1–3. Essentials:
- Empathy ("like me") is required; sympathy ("likable") is optional (McKee; Howard & Mabley). We follow monsters if we
  see "the human heart suffering inside" and they are actively doing something.
- Give the protagonist a **Center of Good**: at least one real positive quality, set against a darker surrounding world
  (McKee).
- Truby (via outline) puts it starkly: the audience should empathize with the hero "but not sympathize". Read it as a
  warning against making protagonists pleasant at the cost of making them true.

## 6. Opposition: the principle of antagonism
- **A protagonist and story are only as compelling as the forces of antagonism make them** (McKee). Sum the inner,
  personal and extra-personal opposition: at the inciting incident the protagonist should be the underdog.
- **Take the core value to its limit.** McKee's value spectrum runs from the positive, through the **contrary**
  (somewhat negative), and the **contradictory** (the direct opposite), to the **negation of the negation** (a
  qualitatively worse, compound negative). Stopping at the contrary or contradictory makes a story competent but
  rarely profound.

| Value | Contrary | Contradictory | Negation of the negation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Love | indifference | hate | self-hate; hatred posing as love |
| Truth | white lies, half-truths | lies | self-deception |
| Justice | unfairness | injustice | tyranny |
| Loyalty | split allegiance | betrayal | self-betrayal |
| Freedom | restraint | slavery | slavery perceived as freedom; self-enslavement |
| Courage | fear | cowardice | cowardice perceived as courage |
| Success | compromise | failure | selling out |
| Communication | alienation | isolation | insanity |
| Consciousness | unconsciousness | death | damnation |
(Rows from McKee's spectra.)

- **How to make "worse" qualitatively worse** (a synthesis of McKee's examples): turn the negative **inward** (self-hate,
  self-deception, self-betrayal); **disguise it as its positive** (hatred masquerading as love, slavery perceived as
  freedom); **legitimize** it (injustice made law: tyranny); **compound** it (a secret inside a secret, as in
  Chinatown).
- **Typical progression:** contrary early, contradictory in the middle, negation of the negation in the last act;
  then a tragic end, or a return to the positive "with a profound difference" (McKee). A story can also open at the
  negation and climb (Casablanca's Rick begins in self-deception).
- **For sad and haunting stories**, the negation of the negation is often where grief becomes unbearable: the mourner who
  blames themselves, the love that became control, the truth a character has hidden even from themselves. Find it.
- **The best opponent wants the same thing** as the protagonist (the same prize, person, territory, or truth), which
  forces repeated direct conflict, and is the one best placed to attack the protagonist's weakness (Truby, via outline).
  Opponents often mirror the protagonist.
- **Write antagonists from the inside.** They believe they are decent and justified, and try to get away with it
  (McKee); give them a strong but wrong argument (Truby, via outline). A humanizing, surprising detail makes a threat
  more frightening, not less.
- For hidden opposition (conspiracies, unknown culprits), see mystery-and-clues.md §2.

## 7. Designing the cast
- **The protagonist creates the cast** (McKee's "solar system"): each supporting character exists to draw out one side
  of the protagonist's contradictions, and supporting characters also reveal each other. Minor characters can be flat
  but must not be dull.
- **Character web** (Truby, via outline): define each character in relation to the protagonist by
  - *function:* protagonist, main opponent, ally, fake-ally opponent (seems a friend, works against), fake-opponent ally
    (seems hostile, helps), subplot character (faces the same problem differently, with a different outcome);
  - *theme:* each major character answers the story's central moral problem differently;
  - *opposition:* place the protagonist, main opponent and two secondary opponents at four "corners," each as different
    from the others as possible (Truby's four-corner opposition).
- **Archetypes are tools, not molds:** a king, mentor, warrior, trickster, lover or rebel brings a strength and a
  shadow (the negative form of the role). Make it specific or it becomes a stereotype (Truby, via outline).
- **The ally** helps and reflects the protagonist's values, confronts them when they go wrong (Truby's "attack by
  ally"), and must never be more interesting than the protagonist.
- **Dinner-party test** (McKee): imagine the cast at one table; if two characters would react identically to the same
  event, merge or cut one.
- **Every character thinks they are the protagonist of their own life** (Howard & Mabley): give each a want, even if
  small.

## 8. Motive, backstory and the ghost
- **The ghost** (Truby, via outline): the past event that still haunts the protagonist, often the source of their
  weakness. It is narrower and more useful than general backstory.
- **Understand motive deeply but leave part of the "why" open.** Never reduce a person to one cause (McKee).
- **Root strong reactions in a formative moment** from the past, and keep reactions in character unless something
  compelling breaks the pattern (Maass, via a summary).
- **Know far more than you show** (Howard & Mabley); backstory is ammunition to spend as turning points, not a
  biography to recite (McKee; see structure-and-scenes.md §10).

## 9. Arcs: change, refusal, tragedy
- **An arc is a change in deep character** over the telling, for better or worse. Change can go downward: Michael
  Corleone ends as a tyrant (Truby, via outline).
- **Common change patterns** (Truby, via outline): child to adult, adult to leader, cynic to participant, leader to
  tyrant, leader to visionary.
- **Under pressure characters often get worse before better:** losing, they cross lines, an ally objects, they justify
  themselves, and the bill comes due at the self-revelation (Truby, via outline).
- **Steadfast protagonists** are valid: the character holds firm while others change around them. Then the arc lives in
  the people they affect and in what holding firm costs them.
- **Tragic arcs:** recognition arrives too late to undo the act, or the long-awaited act misfires (Hamlet stabs Polonius
  through the curtain; Howard & Mabley). The audience grieves the gap between what was understood and what can no
  longer be changed.
- **Passive protagonists can work** only if the passivity is caring paralysis, not indifference, and the world attacks
  it actively; the culmination is the moment they finally act (Howard & Mabley's analyses of *sex, lies, and videotape*
  and *Hamlet*). A fateful decision can be an abdication (Thelma lets Louise think for her), making the arc a takeover
  of agency (*Thelma & Louise*).
- **Whose story is it?** When unsure, ask who makes the pivotal decisions, who changes most, who has most at stake, who
  carries the theme, and what the title points to (Howard & Mabley).

## 10. Writing characters on the page
- **Love every character, villains especially,** without losing judgment (McKee).
- **Source deep character from self-knowledge** ("If I were this person in these circumstances, what would I do?") and
  surface traits from observation, combined rather than copied (McKee).
- **Leave room for the actor or reader:** give want, obstacle and stakes; cut mannerisms and redundant lines (McKee).
- **Prefer details with "push"** (a detail implying pressure or action, such as a packed bag by the door) over static
  trait lists (Beinhart, via a summary).
- **Give each character a distinct voice** and their own way of dodging the truth.
- **Comic characters** are driven by a blind obsession; when they recognize it, the comedy ends and the story can turn
  romantic or dramatic (McKee).

## 11. Character checklist
- [ ] Can you state the protagonist's conscious want, unconscious desire, and need in one line each?
- [ ] Do the key scenes force choices under rising pressure that reveal true character?
- [ ] Is the protagonist the most dimensional character (a consistent contradiction)?
- [ ] Does the audience have empathy (and a Center of Good), even for a dark lead?
- [ ] Is the opposition strong enough for the protagonist to start as the underdog?
- [ ] Does the core value reach its negation of the negation somewhere?
- [ ] Does the main opponent want the same thing, and attack the protagonist's weakness?
- [ ] Does each major character answer the central moral problem differently?
- [ ] Is inner change proven by a choice, never announced?
