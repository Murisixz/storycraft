# Clarity and orientation: a clear scene with a hidden secret

A reader can enjoy not knowing *why someone did it*. No reader enjoys not knowing *who is speaking, where we are, or what just happened*. Mystery, subtext and restraint work only when the reader's picture of the situation stays intact. This file covers how to keep that picture intact, in any language. Arabic-specific mechanics are in `arabic-writing.md`, and the cross-language problems are in `translation.md`.

## 1. The situation model and the two kinds of uncertainty
At every paragraph a reader keeps a running model of six things:
1. **Where and when** are we? Is this the present or a memory?
2. **Who is present,** and through whose eyes are we seeing?
3. **Who is speaking or acting** in this sentence?
4. **What just happened,** physically?
5. **Why does this follow?** That is, the immediate cause, or the answer the line gives to the previous line. A deep motive can be a designed question; the local link cannot.
6. **What is at stake** right now?

**Intended uncertainty** is a question the story chooses to hold open: who did it, what the letter meant, what the creature is, whether she will tell. **Accidental uncertainty** is a gap in one of the six items above that the story does not mean to leave.

The first produces curiosity. The second produces the complaint "I understand the words but I don't know what's happening", and readers usually blame the writing, rightly. Each accidental gap is small, but they add up: after three or four, the reader stops building the model and starts skimming.

Restraint governs how feelings and meanings are stated, not whether the reader can follow events, unless a gap is designed and the reader can tell it is one (§6; SKILL.md, "Calibration").

**Clarity is not simplicity.** Don't simplify vocabulary, explain subtext, gloss what the reader can infer, or vowel everything. Fix orientation, reference and links. A repair adds orientation, never an explanation of meaning.

## 2. The intended-uncertainty list
Before drafting or revising a story that withholds anything, write a short list, kept apart from the text:
- **What the reader must not yet know,** stage by stage (from the information plan, `information-design.md` §9).
- **What the reader may suspect** but must not be sure of.
- **What the reader should infer without being told:** subtext, implication, irony. Test that a cold reader infers it. Never state it just to make sure.
- **What must be clear** at each stage. This is everything else, including the facts the mystery stands on: who the people are, how they are related, where the crime happened, what each document is.

Any reader uncertainty that is on none of these lists is a defect. During revision, test every confusing passage against the lists: if the gap is not there, fill it.

## 3. Orienting scenes and returns
- **At each new scene,** settle the items that changed (place, time, who is present, viewpoint) within the first sentence or two. Do it through action where possible ("By the time the bus reached the prison, the rain had stopped"), not with a label.
- **At a return** from a flashback, a cutaway or another viewpoint, give a re-entry anchor: a time word, a place, or a callback to the last thing the reader saw there.
- **Mark memories** clearly at both ends. In English the past perfect usually carries the entry ("She had written…") and a plain past tense or a time word carries the exit. Other languages need their own markers (Arabic: `arabic-writing.md` §6).
- **Say why the protagonist is here** when a scene opens somewhere new. "She took the early train to the town where her sister was last seen" is one clause, and it is not a spoiler.
- **Use the clocks the characters live by.** A prayer time, a market day, a school bell or a shift change orients and
  can work as a timer ("sunset is near"; the *Khawf* books). Dated or clock headers ("12:01"; "nine years earlier") orient
  instantly and can turn banter into dramatic irony (*Arsis* 1).
- **Re-introduce a long-absent character** with an epithet and a role at the first reappearance ("his old friend, the
  madman of the tunnel"; *Ard Zikola*).
- **Keep deliberate disorientation at one level.** "Which world am I in?" can stay open while the body, the light and
  the ground stay concrete (*Ard Zikola*'s arrival).
- **In darkness,** block positions before the light fails, say what cannot be perceived, and turn action beats into
  sound beats ("I heard him swallow"; *Khawf* 2).
- **Mark every narrator switch the same way,** with a name heading every time. First-person voices look alike on the
  page, and one unlabelled section leaves the reader guessing who "I" is (*Amarita*). *Wadi al-Dhi'ab* keeps six
  narrators clear this way, and gives a continuing narrator only a chapter number.
- **When geography matters at a turning point, let a character orient aloud,** one direction per line (*Amarita*). The
  reader gets the map as action.
- **Do not let layout imply a break** that is not there. A half-blank page or a stray ornament reads as a new section.

## 4. Keeping referents clear
- **The nearest-person rule.** Readers attach a pronoun to the nearest preceding person who fits in gender and number, unless a strong topic chain overrides it. English leans on the topic chain more than many languages. Arabic, with attached pronouns and frequently omitted subjects, leans on the nearest person (`arabic-writing.md` §3). Write for the stricter rule.
- **One reference rule for a whole book** can be taught once. In *Wolf Hall* bare "he" is always the protagonist, other men are named, and an appositive ("he, Cromwell") repairs a sentence where two men meet. Even this stumbles in places (*Bring Up the Bodies*), so check every scene with two men.
- **Two or more people of the same gender in a scene:** re-anchor with a name or a distinctive epithet:
  - at the start of each paragraph;
  - after any sentence that mentions another person of that gender;
  - whenever the subject changes.
- **Don't put a description right after a sentence whose last noun is a different person.** After "The doctor nodded to the porter", a new sentence "He was a small, anxious man…" can attach to the porter, and Arabic readers will attach it to him (`translation.md` §6.2). Fold the description into the subject: "The doctor, a small, anxious man, nodded to the porter."
- **"This", "that" and "it" for actions or ideas:** name the thing when more than one candidate is in view. After "He locked the shed and burned the map", "He regretted it at once" could mean either act; write "He regretted burning the map at once".
- **Objects with two names:** if a character has two keys, or a diary and an address book, say once which is which. Afterwards, use one name per object consistently.
- **Names that are also ordinary words** (in languages without capital letters, or English names such as Hope, Faith, Rose, Will): introduce them with a role noun at first mention, and avoid sentence positions where they parse as the ordinary word. Arabic adds a case trap (`arabic-writing.md` §7).
- **Naming systems for a crowded cast** (examples from the novels studied, usable in any language):
  - **fixed role labels for the unnamed,** never varied ("the sniper", "the official");
  - **labels that change with the viewpoint's knowledge** ("the bearded man" becomes his name once he gives it), with one
    bridging sentence when someone else supplies the name;
  - **epithets built from an earlier act** ("the one who ate the money"), which give instant reference;
  - **for a return after a long gap,** a demonstrative plus an epithet plus a clause recalling the meeting ("that stranger
    I met at the grave");
  - **never one epithet for two people.** If two old men share a scene, use an ordinal or a stable visible attribute
    ("the man sitting" / "the man standing");
  - **anonymous fighters or guards** are tracked by number and position ("one of the three", "the nearer guard"), or
    by "one… another… a third" chains with the leader picked out by his gear (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*), and the actor of
    each sentence stays explicit.
- **Three people of one gender:** name one, give the second only "said" plus a fixed attribute (his beard), and keep the
  first silent while the second speaks (*Khawf* 2).
- **Withholding an identity inside a clear scene:** use a stable kinship or role construct ("Salim's elder brother") and
  let the name land in the last line, with every line clear (*Khawf* 1). Or name a disguised character through
  another character's vocative, so the name lands as an event (*Arsis*).

## 5. Links: making "why this follows" recoverable
- **Local links.** A reply must be recognisable as a response: to the line before it, to an earlier question, or to what lies under it (`voice-and-dialogue.md` §3). A dodge, a refusal or an answer to the subtext is fine when the reader can tell what it answers. A non sequitur the reader cannot connect is the defect. An action must follow from a cause the reader has or can infer, unless its motive is a listed question; then make the action itself concrete.
- **The distance rule.** If following the plot (not feeling its resonance) depends on matching a moment with something 30 or more paragraphs earlier, use an **echo**: the same words or the same object. If a cold read shows readers still miss the match, let a character make it once, briefly. Resonant echoes and social facts left without comment (`mystery-and-clues.md` §17) stay unexplained: the reader's own match is the payoff.
- **The premise behind a comparison or a reason** must be on the page. "Better to lose the farm this year than next" needs the missing premise ("the debt doubles every winter"), or the comparison reads as backwards.
- **One enigmatic signal per beat.** A gesture, an aphorism and a coded instruction in the same paragraph cannot all be decoded. Make one the mystery and keep the other two plain.
- **Make reasoning audible.** Lay an inference out as "if… and if… then…", and stage a decision as parts of the self
  ("part of me wants… another part…"; *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). The reader follows the link instead of having to build it.
- **Connectives carry logic.** Languages differ in how much logic they leave implicit (`translation.md` §6.1; `arabic-writing.md` §5). When in doubt, mark cause, contrast and sequence.

## 6. Turning points: what the reader understands, and when
A **hinge** is a moment where a character learns something, decides, or changes course, or where a relationship turns. A **decisive event** is a hinge that cannot be undone: a death, an arrest, a betrayal discovered, a departure, a choice made. The question at each one is not "did I state it?" but **when should the reader understand what happened, and what will make that happen?**

Restraint and ritual work at these moments when the reader can still follow the situation, or can tell that a doubt is meant. A missed hinge is the costliest accidental gap, because the story moves on without the reader. A reader who meets a death only when a burial is mentioned has lost the scene, and everything said about the death has been lost with it.

### 6.1 Choose when the reader should know
Put the choice on the intended-uncertainty list (§2):
- **At once.** This gives shock, or witnessing together.
- **By the end of the scene.** The reader shares a witness's slow recognition, and the scene closes it (*The Art of Death* on Morrison's Pilate: "it took a while for him to realize that she was dead").
- **Later, by design.** The viewpoint is absent, asleep, deceived or refusing. The reader then learns at a planned point, and meanwhile knows that *something* happened: a gap, a summons, a closed door. Or the reader is misled with fair signals.
- **Known in advance.** A prologue, a flash-forward or the paratext gives the outcome ("The boy, Hamnet, died in 1596, aged eleven."; "which is kill her", *Bring Up the Bodies*). Suspense moves to how and why.
- **Kept uncertain on purpose.** Alive or dead, accident or murder, which version is true: the story wants the doubt. It works when the reader can tell that they do not know (§6.3). The doubt can be short: *Palace of Desire* narrates a father's collapse with the technique its first volume used for a son's death, then holds the outcome for one page across a chapter break. The returning reader's fear is the suspense, and the man lives.

### 6.2 Choose the channel that fits the point of view and the pace
Several channels can make an event unmistakable. Each buys something and costs something. The choice depends on whose eyes we are behind, how busy the scene is, and what the reader should feel there.

| Channel | What it achieves | Works when | Gets in the way when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A plain line** ("had died"; "As she draws her last breath") | Certainty, which lets everything around it stay understated | The scene is busy or the viewpoint is distracted. The death interrupts another scene: *The Moonstone* lands Lady Verinder's death in six steps inside one paragraph, ending on a plain verb | The power of the moment lies in the reader working it out; the line then flattens it |
| **An act that fits only this event** (a tactile check: "the body was cold and silent"; eyes closed; a face covered; hands folded) | Physical certainty with a silent narrator | The act cannot be mistaken (*Men in the Sun*; Danticat's account of her mother) | The act also fits another reading: pressing on a chest reads as rescue, not as death |
| **A ritual or formula** (washing, a shroud, condolences, «إنا لله», a funeral notice) | Feeling and certainty together, true to the culture | It belongs to *after* death. Or the narration frames it: *Bring Up the Bodies* opens Katherine's anointing with "later he will hear", so the rite reads as dying, not as atmosphere. Or one character states the convention for outsiders beforehand. In *Palace of Desire* a friend says a marriage is "a word, then an ululation", so the ululation the excluded hero later hears makes the contract certain | It belongs to *dying* (prompting the dying to say the creed), the reader cannot read the custom, or the book uses the same formulas in jest and courtesy elsewhere. Pin it with a plain noun, a sound or a reaction nearby. *Palace Walk* prepares a condolence formula with the illness before it and a lowered head beside it |
| **A reaction the reader can read** (a cry; a refused sentence; Mercy in *Wolf Hall* who "looks away when she says, I am so sorry") | The most feeling, carried by a relationship | The reader is primed: the event was foreseen, or the signs of life were named earlier (*Men in the Sun* teaches the rising chest twenty pages before the tank) | The reader is unprimed, so a silence reads as a scene break |
| **A second channel that restates it** (a chapter titled for the grave; "three graves"; a noun that changes from "body" to "corpse"; a burial notice) | Secures a restrained scene without spoiling its restraint | It follows within a page or two | It comes only after the plot has moved on. By then the reader has read the aftermath without its cause |
| **A report** (a messenger; a letter; the telephone) | Distance, and a teller whose relationship colours the news | The teller and the hearer matter, and the hearer has room to disbelieve and question | The hearer accepts at once. Danticat's own revision verdict: "I wish now that I had included more confrontation" |
| **An announcement in advance** | Removes *whether*; every living scene is read under it | The middle keeps renewing the question of how | Nothing in the middle earns the wait |
| **Silence or white space** | The weight of the unsaid | The fact is already secure, by announcement, frame or ritual | Nothing else secures it. The gap reads as a missing scene |

**Imply at the moment, confirm soon after.** This pattern keeps restraint and clarity together. An act, a sound or a formula carries the moment itself. Soon after, often within a page, the plain word comes from a witness, a messenger or the bereaved, and that is where the characters' acceptance happens too. *Palace Walk* uses it again and again. When a neighbour dies, a wail comes from outside, a daughter infers what it means, and her mother goes to see and comes back saying that he really is dead.

**Point of view decides more than style:**
- A distracted or absent viewpoint misses the moment. Either frame it as news that will reach them, or let it land plainly when they learn it.
- A dying viewpoint, in first person or close third, cannot report its own death. The failing perceptions can carry the moment. Then white space, a frame, another voice or a later scene usually confirms it, unless the story chooses uncertainty. *Palace Walk* breaks the chapter and has a formal announcement reach the father within a page and a half. A fast reader can take failing perception for a faint, so the confirmation cannot wait long.
- A guarded viewpoint may refuse the word, provided acts and ritual carry the fact.

**Pace decides how many channels you need.** A death inside a busy scene usually needs two or three channels. A quiet scene can carry it with one act. If the plot must move on at once (an inquiry, a chase, a flight), make sure the reader has the fact before it does.

### 6.3 Keep the fact and the manner apart, and label what stays open
- **A story can make clear *that* someone died while holding back *how*, *by whom* or *why*.** This is the commonest strong design.
  - *The Moonstone* settles a disguised man's death in three lines ("He's in a fit!" / "He's dead,") and delays only who he is, by a visible unmasking.
  - *Celestial Bodies* states a son's death in its first mention and gives the manner fifty pages later, in what the father tells his lover.
- **When the fact itself is meant to stay uncertain, let the text show it:**
  - a character asks aloud ("tears or sweat?", "why didn't they knock?", *Men in the Sun*);
  - the narrator admits he does not know;
  - each version has a named teller with a visible motive;
  - a fair counter-signal appears at once ("still warm", when *Bring Up the Bodies* reports the king dead).

  Then the reader can tell an intended gap from a missing sentence.
- **A soft word** ("gone", "slipped away", «راح») is ambiguous on its own. "Gone" can mean left or dead, and *Men in the Sun* uses «راح» of a brother who is alive. A physical fact, a ritual noun or a reaction nearby settles it, unless the ambiguity is the point.

### 6.4 Elegance around the hinge
- An image, an aphorism or an "as though…" simile can sit *around* the hinge. It can take the hinge's place only when the image itself is unmistakable.
  - "She studied him as if he had answered a question" leaves the reader guessing what she learned.
  - "She studied him, and understood that he had never opened the letter" keeps the understatement and states the change.
- A decisive fact placed inside a figure of speech is easily skimmed. *Palace of Desire* finally gives the manner of a death as the literal half of a simile, and only an attentive reader catches it.
- A closing metaphor at a death works after a stated or shown fact (Anna's candle in Tolstoy follows the train, as Danticat notes). Without that fact, the metaphor is the only signal.
- If what was learned is itself on the intended-uncertainty list (a detective's withheld deduction), state that the change happened and keep its content back: "She read the ledger twice, and knew which of them had lied." Heighten the language around a revelation, not instead of it (`prose.md` §2).

### 6.5 The first-reading test
After each turning point, ask what a first-time reader believes happened, and when they learned it. If the answer differs from the plan, move or add a channel: a plain line, an act, a reaction, a restatement. Do not explain the feeling.
- **Planned clear, reader unsure:** a channel is missing.
- **Planned uncertain, reader thinks a sentence was skipped:** label the doubt.
- **Last lines:** accidental ambiguity in a last line reads as a mistake. If the last line is meant to stay open, design the openness: two or three readings the story supports (`endings.md` §11), or a mystery of meaning (`mystery-and-clues.md` §1).

The room given to consequences after such moments is a separate choice (`emotion.md` §6). Case studies are in `novel-studies.md` §8.

## 7. Speech, quotation and mentioned words
- **Attribution.** This is the one rule other files point to:
  - **Two speakers:** alternation can carry a run. Re-anchor with a name, a tag or an action beat every four to six lines, and after any action beat.
  - **Three or more speakers:** name the speaker of every line where the speaker changes, unless the line's own form fixes it (a vocative, a gendered or dual verb).
  - **In Arabic,** the tag or label goes before the line (`arabic-writing.md` §4).
- **Unattributed lines** must have only one possible speaker.
- **Speech reported inside speech** needs its own marks, or an introducing verb plus a clear end.
- **Mentioned words are not speech.** When a word itself is the subject ("the sign said *closed*", "she said *never*"), mark it as a word: italics, quotation marks, or "the word…" / "the form…". In languages where person or tense lives inside the verb, don't refer to pronouns at all. Quote the actual form, or name the ending (`translation.md` §6.4).
- **Keep perception verbs literal.** If "hear" means *perceive sound* in one scene and *understand* in another, the reader sees a contradiction. Use different verbs: heard / understood / wrote down / corrected.

## 8. Explaining a plot-carrying mechanism
When a plot turns on a mechanism (a letter of the alphabet, a legal technicality, a medical sign, a game rule, a magical law):
- **Explain the rule once, plainly, before it is needed,** in the reader's own language and medium. If the mechanism *is* the solution, plant the general rule (how the script joins letters, what the statute says) without pointing at the instance that will matter. The instance is the reveal.
- **Use the same term every time** afterwards.
- **A hard rule can be reread on the page:** state it once in exact words, have a character reread it, give one
  concrete example, then say what he now understands (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*'s rule for returning through time).
- **Keep a mechanism ledger** (`mystery-and-clues.md` §19). If any row is vague in the writer's own notes, it will be vague on the page.
- **Make sure the mechanism still matters** after later events. If others confirm the false version independently, the mechanism has become an entry point, and the story must find what *keeps* the wrong state alive (silence, fear, a second choice).

## 9. Background at first need
Who someone is to the protagonist, what a place is, a job, a nationality, a date: none of these is a mystery unless the intended-uncertainty list says so. Give each in a clause where it first matters:
- "her son Tomas", not "Tomas" followed by three pages of hints;
- "the hotel where her brother worked and where the fire started";
- "Pell, the court's usual clerk".

Make readers work for what the information plan withholds, never for the basic situation (`information-design.md` §8).

**A fact the plot will stand on needs a main clause the first time,** or a reaction, or a repetition before it matters. A pregnancy or a divorce tucked into a subordinate clause, a list or a flashback is where readers lose it (*Palace Walk* repairs each such fact later, which shows the cost).

**Cultural decoding is not shared.** A brand of cigarettes, a coin or a stamp may date or place a scene for one reader and mean nothing to another. If the plot needs the fact, state it.

## 10. Elliptical dialogue that works and dialogue that strands the reader
Elliptical lines ("You?" "Me." "All day?") are good when three conditions hold:
1. the subject was just established;
2. the gap is the character's evasion, not the writer's;
3. the next line or beat settles the literal sense.

They strand the reader when the line carries **plot information the reader does not yet have** ("Was it hers?", with no clear owner), or when two ellipses follow each other.

**Aphorisms** ("Nobody tells the priest the truth"; "You keep the books") land when the situation they summarize is already clear. They fail when they are asked to *create* the situation.

## 11. Concealment with clarity: techniques
- **Withhold the interpretation, not the event.** Show what was said, done and found, and keep back what it means.
- **Show effects, keep causes.** A torn sleeve, a freshly painted step and cash in a biscuit tin are all clear objects whose cause is hidden.
- **Keep the camera steady while the meaning moves.** Physical action, positions, who holds what and who speaks should be concrete exactly where the meaning is most hidden.
- **Name the question.** Let a character ask the open question aloud or in thought. The reader then knows what is unknown, which is the difference between mystery and fog.
- **Delay by order, not by vagueness.** Put the explaining fact later in time, not later in the same sentence or behind a pronoun.
- **Hold back one thing per scene,** and make everything around it plain.
- **Make the result concrete, hide the method, refuse openly.** "I won't describe the method; the result: a city, a
  house, alive" (*Khawf* 2). The reader knows exactly what is withheld.
- **Show the effect without the content.** We hear that a storyteller's tales hypnotize; we never hear the tales.
- **Give a sensory reason for not knowing an identity:** an echo, a voice that could be a man's or a woman's (*Arsis* 1).
  The action stays clear.
- **Return a known character by situation and ritual,** and put the name at the end of the unit.
- **A fair deceptive line** is literally true in both of its readings ("the mission is over", *Arsis* 2). Never back it
  with a false narrated emotion, and never let first-person narration state a false fact about the basic situation
  without an in-scene signal, such as a sceptic noticing a glitch (`viewpoint-and-narration.md` §6).
- **The worst case:** lore or history that removes the present situation. When five new institutions arrive in two
  lines, the reader loses who the narrator is (*Khawf* 2). Withhold history, not the present.

Examples from the novels studied, in both languages: `novel-studies.md` §13.

## 12. The cold read: checking comprehension without spoiling the secret
Use this after drafting or translating, before presenting a story that withholds anything.
1. **Separate the reader from the plan.** The checker (a second pass, a fresh session, or a human) gets only the text, never the outline or the intended-uncertainty list. If you must check your own text, read it paragraph by paragraph and answer the six questions in §1 *using only what came before*. Mark any answer that relies on later text or on what you know.
2. **Log orientation per paragraph.** For each paragraph, write one line on where and when, who is present, who last acted, and what is being pursued. Note the reader's best guess about each open question, which also feeds the leak audit (`revision.md` §6). Then log each stumble with:
   - a type: referent, speaker, thing, name-or-word, homograph, place-time, action, link, unestablished, mentioned-word or mechanism, dialect or foreign, register or collocation, layout;
   - the exact words;
   - a best guess and a confidence;
   - a severity:
     - minor: a pause, resolved within the sentence;
     - moderate: a reread, or a wrong guess carried for a paragraph or more;
     - serious: a wrong picture of a plot fact that persists, or the reader stops building the model;
   - where (if anywhere) it is resolved.
3. **Classify against the intended-uncertainty list.**
   - **A stumble on a "must not yet know" or "may suspect" item is intended.** Keep it, but check that the reader knows the question exists.
   - **A missed inference on the "should infer" list is a cue problem.** Strengthen the cue (an echo, a gesture, an earlier plant); never add a statement.
   - **A stumble on none of the lists is accidental.** Fix it.
4. **Fix in this order** (the canonical order that other files follow):
   1. links at hinges;
   2. background facts;
   3. referents and speakers;
   4. mentioned-word and mechanism language;
   5. names and homographs;
   6. collocation.

   Most comprehension failures come from the first three, not from word choice.
5. **Re-check only the repaired paragraphs** and their neighbours. Make sure the fixes did not spoil a listed secret: a fix should add orientation, not explanation of the mystery.

A comprehension log template is in `assets/templates/comprehension-log.md`.

## 13. Failure patterns and repairs
| Pattern | Symptom | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| The fog mystery | The reader cannot tell what is unknown, so there is no curiosity, only confusion | Name the question; make the situation concrete; withhold one thing |
| The missed plot match | Following the plot depends on remembering exact words from far back | Echo the words; if a cold read still misses it, let a character make the match once |
| The drifting pronoun | "He" attaches to the last man mentioned, not the intended one | Re-anchor with a name at paragraph starts and after interposed mentions |
| The elegant hinge | A simile or aphorism stands where the reader needed to learn what changed | State the change, or make an act or reaction carry it; keep the image around it (§6.4) |
| The missed decisive event | A death or other irreversible event is understood only later (from a burial, a mourning dress, a changed name), after the story has moved on | Decide when the reader should know (§6.1). Add a channel that fits the point of view: a plain line, an unmistakable act, a framed ritual or a primed reaction (§6.2). If the doubt is intended, label it (§6.3) |
| The unexplained place | A new scene opens somewhere whose function is unknown | One clause: what this place is to the story, and why she is there |
| The mentioned word read as speech | "She wrote: we" is read as a statement | Mark the word as a word, or quote the actual form |
| The shifting verb | "Heard" means sound in one place and understanding in another | Separate the verbs |
| The cultural cue taken for a statement | A key fact is signalled only by a detail some readers cannot decode | State the fact once |
| The stacked signals | A gesture, an aphorism and a coded instruction share one beat | Keep one enigmatic signal; make the others plain |
| The buried fact | A plot fact sits in a subordinate clause, a list or a flashback, with no one reacting, and readers meet its consequences without it | Give it a main clause or a reaction, or repeat it with rising stakes before it is needed |
| The layout break | White space or an ornament suggests a scene change that isn't there | Fix the layout or add an anchor |

## 14. Checklist
- [ ] The intended-uncertainty list exists (must not know / may suspect / should infer / must be clear), and every other fact is recoverable where it is needed.
- [ ] Every scene opening settles what changed (place, time, cast, viewpoint) within the first sentence or two.
- [ ] No pronoun or omitted subject can attach to a wrong nearby person.
- [ ] Every reply is recognisably a response (to the line, to an earlier question, or to the subtext), or a visible refusal.
- [ ] Every plot-critical long-distance match has an echo, and a cold read shows readers make it.
