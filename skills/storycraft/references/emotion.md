# Emotion: making the audience care, and making sadness mean something

Emotion in story is produced, not described. It comes from an audience that cares about someone, understands what they
stand to lose, and watches them choose under pressure. A character crying on screen moves no one unless we know who
they are, what led here, and what it costs (Howard & Mabley).

**The core mechanism** (McKee): the audience feels when a value it cares about changes charge. That needs (1) empathy
for a character, (2) a desire the audience wants fulfilled, (3) clear stakes, and then (4) a turn. Positive turns
produce positive feeling, negative turns negative feeling. Mood, music and description can make an emotion specific,
but they never replace the turn. Cause emotion; don't cue it.

## 1. The bond: empathy before jeopardy
- **Empathy is required; likability is optional** (McKee). Sympathy = "I like them." Empathy = "they're like me": the
  audience recognizes a shared humanity and therefore wants the character to get what they want.
- Audiences empathize with characters "because of what they are doing about" their pain, not because they're in pain
  (Walter Bernstein, quoted by Howard & Mabley). Effort earns attachment; passivity drains it.
- The audience's concern grows with the character's own intensity of desire: a protagonist who barely cares is poor
  dramatic material (Howard & Mabley).
- **Feeling *with* vs feeling *for*.** When the audience knows what the character knows (suspense), it identifies and
  feels with them. When it knows more (dramatic irony), it watches from above and feels for them: dread and compassion
  (McKee). Choose per sequence; sad stories usually need both.
- **Order matters:** establish the bond, then threaten it. Pixar's *Up* first builds a lifelong marriage (the house,
  the unkept promise of adventure) so that everything after carries its weight (Docter, in The Ringer's making-of
  account).

## 2. Building attachment fast
Use several of these in the first beats:
- **A specific want, actively pursued** (even a small one: to get the boots on, to fix the clock before she wakes).
- **Competence or effort** under difficulty: we admire trying more than succeeding (Coats's Pixar rule #1).
- **Vulnerability or injustice:** an undeserved misfortune or a hidden fear (claustrophobia, a dying parent, debt).
- **A bond shown in action:** a ritual between two people, a kindness that costs something, a shared joke, a promise.
- **Specific, concrete detail:** the named object, the habit, the way they make tea. Specificity is what makes a
  character feel real enough to lose.
- **Conscience:** let us see them struggle with right and wrong (Macbeth's soliloquies make a murderer tragic; McKee).
- **The Center of Good:** the audience searches the opening for the positive core of the story and attaches there
  (McKee). Make sure your protagonist holds it.

## 3. Dark or difficult protagonists
- Give them at least one genuinely positive quality (loyalty, courage, love for someone, wit, honesty about themselves)
  and **surround them with worse** (McKee: the Corleones' loyalty among backstabbers; Lecter's poise among a sadistic
  psychiatrist and a lying FBI).
- Let us see "the human heart suffering inside" (Howard & Mabley), and their attitude toward their own flaws.
- Avoid hypocrisy and self-pity without action; audiences detach from hypocrites (McKee's critique of a whining
  protagonist who could end his misery but doesn't).
- A sympathetic hero needs an undesirable side, or there is no tension about whether they'll do what it takes
  (Howard & Mabley).
- Keep the protagonist the most dimensional character, or empathy drifts to a more contradictory rival (McKee; see
  character.md §4).

## 4. Objective and subjective drama
- **Objective drama** is dramatic on the surface without knowing anyone: a baby crawling toward a cliff edge, a gun, an
  explosion. **Subjective drama** depends on knowing and caring: a claustrophobic man who must lock himself in a closet
  to get what he wants even more (Frank Daniel via Howard & Mabley).
- Pure objective drama escalates into numbness ("the guns have to get bigger"); pure subjective drama risks "too little
  happens." The most memorable moments fuse both (a blind woman we care about, stalked by killers we can see before she
  can).
- For sad stories, invest in subjective drama: small events become devastating when we know what they mean to someone.

## 5. Showing feeling: actions, objects, subtext
- **Externalize the internal.** Find actions that reveal inner states: slamming someone against a wall says more than
  "I'm angry" (Howard & Mabley). Order of invention: actions first, then activities, then dialogue.
- **Subtext:** what appears to be happening vs what is really happening. Never write a scene "about what it's about":
  put the real feeling under a surface task (McKee). A gun pulled on a lover can play as love, admiration and apology
  (Casablanca). Build it by controlling who knows what.
- **At the emotional peak, let the body, the object or the silence carry it:** words the body contradicts, strained
  politeness, a refusal to answer, a hand that keeps working (Maass, via a summary; consistent with McKee and Howard &
  Mabley). Anchor the beat in one concrete sensory detail rather than a named emotion.
- **Activities that reveal tenderness** without advancing plot can be the most moving moments (Evelyn cleaning Jake's
  cut nose in Chinatown).
- **Objects as carriers of emotion:** a planted object that returns with a new meaning becomes a metaphor (the palm tree
  in *Mister Roberts*; *Up*'s house). For sad stories, choose an object the audience watches the character love.
- **Attitude toward traits** creates character: Cyrano's nose matters because of how he feels about it (Howard &
  Mabley).
- **Write from the inside out:** ask "If I were this character in these circumstances, what would I do?", feel it,
  then step back and ask "What is the opposite of that?" to find the reaction that opens a gap (McKee). Emotional
  truth comes from inhabiting the character; outside-in writing produces clichés.
- **Emphasize reactions.** Many events are expected; the emotion lives in how they land on a particular person (McKee).
- **Let awkwardness run.** Refusing to relieve an uncomfortable moment reveals character and holds the audience
  (Howard & Mabley on *sex, lies, and videotape* and *Annie Hall*).
- **Restraint:** in *Up*'s montage Docter kept removing material and found "the less we had the more emotional it
  felt." Don't explain the feeling the image already delivers.

## 6. Shaping the emotional rhythm
- **Preparation and aftermath** (Howard & Mabley): scenes in which characters and audience brace for, or absorb, a
  major event, often carried by music, atmosphere and image. They don't advance plot; they deepen feeling. After
  intense action, give a quiet beat to absorb it.
- **Preparation by contrast:** before a blow, lift the audience to the opposite emotion ("one of the five best days of
  my life" just before his wife leaves). The bigger the swing, the harder the hit.
- **Alternate the charge of consecutive scenes** (McKee): three scenes in a row turning the same way (three sad, or
  three triumphant) numb the audience; a third consecutive tragic scene can even draw laughter. A same-direction step
  works only if the contrast is big enough to recolor what came before.
- **Alternate tension and release** (McKee): relentless intensity numbs. After each act climax, lower the pressure
  (humor, intimacy), then build higher than before. Slow down at the moment that matters most.
- **Escalate the swings** between hope and fear across the story (Howard & Mabley).
- **Give the sad moment time.** A climactic loss scene is long and slow, not skimmed; "earn the pause" by moving faster
  before it (McKee).
- **Carry emotion across a cut:** a matched transition can carry one scene's feeling into the next (Howard & Mabley).

## 7. Sadness that means something
Research and craft agree on what separates moving sadness from merely depressing material:
- Aristotle: pity is aroused by **unmerited misfortune**; fear by misfortune that befalls **someone like us**.
- Viewers value sad and poignant stories as "moving and thought-provoking" experiences with a lasting effect, a response
  researchers call **appreciation**, distinct from fun or thrill (Oliver & Bartsch, 2010).
- In one study, viewers whose sadness rose most during a tragedy thought more about their own **close relationships**,
  which raised their happiness and their enjoyment of the film (Knobloch-Westerwick et al.; Ohio State release).
  Tragedies work when they point the heart toward love, not when they only display suffering.
Design rules that follow:
1. **The loss must be of something we watched them love** (a person, a place, a promise, a dream, a self-image).
   Define stakes as exactly what will be lost on failure.
2. **The loss must cost a choice or reveal one.** Sacrifice, mistakes, missed chances and unsaid words hurt more than
   random accidents.
3. **The meaning must outlive the event:** the sadness should reveal what mattered (love, loyalty, forgiveness,
   courage), so the ending feels true rather than cruel.
4. **Let the audience arrive first** (dramatic irony) or **arrive together** (shared recognition). Both are stronger
   than being told afterward.
5. **Measure the loss by contrast:** re-stage an earlier happy moment after the loss (the same ritual with one chair
   empty; the lobster scene replayed with a new partner in *Annie Hall*), or let a beloved line return in another mouth
   (Howard & Mabley).
6. **Leave a trace:** an object, gesture or image that carries the loss forward (the key image; see endings.md).
7. **Hold back.** Under-dramatize the expression of grief; over-dramatize the specific detail.
8. **No false nostalgia:** an idealized world has nothing to lose. Hopes must be able to fail and people must have base
   emotions beside better ones (Howard & Mabley on *Diner*).

## 8. A design sequence for heartbreak
A synthesis drawn from McKee's two full scene analyses (Casablanca's Paris market scene; the climax of *Through a Glass
Darkly*) and his chapters on antagonism and irony. Use it as a checklist, not a formula:
1. **Open on hope.** Establish what the character longs for and let the audience want it too.
2. **Bring it within reach.** Let it nearly happen: the reunion, the confession, the cure.
3. **Let the blow come through the character's own flaw or choice**, not chance: pride, fear, a lie, a promise kept
   too rigidly.
4. **Make the blow destroy the exact basis of the hope**, not something adjacent: the thing she trusted is the thing
   that failed her.
5. **Push the value to its negation of the negation:** grief that becomes self-blame, love that turns out to have been
   control, a truth the character hid even from themselves (see character.md §6).
6. **Let the audience see what the character can't** (dramatic irony): a half-truth revelation gives the character
   false insight while the audience holds the real one.
7. **Give the aftermath its silence,** then a final image that crystallizes the meaning.
Two more patterns from Howard & Mabley's analyses: **doom from locked opposites** (two characters who can't escape each
other, with one exit that would satisfy both, destroyed by a deliberate choice: Stanley exposing Blanche to Mitch in
*A Streetcar Named Desire*); and **downfall through virtue** (the protagonist's best quality causes their destruction,
as McMurphy's defiance does in *Cuckoo's Nest*).

## 9. Tragedy, irony and bittersweetness
- **Tragic recognition:** the protagonist comes to understand the truth, often about their own role, at the moment it
  reverses their fortune (Aristotle's Oedipus).
- **Ironic patterns** (McKee) make heartbreak precise: getting what you always wanted, too late; throwing away what you
  later learn was essential; the act meant to destroy something destroys you.
- **Bittersweet = loss on one level, gain on another.** Lose the external goal, win the internal one (or the reverse);
  or lose personally while the philosophical value triumphs (see endings.md, stakes convergence).
- **Dramatic irony as tragic engine:** open with the ending (a death message, a grave, a final letter), then let the
  audience watch with dread and compassion as the characters walk toward it. Curiosity shifts to why and how.
- **Comedy and tragedy share the gap.** Comedy turns the gap into a laugh; tragedy into insight. Mixing them (the tragic
  in the comic; McKee's "variety within unity") often makes sadness hit harder.

## 10. Sentimentality and other failures
- **Telling the feeling:** characters announcing their emotions or the theme. Fix: action, object, subtext, silence.
- **Manipulative suffering:** a death or cruelty introduced only to make the audience cry, unconnected to choice or
  meaning. Fix: tie it to the protagonist's want and decision; give it consequences.
- **Melodrama:** an event bigger than its motivation. Fix: deepen the motivation rather than shrinking the event
  (McKee).
- **Unearned bond:** jeopardy before attachment. Fix: invest in the relationship first.
- **Monotone grief:** every scene at the same sad pitch. Fix: contrast, humor, warmth, preparation by contrast.
- **Victim protagonist:** a character who only suffers. Fix: give them a want, a plan and a choice. Suffering is not
  drama (McKee).
- **Mood instead of turn:** lyrical atmosphere where nothing changes. Fix: find the value that turns in the scene.
- **Explaining the tears:** a speech after the image lands. Fix: cut it (the Chinatown rewrite).
- **Hypocrisy:** a protagonist complaining about a situation they could leave without cost. Fix: raise the cost or
  change the want.

## 11. Emotion checklist
- [ ] Do we care about someone before anything threatens them? How, specifically (want, effort, vulnerability, bond)?
- [ ] Is the protagonist doing something about their situation?
- [ ] Does every emotional peak come from a value changing charge, not from mood alone?
- [ ] Is the central emotion carried by actions, objects and subtext rather than statements?
- [ ] Are there preparation/aftermath beats around the biggest blows, and at least one preparation by contrast?
- [ ] Do consecutive scenes alternate charge, building higher each time?
- [ ] Is the loss something we watched them love, tied to a choice, and meaningful afterwards?
- [ ] Does the ending deliver the emotion the story promised, with a lasting image?
