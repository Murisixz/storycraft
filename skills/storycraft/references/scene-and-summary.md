# Scene and summary: what to stage, what to compress, how to carry time

Readers live through scenes and are informed by summary. A story whose decisive moments arrive as summary or report
can be beautifully designed and still feel like a synopsis. The skill is in deciding, beat by beat, which moments the
reader must live through, which they only need to know, and how to make the compressed parts carry life too.

## 1. The four speeds and the three frequencies
| Speed | Story time vs reading time | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Scene** | about equal; moment by moment, with dialogue, action and perception | turning points, confrontations, decisions, recognitions, anything the reader must feel |
| **Summary** | story time faster | connective time, routine, background, acceleration toward a scene |
| **Ellipsis** | story time passes with no text | dead time; deliberate gaps whose absence the reader feels |
| **Pause** | reading time slower (description, reflection, a stopped moment) | perception, dread, memory, the beat before a blow |

**Frequency** (often called singulative, iterative and repeating narration):
- **Singular:** tell once what happened once.
- **Iterative:** tell once what happened many times ("every morning he swept the shop step").
- **Repeating:** tell several times what happened once, through witnesses, memories or versions.

Iterative narration is the most efficient way to carry years, and the most dangerous, because nothing in it can turn.
Anchor it (§4).

## 2. The must-stage list
Stage these by default:
- **decisions** made under pressure, especially the crisis;
- **reversals:** the moment things turn;
- **confrontations, confessions, refusals and proposals;**
- **recognitions:** a character learns what the reader knows, or learns the truth;
- **first and last times:** the first meeting, the last conversation, the first night of the long absence;
- **consequences:** the scene after a revelation in which someone acts differently;
- **the obligatory scene** the story has promised (Archer; McKee);
- **anything the ending depends on** emotionally.

**Exceptions, each needing a reason:**
- **The reader already knows** the event and its meaning, so staging would repeat. Summarize, or skip to the reaction.
- **Deliberate ellipsis.** The story withholds the event (from a narrator who can't bear it, or to create a gap the
  reader fills). Then make the reader *feel* the gap: an aftermath scene, changed behaviour, a hole in the telling.
  Make sure the reader knows *that* the event happened, at the point the design intends, unless the doubt is itself
  designed and labelled (clarity-and-orientation.md §6).
- **Offstage events,** which happen where the viewpoint character isn't. Stage the character's *learning* of the event
  as the scene: the news, the evidence, the witness. Give the hearer room to disbelieve, question or resist before
  accepting it. Danticat judged her own early handling of a death reported off stage too accepting ("I wish now that I
  had included more confrontation"). A teller with a relationship to the dead colours the news.
- **Late disclosure in passing** (a death mentioned months later, in a clause) is economy and characterization for
  minor figures: the viewpoint does not dwell. For someone the reader followed, it reads as a gap and risks the reader
  never registering the death (*Wolf Hall* uses it both ways).

If the most dramatic events in the story reach the reader as one character's account in another's scene, check §6.

## 3. When summary is right
- **Transitions** between scenes and places.
- **Routine and habit:** what life was like for ten years.
- **Background** the reader needs quickly and won't feel, such as facts, procedures and history.
- **Acceleration:** summary speeds up toward a scene and makes it land harder by contrast.
- **Attitude:** summary in a narrator's voice, with judgment, humour or evasion, characterizes the narrator while
  covering ground.
- **Mercy:** some suffering is stronger glimpsed than staged at length, but stage the decision that caused it or the
  moment it's discovered.

## 4. Summary that lives
- **Iterative plus instance.** Follow "every Friday she brought him the newspaper" with one specific Friday in scene: what was
  said, what was refused, what went wrong. The iterative gives duration; the instance gives life.
- **Change inside the summary.** A summarized stretch should move something: a relationship cools, a habit hardens, a
  hope dies a little. Summary in which nothing changes is filler. Cut it to a clause.
- **Concrete detail, not event lists.** One exact detail (a price, an object, a phrase someone kept saying) carries a
  year better than a list of what happened.
- **Voice.** Summary told with a narrator's attitude reads as character, not information: what they dwell on, what
  they skip, how they joke about it.
- **Pressure from the present.** Summary placed inside a live scene, as memory triggered under strain, borrows that
  scene's tension. The reader keeps watching the present while the past arrives.
- **One time phrase and one countable result per step.** Carry two years of apprenticeship as day five (a dropped
  bottle), day seven (the first wage), the second year (the first case alone), and dramatize only the failures and the
  turns (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*).
- **Proportion check.** If a summarized moment feels as if it deserves more, it's probably a scene. Stage it.

## 5. Carrying long time spans
*Examples:* novel-studies.md §5 (more than a dozen ways the studied novels carry years).
Stories covering years or decades need a strategy, not a run of "years passed" paragraphs. Choose one or combine a few:
| Strategy | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Stations** | Pick a handful of moments across the span; stage each; each changes something; summary links them | stations that only repeat the situation |
| **Leap with consequence** | Jump years, then open on a scene that shows what time did (aging, new routines, changed loyalties) | leaps with no visible effect |
| **Retrospective selection** | A narrator remembers specific episodes from the span, digresses, returns, and comments from the present | memory that summarizes instead of re-entering scenes |
| **Frame with embedded past** | A present-day line (an investigation, a confession, a visit) drives the uncovering of the past | the present line going slack while the past is told |
| **Braided timelines** | Alternate two periods, each with its own forward pull, converging | one line much weaker than the other |
| **Mobile observer** | A narrator not bound to one time or place (an omniscient voice, a ghost, a historian) surveys many lives | spread so thin that no one is lived with |
| **Documents** | Letters, diaries, records and reports span years quickly, in other voices | documents that explain instead of dramatize |
| **Time-scale skeleton** | Headers that count the span ("the first minute … the first year … the last minute"), or a recurring calendar event counted per character | headers with no turn between them |

**Rules for any strategy:**
- **Keep a present tension running across the span:** a question, a danger, a promise, a clock.
- **Show what time did** in a scene: bodies, rooms, money, relationships, the way people speak to each other.
- **Mark leaps clearly** (date, season, age, an event everyone remembers) and re-establish who, where and what's at stake
  quickly after each one.
- **Let at least one secondary life change** visibly across the span, not only the protagonist's.
- Novella and novel structures for this are in long-form.md §4.

## 6. Tellings, confessions and testimony as scenes
When a character tells what happened, the telling can be a strong scene if it is a scene, not a transcript.
- **The teller wants something** from the listener: forgiveness, belief, help, to wound, to be rid of the secret, to
  recruit. The telling is a tactic.
- **The listener resists, reacts or interrupts.** Questions, disbelief, anger, a refusal to hear the rest.
- **The telling is selective.** The teller omits, softens, lies or saves the worst for last. Readers attend to what is
  being managed.
- **Ground it physically.** Business with objects, a place, an interruption from outside, time pressure.
- **The relationship changes by the end.** The telling is an event between the two people, not only a delivery of
  facts.
- **For long accounts,** let the telling dissolve into dramatized past (a scene inside the account, with dialogue), then
  return to the present listener at moments of strain. Or split the account across several sittings or several
  witnesses, each with their own agenda and distortions (mystery-and-clues.md §10).
- **Interrupt a long account with concrete present beats** (a traffic light, a horn, the listener's interjection), so
  the reader stays in place and time while the past is told (*Khawf* 1).
- **An offstage event can be delivered twice:** first as broken fragments to a helpless listener whose life depends on
  them, then as a coherent account (a battle reported by a medium while the hero's shroud lies ready, *Khawf* 1).
- **Frame an event the viewpoint will only hear about** as a later report ("But later he, Cromwell, will hear…"). It
  can then be narrated as a scene without breaking the point of view (*Bring Up the Bodies*). An offstage killing of
  many can come through a deputy with standing, as a count, while the viewpoint is somewhere meaningful at that hour
  (the same book).
- **Watch the reveal monologue.** One person explaining the backstory to a silent listener is the commonest form of
  explanation overload (information-design.md §7).

## 7. Interiority as scene time
In prose, thought can be scene time. Moment-by-moment perception, calculation, temptation and decision, pressed by the
present situation, is dramatized interiority, and a long scene can be mostly inside one head. Reflection afterwards
("later she saw what the visit had cost him") is summary. Both are legitimate, but don't let reflection replace
the moment it reflects on: stage the moment, then let the reflection be short.

## 8. Chapters, breaks and transitions
- **Chapters are units of tension.** End on an opened question, a turn, a decision pending, or a quiet line that
  reverses the chapter's assumption. Open the next one quickly on who, where and when.
- **Use section breaks to cut dead time** and to make leaps. A break plus a changed situation tells the reader time has
  passed without a sentence saying so.
- **Use transitions to carry feeling** across cuts through a shared or opposed element: an object, a word, a sound, a
  light, a gesture (McKee; Howard & Mabley). Two chapter endings in one sentence frame can link two worlds before the
  plot does: two skies, the same "it has appeared again" (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*).
- **Cross-cutting** between lines can stretch suspense. Its cost is that each cut interrupts immersion, so cut at
  moments of maximum question, not mid-feeling (long-form.md §6). Open each segment with its subject and place in the
  first few words, and mark simultaneity when it matters ("at the same hour…"). Give every re-entry its own clock
  phrase when several strands converge (*Amarita*'s six places before its climax). A slug line (place, then time) lets
  strands run with no connective (*Arsis* 2).
- **State the length of a time skip at the skip** ("a month passed…"). A skip measured only later leaves the reader
  unable to weigh absences and progress (*Ard Zikola*).
- **After a chapter break inside continuous action,** re-enter through the hearer's reaction, a one-line recap of the
  hook, or the same beat replayed from the new narrator's side (*Amarita*). When you return to a strand, repeat its
  last line (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). Chapter breaks can be cliffhanger cuts in the middle of the action, but not between a
  question and the answer the reader needs to follow the scene.
- **A chapter break can carry a death.** One chapter ends on the failing moment, and the next opens elsewhere and
  closes the gap within a page or two (*Palace Walk*). The longer the confirmation waits, the more readers lose the
  event.
- **After a great death, a cut to routine** (letters, an itinerary, the day's work) can show the death being absorbed
  (*Wolf Hall*). It reads as indifference when nothing earlier showed what the death cost.
- **Intercut two events without comment,** and let them judge each other: a corpse prepared while a feast goes on
  (*Wolf Hall*).
- **An iterative opening** ("as every night") sets a baseline so every later deviation reads as an event (*Palace
  Walk*'s first eighteen pages). It costs momentum, so keep it as short as the deviation needs.
- **A happy event offstage:** flag the ellipsis and give its result in one plain sentence (*The Moonstone*).
- **Replays** (a memory, a time loop, a second chance) reuse the exact earlier sentences and headers, so the reader
  recognizes the loop, and change only what has changed (*Arsis* 1).
- **Leaving a flashback:** rejoin the main line by repeating an earlier sentence verbatim (*Khawf* 1), or cut back with a
  sound in the present, such as a bell or a phone (*Arsis*). For the markers that enter a memory, see
  clarity-and-orientation.md §3.

## 9. Proportion: where the words go
- **Measure the shares.** List the beats and their word counts. The beats that turn the story should get the largest
  shares. A major event that gets one sentence while a minor one gets two pages is a design error, unless the
  compression is a deliberate effect the reader can feel.
- **Check what is staged versus summarized.** Mark the must-stage list in the margin with S (staged) or R/Σ (reported or
  summarized). Every R or Σ needs a reason from §2.
- **Weigh the climax scene.** It is usually among the longest scenes, and its aftermath and consequence scenes come
  right after.
- **Proportion can make a statement.** One plain sentence and one personal detail for a minor death (*Bring Up the
  Bodies*), or a page for a neighbour's (*Palace Walk*), says how this world values the dead. The same brevity for
  someone the reader followed reads as neglect. As Danticat puts it, "Death always wants to hog the stage", "But it
  does not get to hog every stage."
- **When deaths cluster,** give each a different room and voice (a child's question, a grandmother's formula, a
  brother's philosophy, a newsboy's cry), and name the cluster only once (*Palace of Desire*'s last chapter).
  Sameness numbs.
- **Dialogue share is a signal, not a rule.** Low-dialogue books can be fully dramatized through perception and
  interiority. A low-dialogue draft with little perception or interiority is almost certainly summary (prose.md §8).

## 10. Failure patterns
| Symptom | Cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| "Summarized more than dramatized" | key moments in summary; scope too large for length | must-stage list; narrow or re-time (revision.md §4) |
| Offstage climax | the decisive event happens between scenes | stage it, or stage the discovery of it |
| "And then years passed" | leap with no consequence | open after the leap on a scene that shows what time did |
| Reveal monologue | backstory told to a silent listener | §6: goal, resistance, selectivity, physical grounding; distribute backstory earlier |
| Montage prose | lyrical summary standing in for scenes | pick the two or three moments that change things; stage them |
| Static scene | a scene in which nothing turns | redesign the turn (structure-and-scenes.md §7) or cut to summary |
| Bloat | everything staged, including routine | summarize routine; save scene time for turns |
| The death learned from its consequences | a death or departure reaches the reader only through a later burial, mourning or absence | decide when the reader should know; add a channel that fits the viewpoint (clarity-and-orientation.md §6) |
| The invested death in a clause | someone the reader followed dies in passing, late | stage the learning, or return to it later through someone close |
| Lost thread after a flashback | the present line went slack | return to the present at a moment of strain; keep its question alive |

## 11. Checklist
- [ ] Is every item on the must-stage list staged, or does it have a stated reason not to be?
- [ ] Is each iterative summary anchored by at least one singular instance?
- [ ] Does each summarized stretch change something?
- [ ] Does the long-span strategy keep a present tension running and show what time did?
- [ ] Are tellings and confessions scenes (goal, resistance, selectivity, change)?
- [ ] Does the share of words follow the importance of the beats?
- [ ] Do chapter endings open questions or turns, and do openings re-establish quickly after leaps?
