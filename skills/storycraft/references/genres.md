# Genres: contracts, conventions, core values

A genre is a promise to the audience: a kind of experience, a value that will be put at stake, and scenes they expect
to see. Audiences arrive with these expectations and judge the story against them (McKee). Use genre as a creative
constraint, not a formula: the convention is required, the cliché is not.

## 1. Genre as contract
- Name the genre (or genres) before designing. It decides the core value at stake, the expected climax charge, the
  obligatory scenes, and how much complexity characters need (simple for action and farce, complex for education and
  redemption plots; McKee).
- Meeting expectations in an unexpected way is the source of genre pleasure. Failing to deliver an obligatory scene
  feels like a broken promise; delivering it in its most familiar form feels like a cliché.

## 2. Conventions: setting, role, event, value
McKee sorts genre conventions into four kinds. List them for your story:
- **Setting:** the kind of world (the frontier, a closed community, a haunted house, a city at night).
- **Role:** the characters the genre requires (detective and culprit; lovers and the forces that block them; victim and
  monster).
- **Event:** the scenes the audience expects (the discovery of the body; the meet; the hero at the villain's mercy).
- **Value:** the core value at stake and the expected charge at the climax (justice restored or betrayed; love won or
  lost; life or death).

## 3. Keep the convention, avoid the cliché
- **Never trust the first idea** (McKee): for each key scene, list 5–15 alternatives and pick the one truest to these
  characters and this world and least seen. If the cliché still wins, list fresh variations of it.
- Treat each obligatory scene as a problem to solve uniquely.
- **Update conventions to the present:** ask "What's to stop them?" (McKee's question for love stories, useful for every
  genre) and choose an opposing force today's audience will believe.

## 5. Focus genres in depth

### Mystery and crime
- Core value: **justice** (and **truth**). The audience expects a crime, an investigator who gathers clues and suspects,
  and an exposure (McKee's crime conventions); the subgenre follows whose point of view we share (McKee).
- The emotional range depends on mode: pure whodunit = puzzle pleasure; suspense and irony = fear for someone (see
  information-design.md). For heartbreak, center the why and the cost of the truth (mystery-and-clues.md §12).
- Push the value to its limit: unfairness → injustice → tyranny (injustice made law, or the investigator's own
  corruption) (McKee's justice spectrum).
- Obligatory scenes: the discovery of the crime; the investigator's commitment; the false solution; the confrontation
  with the culprit; the exposure and its cost.

### Thriller and suspense
- Core value: **life/death**, often with justice or freedom. Expect a protagonist in mortal danger from an opponent with
  a will and a plan, a clock, and a climax where the protagonist is at the antagonist's mercy (McKee lists the hero at
  the villain's mercy as a must for action stories).
- Make the opponent stronger and smarter than the protagonist (the underdog principle), and hide part of their plan.
- Use dramatic irony (show the bomb) for dread; keep two outcomes plausible to the end.
- Cheap surprises (shock cuts, fake scares) are an accepted convention here and in horror, but use them sparingly
  (McKee).

### Horror
- Core value: **life/death**, often deepened to **sanity/madness** or **salvation/damnation** (consciousness →
  unconsciousness → death → damnation in McKee's spectrum).
- Choose the source deliberately: **uncanny** (explicable: a maniac, a creature, science), **supernatural** (a spirit
  realm), or **super-uncanny** (the audience is kept unsure which it is) (McKee). Ambiguity between the two is a strong
  tool for mystery-horror.
- Set the threat's rules early and keep them; dread grows from what the audience can foresee, not from randomness.
- The most haunting horror is about something human: guilt, grief, a secret. Give the monster a meaning.

### Tragedy and disillusionment
- Tragedy turns on a protagonist's error or blind spot, a reversal of fortune, and a recognition that comes too late
  (Aristotle). Pity requires undeserved or disproportionate suffering; fear requires a protagonist like us.
- **Disillusionment plot** (McKee): an idealist's hopes are poisoned turn by turn, ending in a negative worldview.
  **Punitive plot:** a good person turns bad and is punished.
- **Tragic irony** (McKee): the obsessive gets exactly what they wanted, and it destroys them.
- To keep tragedy from feeling cruel, let it reveal what mattered (see emotion.md §7–8).

### Love and longing
- Core value: **love** (and its counterfeits: hatred posing as love, possession, self-sacrifice that erases the self).
- The central design question is "What's to stop them?" Find a blocking force that is credible today: duty, class,
  illness, distance, fear, a secret, another love, the self.
- A **longing story** (one of McKee's subgenres) lives in what is unspoken and unconsummated; a **buddy salvation**
  story replaces romance with friendship.
- Bittersweet endings often belong here: one lover renounces the other for a greater value.

### Quiet, bittersweet and slice-of-life
- These stories still turn values, at a lower temperature (see structure-and-scenes.md §13 on miniplot and low-conflict
  shapes).
- **Positive irony** (McKee): winning by losing, a renunciation that feels like a victory. **Negative irony:** gaining
  the goal while losing the self.
- An ironic ending is one action that says two clear things, each honestly motivated; it is not vagueness or ambiguity
  (McKee).
- Small events carry great weight when the audience knows what they mean to someone (subjective drama). Ration plot;
  intensify specificity.

### Plots of inner change (maturation, redemption, testing, education)
- The value at stake is inside the protagonist; the external plot is the pressure that changes them (McKee). They need
  complex characters and choices that cost more each time.
- **Education plot:** from a negative view of life to a positive one. **Redemption plot:** from bad to good.
  **Testing plot:** will against the temptation to give up. **Maturation:** from dependence to self-possession.
- These pair naturally with any outer genre (a sports story about redemption, a crime story about education).

## 6. The world's rules (setting and probability)
- Fix the setting in four dimensions: period, duration, location and level of conflict (McKee).
- Every world, realistic or fantastic, establishes its own causal laws; once set, they can't be broken. Fantasy is the
  strictest: allow one leap of premise, then no coincidences (McKee).
- Keep the world small enough to know completely; there are no portable stories, as the specific world generates
  specific events (McKee).
- Research through memory, imagination and fact; let research generate events and twists, not information dumps.

## 7. Mixing genres
- Combine genres deliberately, for meaning, character and range of mood (McKee): a crime plot carrying a redemption
  plot; a love story inside a war story.
- Decide which genre leads (which value decides the climax) and which supports.
- Comedy inside tragedy (or the reverse) can sharpen both, through contrast and variety within unity.

## 8. Signaling the genre
- Signal the genre honestly through the title, the opening and the early beats; misleading genre signals breed
  disappointment, however good the story (McKee).
- In short video, the first seconds are the genre signal: an image, sound or text that tells the viewer what kind of
  feeling to lean toward.
