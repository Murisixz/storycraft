# Voice and dialogue: people who sound like themselves

Dialogue is action between people: each line is a move to get something from someone. When every character talks in
the narrator's polished register, the people merge into one voice and the scene becomes a recital. Distinct voices
come from distinct people (what they want, know, fear, notice and avoid), not from accents laid on top.

## 1. What dialogue is for
- **Each exchange is a transaction.** Someone wants something (information, reassurance, power, escape, intimacy,
  revenge) and uses words to get it. The other person wants something too.
- **A good line does several jobs** (Howard & Mabley):
  - characterizes;
  - reveals or hides motive;
  - reflects the relationship;
  - advances the action;
  - carries exposition;
  - foreshadows.
  A line that does only one job, usually information, is a candidate for cutting.
- **Speech is selected, not recorded.** It is compressed and directed while still sounding natural; real talk's
  repetitions and stalls are used only when they characterize.

## 2. Building distinct voices (and the voice test)
Build each major voice from the person outwards. For each character, decide:
- **Diction:** which words they own (technical, slang, formal, borrowed from a trade, a faith, a class, a generation)
  and which words they would never use.
- **Syntax and rhythm:** long and qualified sentences or blunt fragments? Questions or statements? Do they finish
  their sentences?
- **Register shifts:** how they speak upward, to superiors, and downward, to children, servants and juniors. Their
  register at ease and under strain.
- **What they notice and name first:** money, danger, clothes, food, status, feelings, mechanics, weather.
- **How they dodge:** a joke, a question back, a change of subject, pedantry, silence, aggression, sudden courtesy.
- **Verbal habits:** a catchphrase used sparingly, a favourite oath or a refusal to swear, nicknames they give others,
  how they open and close a conversation.
- **Humour:** what they find funny, and whether they joke to connect, to wound or to hide.
- **Knowledge and limits:** what they know too much about and what they get wrong. People's errors are as
  characterizing as their expertise.

**The voice test.** Cover the speaker tags in a scene. If you can't tell who is speaking from the words alone, the
voices aren't built yet. Rewrite from the list above, not by adding accents.

**Keep it proportionate.** Minor characters need one or two marked traits of speech; major characters need a whole
way of talking that changes under pressure.

## 3. Dialogue rhythm and mechanics
- **People don't answer exactly what was asked.** They answer the question before last, the question they wish had
  been asked, or the subtext (McKee). Use this to show what is really on someone's mind.
- **Interruptions, trailing off and overlap** show power and emotion. Who gets to finish sentences?
- **Silence and non-answers** are lines too. Describe the action or stillness that replaces a reply.
- **Vary speech length.** A long speech is an event; give it a reason (persuasion, confession, a lecture someone
  resents) and let someone react to it.
- **Tags and beats:** "said" is invisible; elaborate verbs draw attention. Use action beats (what someone does while
  speaking) to stage the scene and show subtext, not as decoration on every line. Make the speaker clear in every
  exchange of three or more lines.
- **Put the most important word last** in the line, and the most important line last in the exchange (Howard & Mabley;
  Truby, via outline).

## 4. Subtext and indirection
- **Never write the scene about what it's about** (McKee). Put the real conflict under a surface task or topic: a
  quarrel about the dishes that is really about the affair; a polite tea that is a duel.
- **Characters rarely name their feelings.** When they do, it is often a tactic (to disarm, to accuse, to end the
  conversation), or a breaking point.
- **Lies and evasions characterize.** Note what each character lies about, how badly, and who notices.
- **Let the reader know more than one speaker** (dramatic irony). Then every line plays double (information-design.md
  §5).

## 5. Dialogue as conflict: tactics and power
- **Map tactics, not topics.** For each beat, name the move:
  - demand, plead, bargain;
  - flatter, threaten, shame;
  - seduce, deflect;
  - withdraw, appeal to rules, appeal to the past.
  Tactics change when they fail (structure-and-scenes.md §7).
- **Power shifts** inside a conversation are turns. Track who holds the advantage at the start and at the end.
- **Allies argue too.** Friends, lovers and family want different things and say so, often unfairly. Conflict between
  people who love each other is the most revealing kind (character.md §9).

## 6. Exposition in dialogue and the explainer problem
- **Ban "As you know".** Characters don't tell each other what both already know.
- **Make information ammunition** (McKee): someone uses a fact to win, to hurt, to persuade or to protect themselves.
- **The explainer character** (the expert, the guide, the witness who lectures) stalls scenes and flattens people into
  delivery systems. Fixes:
  - give the explainer a stake in being believed;
  - let them be partly wrong or self-serving;
  - let the listener resist, mock or interrupt;
  - break the explanation with action or danger;
  - deliver some of it through discovery instead of talk.
- **Distribute it.** Don't park the backstory in one speech; spread it across scenes as small contested pieces
  (information-design.md §7–8).

## 7. Testimony, interrogation and confession
- **Every witness has an agenda:** to look good, to protect someone, to settle a score, to be rid of guilt, to be paid.
  Their account bends towards it (mystery-and-clues.md §10).
- **Give each teller a different voice and a different gap.** What each leaves out is as important as what they say.
- **Contradictions are clues.** Two accounts that don't fit, or a self-betraying detail, are the reader's evidence.
- **Confession is a negotiation.** The confessor wants something (absolution, understanding, a promise) and the
  listener's response shapes what comes next. Stage the listener's resistance (scene-and-summary.md §6).

## 8. Group scenes
- **Not everyone speaks equally.** Decide who dominates, who is ignored, who mutters, who stays silent and is watched.
- **Side conversations and glances** reveal alliances and give the reader several things to track.
- **Differentiate fast.** In a large cast, establish each person through one vivid speech habit and one want in the
  first group scene, then reinforce it.
- **Keep the staging clear:** who is where, who moves, who leaves. Each entrance or exit changes the dynamic (McKee's
  "French scenes").

## 9. Period, dialect and translated speech
- **Suggest dialect through vocabulary, idiom and syntax,** not phonetic spelling. Heavy phonetic rendering slows reading
  and can caricature.
- **Period speech:** use the era's idioms and forms of address lightly, and avoid modern slang and modern psychological
  vocabulary in historical characters' mouths.
- **Speech "in another language":** when characters are understood to speak a language other than the text's, render
  it as natural speech in the text's language. Don't calque foreign idioms word by word unless the strangeness itself
  is the point (prose.md §10).

## 10. Narrator voice versus character voices
- **The narrator's register shouldn't leak into everyone's mouth.** A polished, aphoristic narrator makes every
  character sound like a polished aphorist. Let characters be plainer, cruder, funnier, more confused or more formal
  than the narrator.
- **In first person, the narrator's voice dominates everything.** Make the other characters' voices strong enough to be
  heard through the narrator's reporting: their exact phrases, their habits, what the narrator finds odd about how
  they talk.

## 11. Failure patterns
| Symptom | Cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone sounds the same | voices not built; the narrator's register leaks | §2 dossier and voice test; §10 |
| "As you know, Bob" | exposition aimed at the reader | make it ammunition, or cut it |
| Lecture scenes | the explainer problem | §6: stake, resistance, error, interruption |
| On-the-nose dialogue | characters state feelings and themes | move it into subtext; give them a surface task |
| Ping-pong Q&A | one person interrogates, the other informs | give both wants; let answers dodge |
| Quips everywhere | every character shares the author's wit | ration wit to the characters who'd have it |
| Unclear speakers | missing tags in long exchanges | tag every three or four lines; use action beats |

## 12. Checklist
- [ ] Does each major character have a voice built from diction, rhythm, what they notice and how they dodge?
- [ ] Voice test passed (speakers recognizable without tags)?
- [ ] Does every exchange have opposed wants and changing tactics?
- [ ] Is exposition used as ammunition, distributed, and contested?
- [ ] Do witnesses and confessors have agendas, gaps and different voices?
- [ ] Is the narrator's register kept out of the characters' mouths?
- [ ] Is dialect suggested through idiom and syntax rather than spelling?
