# Revision: testing a draft and making it live

Revision means seeing the story again, not tidying its sentences. Work from the largest unit to the smallest, measure
before changing anything, and fix causes rather than symptoms. A sentence can't repair a scope problem; polish laid over
a missing scene only makes the machinery shinier.

## 1. Ground rules
- **Big to small.** The order is scope → spine → information → scenes → people → world → ending → prose → continuity →
  clarity.
  A later pass can't rescue an earlier failure.
- **Measure, then judge.** Counts and tables expose habits the eye skips:
  - the scene/summary mix;
  - reported versus staged events;
  - comparisons and number words per 1,000 words;
  - the dialogue share;
  - the point where a reader could first infer each secret.
- **Protect what works.** List the strengths before cutting. Each repair keeps them or replaces them with something stronger.
- **Treat symptoms as clues.** Too many similes often means a summary is being decorated because no scene exists. A tidy
  ending often means the consequences were never staged. Repair the cause.
- **Keep the intent and change the means.** The author's core idea, emotional destination and voice are the brief, not
  the problem.
- **Ask one question per pass.** Reading for everything at once finds nothing.

## 2. Investigating feedback against the manuscript
Treat criticism, including your own, as a hypothesis about the text, then test it.
1. **Split it into claims.** Turn the critique into separate, testable statements, one problem each.
2. **Find the evidence.** Locate the passages, quote them briefly, and count where counting settles the question, e.g.:
   - words of summary versus scene;
   - events reported versus staged;
   - comparisons per 1,000 words (prose.md §8);
   - the section after which a sharp reader could guess the secret (§6).
3. **Give a verdict:** confirmed, partly confirmed, not confirmed, or unclear (say what would settle it). Record what the
   critic missed or got wrong, especially strengths to protect.
4. **Find the cause one level down.** It may lie in:
   - scope;
   - the information plan;
   - character design;
   - scene selection;
   - world-building;
   - a prose habit;
   - the process, e.g. a checklist followed too literally, or several good techniques stacked until the pattern shows.
5. **Prescribe at the level of the cause.** Make concrete changes that use the author's own material, ranked by impact.
6. **Check the reading conditions.** Did the critic read a translation, an excerpt or a different edition? What genre
   expectations did they bring? A translation can sound translated when the original doesn't, though the original may
   have the same habit (prose.md §10).

Record it as a table: claim | evidence | verdict | cause | fix. Template: `assets/templates/doctor-report.md`.

## 3. The passes at a glance
| Pass | The question | Tool | Typical repairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Scope | Does the material fit the length and form? | `scope-budget` template | narrow the time window; cut an arc; or move to a longer form |
| 2 Spine | Does each event cause the next, and does someone choose? | `causal-spine` template | relink; add choices; stage consequences |
| 3 Information | When could a sharp reader guess each secret? Does each reveal re-see or merely confirm? | inference timeline (`information-plan`) | remove leaks; add a rival explanation; switch the secret to deliberate irony |
| 4 Scene/summary | Are decisions, reversals, confrontations and consequences on stage? | staging list | turn reported events into scenes; anchor summary in instances |
| 5 People | Does everyone have a life, an agenda and a voice? Is there friction? | `character-dossier`, voice test | competing wants; self-interest in good people; scenes for the secret-keeper |
| 6 World | Would the barriers hold? Is the society concrete? | channel audit | close channels; show enforcement and how people live with it |
| 7 Ending | Is the settlement proportionate? Who keeps paying? | cost ledger, tidiness tally | leave residue; partial reconciliation; a consolation that costs |
| 8 Prose | Where does the machinery show? | motif inventory, tic counts | cut echoes; break symmetries; vary syntax; specify |
| 9 Continuity | Do dates, ages, counts, names and knowledge agree? | master timeline | fix |
| 10 Clarity | Can a fresh reader follow who, where, when and the link to the previous beat at every paragraph, while the secret stays hidden? | cold read (`comprehension-log`) | orient scenes; re-anchor referents; state hinges; give background at first need |

## 4. Pass 1: scope and shape
**Symptoms:**
- summary carries the story;
- decades pass in a paragraph;
- many characters get a line each;
- the climax is explained rather than played;
- it reads like an excellent outline.

**Measure:**
- story time span;
- number of viewpoint characters;
- characters who need an arc;
- major reveals;
- settings and world rules that need establishing.

Set these against the word budget using premise.md §7 and long-form.md §2. Then list the scenes the story cannot do
without: each decisive choice, reversal, confrontation, recognition and consequence. Estimate the words each one needs
to live (a full dramatic scene in a novel usually runs to thousands of words, not hundreds).

**Repairs.** Choose one direction; don't split the difference:
- **Narrow** the window to one night, one day or one journey, with fewer arcs. The past enters through what people say,
  withhold, find and do. This suits short stories.
- **Expand** to the form the material needs, a novella or novel, and stage the long span as chosen moments
  (scene-and-summary.md §5).
- **Re-time:** use a frame with an embedded past, braided timelines, or a retrospective narrator who selects scenes
  (viewpoint-and-narration.md; long-form.md §4).

**Check:** can you list the scenes, and is every major choice and consequence among them?

## 5. Pass 2: spine, agency, consequence
- Rebuild the beat list with therefore/but links (diagnostics.md §2). Mark who chooses at each turn.
- **Consequence check:** for every major event, find the scene where someone *acts differently because of it*. An event
  with no later behaviour attached to it is decoration.
- **Reported climaxes:** if the most dramatic events reach the reader only as someone's account, ask whether the telling
  itself can be the drama. It can if the teller wants something from the listener, the telling costs them, and the
  listener resists. Otherwise stage the event.

## 6. Pass 3: information and inference (the leak audit)
**Leak audit:**
1. List each secret and open question.
2. Read the draft section by section as a sharp, genre-literate first reader would, section by section. After each one, write down what a sharp, genre-literate reader would now
   guess and how sure they would be (low, medium or high), using only the pages so far.
3. Mark the **inference point**: the first section where the true answer becomes the reader's best guess.
4. Compare it with the reveal point. If inference comes well before the reveal, the reveal will merely confirm. Choose
   one fix:
   - **Restore the mystery:** soften or cut the leaks and keep a rival explanation alive with real support in the text
     (information-design.md §10).
   - **Convert to irony on purpose:** reveal earlier and spend the middle on scenes in which characters act on the false
     belief, so dread and compassion replace curiosity (information-design.md §5).
   - **Move the payload:** let the reveal's force come from what the fact *means* or *forces someone to decide*, not from
     the fact itself, which may then be guessable.
5. Check the reveal type. **Recontextualizing** reveals re-see earlier scenes. **Confirming** reveals close a guess.
   Mystery-led designs need the first kind.

**Common leak sources:**
- a second voice (an interlude, prologue or document) that knows more and hints;
- details that make sense only if the secret is true;
- emphatic repetition;
- symmetries that point, such as two matching scars or matched counts;
- "there was one thing she refused to think about" lines;
- jacket copy or chapter titles.

## 7. Pass 4: scene and summary
**Staging list.** Write down every event that is:
- a decision;
- a reversal;
- a confrontation, confession or refusal;
- a first or last time;
- a revelation's consequence.

Mark each as staged, reported or summarized. By default, stage them all. Summarize only for a reason:
- repetition (the hundredth night);
- transition;
- background whose meaning is already clear;
- a deliberate omission that the story then makes the reader feel elsewhere.

**Ways to turn summary into life** (scene-and-summary.md §4–6):
- **An iterative summary plus one singular instance:** "Every Friday she brought him the newspaper", then one Friday staged,
  with friction.
- **Report to scene:** move the event on stage, or stage the *telling* as a scene with its own conflict.
- **A sequence of moments** that each change something, rather than a montage of the same mood.
- **Anchoring a long span** to a present-tense action line, so the years are summoned under pressure.

**Check:** the share of scene rises where the story turns.

## 8. Pass 5: people
- **Life beyond the plot.** For each major character, list three things they care about that the plot doesn't need:
  work, an appetite, an irritation, a joke, a hobby, a person. Show at least one in action. (character.md §8)
- **Friction.** Allies disagree. Good people want something for themselves. Kindness costs, and is sometimes resented. A
  saintly character usually means the author has given them only the plot's job.
- **Secret-keepers** get their own side:
  - a scene where the secret presses on them;
  - their rationalization;
  - their cost;
  - a moment when they nearly tell or decide not to.
  (character.md §10)
- **Voice test.** Cover the speaker tags. If you can't tell who is talking, rebuild each voice from diction, rhythm, what
  they notice and how they dodge (voice-and-dialogue.md §2).
- **Surprising but true.** Each major character gets at least one unexpected action that follows from an established
  contradiction (character.md §11).

## 9. Pass 6: world and plausibility
- **Channel audit.** List every way the problem could be solved trivially:
  - talk;
  - write;
  - send word through a third party, institution or relative abroad;
  - leave;
  - pay someone;
  - go to the authorities;
  - check a record.
  Close each with a concrete reason the story shows or implies (world-and-society.md §4).
- **Enforcement and texture.** Show a rule being enforced on someone. Show how ordinary people live alongside it:
  workarounds, black markets, informers, jokes, fatigue.
- **Specificity.** Name places, institutions, prices and procedures, unless abstraction is a deliberate choice. If it is
  (fable, allegory), keep it consistent and make up for it with physical and social specificity
  (world-and-society.md §6).

## 10. Pass 7: emotion, ending, proportion
- **Cost ledger.** What did each character lose? What stays unresolved? Who keeps paying after the last page?
- **Proportion test.** Do the size and speed of the emotional settlement match the size of the loss or deception?
  Forgiving a decades-long deception in one afternoon needs a cost, partialness or time (endings.md §10).
- **Tidiness tally.** In the last tenth, count:
  - threads closed;
  - relationships reconciled;
  - motifs returned;
  - consolations granted.
  If everything closes, reopen the thing that would honestly stay open.
- **Consequence scenes.** After the main revelation, at least one scene should show a choice made because of it,
  preferably one that costs the chooser.

## 11. Pass 8: prose and visible machinery
- **Motif inventory.** List every object, phrase, gesture and number that recurs. Mark as load-bearing only those whose
  return changes meaning. Cut, vary or let fall the rest, and let most details be merely real (prose.md §6).
- **Symmetry check.** List mirrored events, such as matched counts, the same line in a second mouth, or the same action
  on two sides. Keep the one that matters and break the others by changing the number, the timing or who acts.
- **Tic counts per 1,000 words.** Count:
  - comparison markers ("like a", "as if", "as though", "the way you…");
  - number words;
  - one-line aphoristic paragraphs;
  - anaphora.
  Compare with the benchmarks in prose.md §8, and look harder at any single construction that repeats.
- **Read aloud.** Vary sentence length by function. Keep heightened language for rare peaks.
- **Register.** Decide where the prose is plain and where it rises, and why (prose.md §2).

## 12. Pass 9: continuity
Check against a master timeline:
- dates, ages and durations;
- counts (days, nights, steps);
- names and titles;
- geography and travel times;
- who holds which object when;
- what each character could know at each moment.

## 13. Pass 10: clarity (the cold read)
Run this after the text is stable, and again after translating it.
1. **Write the intended-uncertainty list:** what the reader must not yet know, may suspect, should infer and must have
   clear, stage by stage (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §2).
2. **Do the cold read and the repairs** in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §12 (template `comprehension-log`). For a
   translation, add `translation.md` §8.

## 14. Restructure or revise?
**Restructure** (reconceive and redraft) when:
- the scope and the length don't match;
- the protagonist is wrong;
- a mystery's inference point falls before the midpoint;
- the central relationship is never staged;
- the climax depends on events the story only reports.

**Revise** (keep the design) when the spine works but:
- scenes are thin;
- voices blur;
- leaks are local;
- the prose has tics.

Before a restructure, list what must survive (lines, images, the core idea) and carry it across.

## 15. Reporting a revision
Lead with what works. Then give:
- the core problem in one sentence, with evidence;
- the ranked issues, each with its evidence, cause and fix;
- the revised spine;
- what was deliberately left alone.

When responding to outside feedback, include the claim-by-claim verdict table (§2). Template:
`assets/templates/doctor-report.md`. Failure catalogue: `diagnostics.md`.
