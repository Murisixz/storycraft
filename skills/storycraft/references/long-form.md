# Long form: novellas and novels

A short story can live on one situation, one turn and a few staged moments. A novel has to keep a reader for
eighty to a hundred and sixty thousand words. That means it needs several lines of interest, a way of carrying years,
a way of handing on its questions as old ones are answered, and a middle that does not sag into repetition. This file
covers the architecture. The examples come from novels read in full for this skill (novel-studies.md); they show
contrasting solutions, not a formula.

## 1. What changes at length
- **More lines, not longer lines.** A novel braids a main line with secondary lives, a hidden past, a relationship
  plot and a social world. Each needs its own want and its own turns. Stretching one line to novel length produces
  repetition.
- **Questions must be replaced.** A short story can hold one question to the end. A novel answers questions along the
  way and must open new ones before the old ones close (§7).
- **Time must be carried.** Novels cover days to decades. The question is not whether to summarize but how to make
  summary live (scene-and-summary.md §4–5).
- **People need lives.** At length, a character who exists only for the plot becomes obvious. Minor characters need a
  habit and a want; major ones need a life the plot interrupts (character.md §8).
- **The reader remembers.** Plants, contradictions and continuity errors that pass in a short story get noticed across
  three hundred pages (§10).

## 2. Budgets: words, scenes and scope
Set a budget before drafting and check it after. Use premise.md §7 to test whether the material fits the length, and
the scope-budget template to allocate it.

**Typical proportions in the studied novels** (measured on the texts read for this skill; chapter counts from the
editions used):

| Novel | Words | Chapters | Median chapter | Story time of the main line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *And Then There Were None* | ~56,000 | 16 + epilogue + document | ~2,900 | four days |
| *Never Let Me Go* | ~97,000 | 23 in three parts | ~4,100 | age five to thirty-one, told from thirty-one |
| *The Lovely Bones* | ~99,000 | 23 + two titled | ~4,100 | eight and a half years, plus an epilogue |
| *Atonement* | ~124,000 | 14 + two undivided parts + coda | ~4,700 (Part One) | one day; three days; weeks; one day 59 years on |
| *The Da Vinci Code* | ~139,000 | 105 + prologue, epilogue | ~1,200 | about eleven hours, plus a coda |
| *Rebecca* | ~154,000 | 27 | ~6,000 | about four months, plus an undated exile |
| *The Shadow of the Wind* (in translation) | ~163,000 | about 60 in dated parts | ~2,100 | eleven years, plus a past of fifty |

Read the table for ranges, not targets:
- **Chapter length is a tempo choice.** Units of about 1,000 words cross-cut fast. Units of 4,000–6,000 words let a
  social scene or a remembered episode develop in full.
- **Story time does not predict length.** The longest book here covers four months. A thriller of eleven hours runs to
  139,000 words because it stages almost everything at near real time. A book of eight years stays under 100,000
  because it summarizes most years and stages only chosen moments.
- **Scene share.** Most of these novels run roughly 45–65% scene overall. The share rises at crises (Rebecca's
  inquest and tribunal chapters reach about 85%) and falls in embedded histories and memoirs (25–50%). The book that
  stages least (*The Da Vinci Code*'s lecture chapters, about 25%) is where readers report the life draining out. That
  failure comes from exposition, not from summary (§9).

**Budget procedure:**
1. List the must-stage moments (scene-and-summary.md §2) for each line.
2. Give each a word estimate. Turning points and recognitions get the most.
3. Add the connective summary each span needs, and the aftermath each major event needs (emotion.md).
4. Compare the total with the target length. If it's far over, cut a line or narrow the span. If it's far under, the
   material may be a novella, or the lines need lives.

## 3. Movements and parts
A long book is read in movements, whether or not the author labels them. Decide what defines each movement:
- **Time.** Dated parts move through a life or an era (*The Shadow of the Wind*'s parts headed by years; *The Lovely Bones*' first winter, first year, the years, the return).
- **Place.** Each movement is a new arena with new institutions against the hero (*The Da Vinci Code*'s museum, bank, château,
  England). A new arena refreshes stakes and texture.
- **Viewpoint or form.** Each part changes whose eyes or what kind of account we're in (*Atonement*'s rotating country-house day, then one soldier's retreat, then one nurse's London, then a first-person coda).
- **A returning frame.** Each part opens in the narrator's present and then goes back, so the reader is reminded where
  the past is heading (*Never Let Me Go*: each part begins with the carer at thirty-one, driving).
- **Knowledge.** Movements are defined by what the reader knows: before and after the central reversal (*Rebecca* turns at 70%, when the love story becomes a murder story).

**Unequal blocks are normal and useful.** One day can take half a book, and fifty-nine years a few pages. The
proportion tells the reader what matters. Staging one day in full and summarizing the years that follow says that the
day decided the years.

**Mark the turns.** Note where the big reversals fall. In the studied novels the central reversal sits anywhere from
the first page (the crime given away at once) to 41%, 53% and 70%. Each placement makes a different book (§7).

## 4. Re-timing: frames, rewinds, braids and embedded accounts
Novels rarely tell events in plain chronological order from start to end. These structures change *when* the
reader learns things, which changes suspense, irony and grief.

| Structure | How it works | What it buys | What it costs | Examples studied |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrospective frame that leaks the outcome** | the narrator tells from after the end and says what was lost, not how | dread and elegy; suspense moves from whether to how | the reader can't fear the leaked outcome, so the frame must leak only part | *Rebecca*'s first chapter says the house "was no more"; the rest shows how |
| **Frame that is itself a reveal** | the account turns out to be written by a character for a reason | recontextualizes everything before; moral weight | must be planted many times and deniably; risks feeling like a trick | *Atonement*'s coda |
| **Rewinding chapters** | consecutive chapters re-see the same hours from different minds | dramatic irony; misunderstanding becomes inevitable | repetition; the reader must want to see it again | *Atonement*'s country-house day, re-seen from four or five viewpoints |
| **Parallel parts** | two parts cover the same weeks in different places | the reader holds both while each character holds one | the second part must not simply wait | *Atonement*'s Dunkirk and London hospital, May–June 1940 |
| **Braided past and present** | the present line investigates a past that arrives in pieces | curiosity sustained across decades; mirrored generations | the past arrives through convenient witnesses; mirrors can feel schematic | *The Shadow of the Wind*'s investigation of a fifty-year past |
| **Embedded memoir or document** | a long account in another voice delivers the hidden story | a second voice; the keeper's own side | removes the hero from the action; the writer may "know" too much | the memoir in *The Shadow of the Wind* (75–94%); the closing manuscript in *And Then There Were None* |
| **Frame converging on the present** | a retrospective narrator tells forward until the story reaches the moment of telling | the ending is spoken from inside the narrator's present; grief has a now | needs a narrating present with its own pressure | *Never Let Me Go* closes "a few days ago" |
| **Published shape** | the plot's pattern is announced early (a rhyme, a count, a prophecy) | the reader watches for fit and method; a visible scoreboard | each step's method can become predictable | the rhyme and ten figures in *And Then There Were None* |
| **Dated vignettes** | a run of short dated sections, each with one object and one turn | carries years without draining life | becomes status updates if a vignette has no turn | "Snapshots" in *The Lovely Bones* |

**Choosing.** Ask what the reader should know first and what should be held:
- Lead with the outcome when the subject is how people live with it.
- Lead with the misreading when the subject is how people come to believe it.
- Embed the past when the subject is what the past did to the present.

**Close every frame with consequence.** The telling should change the teller or the listener
(viewpoint-and-narration.md §7).

**Braids that converge** (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*, which braids 1921, the present and another world):
- **Give the reader facts that fit one person before any character can connect the strands.** A mad old man with a dead
  wolf, an axe and a protective verse is plainly the 1921 narrator, and the reader knows it about 25 pages before the
  visions confirm it. The dramatic irony costs nothing.
- **Let a place or a person appear in two times before anyone names them:** a ruined round building with animal hair
  in its walls, which only the reader recognizes as the ice house built twenty years earlier in the other strand.
- **Let a causal link cross the strands unstated.** The hero's grave robbery in one world brings back the moon that
  saves a stranger at a gallows in another, and only the reader sees the connection.

## 5. Viewpoint at length
- **One narrator for the whole book** gives intimacy and a consistent filter. It needs strong other voices heard
  through the narrator, and an occasion for the telling (viewpoint-and-narration.md §2). It limits what the reader can
  know. *Rebecca* turns this limit into its engine: the reader shares the narrator's misreading for 70% of
  the book.
- **Rotating close third** buys dramatic irony and breadth. At about fifteen viewpoints (*The Da Vinci Code*), each minor
  viewpoint must earn its chapter with a petty stake of its own (a manager's lucrative client, an altar boy's fear of
  his priest), or it becomes a camera.
- **A near-omniscient first person** (a dead narrator who can enter every mind) gives the family's whole grief, but it
  dissolves first-person limits. The cost shows when the narrator glosses what the scene already showed.
- **Testimony that becomes omniscient** without acknowledgement is a recurring weakness: witnesses narrate thoughts they
  could not know. Either acknowledge the reconstruction ("this is what I imagine") or keep testimony within what the
  witness saw (viewpoint-and-narration.md §9).
- **A viewpoint who cannot know** can withhold a crisis honestly, as characterization rather than a trick. In *Atonement*, the migrainous mother lies indoors in a sound-proofed house during the search.

## 6. Momentum and its costs
Momentum is what makes a reader start the next chapter. Each device has a price.

| Device | Effect | Cost when overused |
|---|---|---|
| **Short cross-cut chapters** | speed; several threats at once | thin characters; readers stop caring who is in which chapter |
| **Chapter-end withholding** (a character sees something; the narration cuts away) | strong hook | visibly mechanical by the tenth use; readers feel managed |
| **Explicit clocks** ("fifteen minutes from now") | urgency | implausible time budgets; every clock must be honoured |
| **Countdowns from the narrator** ("In seven days' time, I would be dead.") | dread in place of curiosity | must be paid literally, or it's a cheat |
| **Puzzle chains with stated rules** | the reader judges fairness | a nested lock without new rules feels like padding |
| **Threats converging on a static scene** | carries exposition | too sparse a cut-away can't cover a long lecture |
| **A visible count** (figures removed, days left, donations numbered) | a clock the reader can see | must keep changing what each step costs |
| **Leaked outcome** | grief and irony throughout | needs a strong "how" to replace "whether" |

**Rules:**
- **Switch at the moment of maximum question,** not in the middle of a feeling (scene-and-summary.md §8).
- **Vary the chapter-end move.** Rotate the devices: a turn, a decision pending, a quiet image, a comic button, a
  withheld fact. Using one move ten times reveals the machine.
- **Momentum isn't the only virtue.** The slower books studied here hold readers with social pressure, irony and
  people. Their momentum comes from accumulating dread, not cut-aways. Choose the engine that fits the book.

## 7. Handing on the question
A long book answers questions. The skill is opening the next one before closing the last.

- **Hand off in the same breath.** When *The Da Vinci Code* reveals its central secret at about 53%, it opens three new
  questions in the same chapters: who the hidden enemy is, where the object is, and what the heroine's family truth
  is. Each gets a different information strategy (information-design.md §10).
- **Give away the event, keep the meaning.** Three of the studied books give away a terrible event early:
  - *The Lovely Bones* gives away the murder on page one;
  - *Atonement* announces the false accusation at 41%;
  - *Rebecca* leaks the house's loss in its first chapter.
  Each moves suspense from *whether* to *how*, *who will know* and *what it will cost*.
- **Concede the guess, move the question.** When readers will guess the secret, confirm it early and shift curiosity
  to why and to whether hope is real. *Never Let Me Go* states its central fact at 28% ("We already knew all that")
  and replaces it with the hope of a deferral, which it refutes at 89%.
- **Publish the shape, withhold the agent.** A closed-circle mystery can announce how its victims will die and keep
  only who. Suspense moves to fit and method (*And Then There Were None*).
- **Keep alternative explanations alive.** *The Shadow of the Wind* gives each threat two or three plausible authors, so facts
  can leak without the solution leaking.
- **Plan the replacement chain.** For each major question, note where it opens, where it closes, and which question
  takes its place. If a question closes and nothing replaces it, the book stalls. In the studied novels this happens
  after the killer leaves town and when an aftermath runs on sentiment.

## 8. The middle
Every novel studied here has at least one sag the readers' notes flag:
- *Rebecca*'s middle repeats the narrator's insecurity in new social settings;
- *Atonement*: the mother's chapters and the hospital-regime summary;
- *The Da Vinci Code*: the lecture block;
- *The Shadow of the Wind*: a run of embedded Gothic histories;
- *The Lovely Bones*: the stretch after the killer leaves.

**Repairs observed:**
- **Escalate by stripping supports.** Take away the protagonist's allies one at a time (the father in *The Lovely Bones*' first act loses the police, the detective's boundary, his wife and his job before the cornfield).
- **Change the kind of pressure at the midpoint.** Social and psychological pressure in the first half can become plot
  pressure in the second. In *Rebecca*, the threat-and-reprieve swings come every few pages after the
  reversal.
- **Carry key plants inside the repetitive stretch,** so even a sag sets up the climax.
- **Cut the sequence of like episodes.** Three consecutive embedded histories stall a plot. Keep the one with stakes
  and turn the others into a sentence each.
- **Let a secondary life take over** for a stretch, with its own turn, while the main line rests.

## 9. Exposition at length: testimony, documents, lectures and memoirs
Novels carry far more backstory than short forms, so the delivery systems matter.
- **Testimony chains.** Each witness opens one new door and pays a price for speaking: a confidence, a fear, a beating.
  Give witnesses physical business (three cigarettes counted, eight sugars in a coffee) and an agenda
  (voice-and-dialogue.md §7).
- **Framing instead of withholding.** Put the hard facts on the page early, each told by someone with another purpose,
  and let a biased viewpoint misread them. By 43% of *Rebecca*, every incriminating fact is on the page,
  inside a grieving servant's tour.
- **Lectures** stall unless danger converges on the place where they are delivered, or the listener resists. Avoid
  the seminar of a naive learner and an expert who is always right.
- **Memoirs and documents** let the hidden story arrive in another voice and give the keeper a side. Keep the reader's
  surrogate reacting in the frame before and after, let the document address someone, and keep the writer's knowledge
  within what they could know.
- **The late explanation.** If a long explanation is unavoidable, break it with domestic interruptions and give it
  a hostile second voice. Then put the emotional consequence in a separate, later scene, as *Never Let Me Go* does with
  doorbells, a heckling listener, and a rage in a field afterwards. Avoid an explainer who exists only to explain.
- **Characters fail, then professionals fail, then the explanation.** *And Then There Were None* lets everyone die and
  the police give up before the killer's document. Each failure makes the explanation welcome, and the confession has
  a motive of its own (vanity) that condemns its speaker.
- **The confession.** Distributed in pieces with recaps ("it all fits now"), it shows machinery. Keep recaps short and
  let the listener resist (scene-and-summary.md §6).

## 10. Continuity and ledgers
Across the studied books, the readers found slips a careful reviser would catch:
- a husband's age given two ways;
- a letter postmarked after its writer's death;
- a rejection that arrives in two different months;
- a day-count that runs into the next year;
- an injury that moves from arm to face.
At novel length, keep:
- **a master timeline** with dates, ages and durations;
- **a setup ledger:** every plant, its first appearance, its reminders and its payoff or deliberate abandonment (the
  clue-ledger template works for any genre);
- **an information ledger:** who knows what, when, and what the reader knows (information-design.md §9);
- **a cast sheet** with names, spellings, titles and relationships.
Run revision.md §12 (continuity) against them before calling a draft finished.

## 11. Failure patterns
| Symptom | Cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| The middle repeats | one line stretched; the same ordeal in new settings | add a line; change the kind of pressure; cut like episodes (§8) |
| The book stalls after a reveal | no replacement question | hand off in the same breath (§7) |
| Years pass and nothing lives | summary without objects or turns | dated vignettes, sensory memory inside a present crisis, iterative summary with particulars (scene-and-summary.md §5) |
| Hooks feel manufactured | one chapter-end device used too often | rotate the moves (§6) |
| The hero vanishes for a fifth of the book | a document carries the solution | keep the hero reacting in the frame; break the document; shorten it (§9) |
| Witnesses know too much | testimony slides into omniscience | acknowledge reconstruction or limit the witness (§5) |
| The parallel is obvious | mirrored generations stated repeatedly | state it once, through a character, and stop (novel-studies.md) |
| The ending summary lists fates | the coda is a checklist | give two or three fates a scene or an image; let others go |
| Continuity slips | no ledgers | §10 |

## 12. Checklist
- [ ] Does the material fit the length (premise.md §7)? Is there a word and scene budget?
- [ ] Are there several lines, each with its own want and turns?
- [ ] Are the movements defined (by time, place, viewpoint or knowledge), and do the big reversals fall where they
  make the intended book?
- [ ] Does the chosen re-timing structure serve the subject (outcome first, misreading first, or past embedded)?
- [ ] Is every answered question replaced by another before the pace drops?
- [ ] Are the middle's sags repaired, by stripping supports, changing pressure, or cutting like episodes?
- [ ] Do testimony and documents cost their speakers something, and stay within what the speaker could know?
- [ ] Are chapter-end devices varied?
- [ ] Are the timeline, setup, information and cast ledgers kept and checked?
