# Arabic writing: the Arabic language layer

**This is a language layer, not a separate storytelling system.** A story written in Arabic is designed and drafted with the same shared craft as any other story. That means the SKILL.md workflows and whichever references the story needs:
- premise, structure and scenes;
- character, dialogue and viewpoint;
- information design, mysteries and horror;
- emotion, endings, long form and series;
- prose and clarity.

All of it applies in Arabic, including every lesson drawn from the English-language novels. This file adds what changes when the story is *expressed* in Arabic: narrative voice and register, reference mechanics, dialogue layout, sentence construction, orientation devices, names and vowelling, idiom and formulae, and punctuation. For an Arabic edition of a story written in another language, `translation.md` adds the transfer procedure on top of this file. For narration that is heard rather than read (a documentary voiceover, a narrated video or podcast), §12 adds what changes for the ear, on top of `documentary.md`.

The evidence comes from twelve Arabic novels read in full (`sources.md` records editions and gaps):
- eight popular novels: the *Khawf* trilogy, *Arsis* 1–2, *Ard Zikola*, *Amarita* and *Wadi al-Dhi'ab al-Mansiyya*;
- four literary novels added in v4: Ghassan Kanafani's *Rijal fi al-Shams* (*Men in the Sun*), Jokha Alharthi's *Sayyidat al-Qamar* (*Celestial Bodies*), and Naguib Mahfouz's *Bayn al-Qasrayn* (*Palace Walk*) and *Qasr al-Shawq* (*Palace of Desire*).

Their craft lessons are filed by function in the shared references and compared in `novel-studies.md`. Every one of them has real weaknesses as well as strengths. Use them for methods, never as models to imitate, and never reuse their phrases, names or plots.

## 1. How this layer works with the shared craft
- **Load the craft references the story needs first,** exactly as for a story in English, then this file. Never use this file alone: it says how to *say* the story, not how to *build* it.
- **The clarity method is shared** (`clarity-and-orientation.md`): the situation model, the intended-uncertainty list, hinges, background at first need, the cold read. Arabic needs it more than English, because Arabic lacks capital letters and italics and resolves pronouns more strictly. A reader can understand every word and still lose the situation.
- **Clarity is not simplicity.** The novels studied use ordinary literary vocabulary and keep readers oriented through structure:
  - a time and place in the first sentence or two of a scene;
  - names or labels on every speaker;
  - the decisive fact legible at each hinge, through a plain line or an act, ritual or reaction this readership can read (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §6).

  Don't simplify vocabulary, explain subtext or vowel everything. Fix orientation, reference and links (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §3–5).

## 2. Narrative voice and register
**Choose the narrative voice for this story** (the general options and their costs are in `viewpoint-and-narration.md`). The novels studied show three voices that suit their stories:
- **A retrospective first-person memoir** with essayistic asides, for occult testimony that may or may not be believed (*Khawf*);
- **an external narrator close to one man,** with clock headers, for a thriller built on dramatic irony (*Arsis*);
- **a close third person** following a village hero whose colloquial voice is set against a formal other world, for a portal fantasy (*Ard Zikola*).

The version 4 novels add three more:
- a close third person rotating among three men, then their driver, for a journey toward death (*Men in the Sun*);
- a roaming third person that moves through a family's minds, with an analytic narrator (*Palace Walk*, *Palace of Desire*);
- a husband's first-person chapters alternating with third-person family chapters (*Celestial Bodies*).

Decide the narrator's person, distance and register height together, and keep them.

**Narration** in all twelve novels is standard Arabic (فصحى), at different heights:
- plain and conversational;
- plain modern with an authorial voice and aphorisms;
- simple, fast, popular;
- ornate and analytic, with long periodic sentences (*Palace Walk*).

Colloquial words in narration should be rare and deliberate.

**Register can signal gravity.** *Palace Walk* never lets dialect into its narrator's voice, and lets it into speech only through formulae, oaths, songs, panic and comedy. The gravest scenes are the plainest: short فصحى fragments and formal religious diction. A colloquial verb in a composed man's thoughts then marks the loss of composure itself. *Celestial Bodies* keeps its narration in فصحى, fences dialect in guillemets, and footnotes proverbs and garments.

**Forms of address mark the moment as well as the relationship.** A son who says «يا بابا» when told he will marry says «يا أبي» when told of his divorce (*Palace Walk*). In *Men in the Sun* the forms of address encode a hierarchy no one states.

**Dialogue: three workable strategies,** all present in the corpus (`novel-studies.md` §14 compares the books):

| Strategy | Seen in | Gains | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| All dialogue in فصحى, made to sound spoken (short turns, formulae, address forms), with dialect only as single quoted tokens | *Khawf 2*, *Arsis*, *Wadi al-Dhi'ab* (no dialect at all) | pan-Arab readability; one voice level; otherness marked by register height (archaic for jinn, legal for a tempter) | social texture flattens (a healer, a tycoon and a housewife sound alike); comedy must come from situation, not idiom |
| فصحى by default; dialect for one character's identity and for the hero's home social world | *Khawf 1* (a devil and the hero's friends) | register itself attributes the dialect speaker; menace and warmth; uncanny familiarity | family scenes in stiff فصحى; regional slang opaque elsewhere |
| Dialect for the characters from a real locale, فصحى for another world | *Ard Zikola* (Egyptian hero, فصحى-speaking fantasy world) | instant world-marking with no exposition; standard Arabic becomes the "foreign" register; character warmth | readers outside the dialect region work harder; mutual intelligibility between worlds is assumed, not explained |

**Rules** (the general principle of giving a register a job is in `voice-and-dialogue.md` §9):
- **Plan register per character before drafting,** and keep the narrator's grammar out of any dialect speaker's mouth (and the reverse).
- **A dialect token goes in guillemets, attributed** («قالت الجارة إنّ الولد "مش طبيعي"»). One token can place a speaker socially; three paragraphs of dialect can lose readers from other regions.
- **Make فصحى banter sound spoken:**
  - very short turns;
  - idioms and fixed formulae instead of subordinate chains;
  - vocatives;
  - gendered second-person forms doing the attribution (*Arsis*).
- **Mark otherworldly speakers by register:** by height (archaic, laconic فصحى and address by substance, يا ابن آدم, for the jinn of *Khawf 2*), or by an incongruously familiar dialect (*Khawf 1*'s devil).
- **Give dialect a clear job.** In *Khawf 1*, dialect belongs to one devil, whose register attributes his lines, and to the hero's friends, his home world. The devil switches to فصحى when he plays a servant, and his long backstory is retold in فصحى indirect narration, with dialect kept for reactions.
- **A change of register can close a quotation.** When narration is فصحى and speech is dialect, narration may resume inside the same dash paragraph and the reader still knows where the speech ended. When both are فصحى, end the paragraph after the speech, or start the narration with an explicit subject (*Ard Zikola*).
- **A regional voice inside an all-فصحى text:** let one fixed phrase or form of address carry the region («يا ولدي»). Avoid stray words from a third region (a Levantine «البندورة» in a Saudi narrator; *Arsis 2*). Social texture can also be carried by reference (institutions, brands, events) rather than by voice.
- **For a pan-Arab readership,** a reader who skips every dialect line must still be able to follow the plot from the narration and the فصحى lines. A book written for one dialect's readers may carry plot in dialect (*Ard Zikola*).
- **A dialect community written in فصحى** (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*'s 1921 village): date the place and period by objects and trade words; footnote one technical or period term; fence any real colloquial phrase in guillemets and gloss it («بلاد برّة»); mark identity by belief, profession and lexicon rather than accent. The cost: home scenes can read like a translation of speech.
- **Name the code in the tag** when it matters, from where the narration stands: «وقال خالد بلهجته الغريبة ضاحكًا», «فنطقت منى بلهجتنا فجأة» (*Amarita*).
- **Answer a dialect speaker in the listener's own register.** The reply shows the line was understood, with no translation line (*Amarita*). Decide how far the worlds understand each other, and apply it evenly.

## 3. Reference in Arabic
**Why Arabic is stricter.** An attached pronoun (ـه، ـها) or an omitted subject in a verb-initial sentence tends to attach to the most recent, most salient person of matching gender and number: the nearest-person rule (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §4). With several men or several women in play, the nearest one is often the wrong one. This is the main mechanical cause of confusion in Arabic translated from English. The general naming systems for a crowded cast (fixed labels, epithets from acts, labels that follow knowledge, no shared epithets) are in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §4. Below are the Arabic means.
- **Parentheses on names** throughout ((عمار)، (خوف)), as in *Khawf 2*. This separates names from ordinary words (the narrator's alias خوف versus خوف, "fear") and from verbs (أحمد). Use the convention consistently or not at all.
- **Let morphology carry attribution.**
  - Duals can keep a four-person chase clear (*Ard Zikola*).
  - Gendered verbs track a man and a woman with minimal tags (*Khawf 1*).
  - «أنتِ / أنا» can split an inner debate that dashes alone muddle.
  - A teacher addressing a pair, then one of them, uses the dual, then a vowelled feminine suffix (*Khawf 3*).
- **Vowel the second-person suffix** where the addressee's gender matters (عليكِ، أنتِ). One mark removes a whole class of ambiguity (*Arsis*).
- **Two women:** never follow a bare «قالت:» with a line either could say. Name the speaker, or let the line's own morphology fix her.
- **Two women and a man:** open each sentence with a name, or keep the previous sentence's subject, so feminine suffixes point back to the last named woman (*Arsis 2*).
- **A dash apposition** («عنه - عن سامر -») repairs a pronoun whose nearest antecedent is the wrong person. Use it only then; as a reflex it sounds like the narrator talking down.
- **A vocative names a new or disguised character** («يا سيدة …»), so the name lands as an event, not as a label from the narrator (*Arsis*).
- **Lookalike names** (ماجد / هاجر) need re-naming at paragraph starts and distinct epithets or action beats (*Khawf 3*).
- **In reported speech about a third party, repeat the noun at every change of referent.** هو/هي and attached pronouns multiply in indirect speech (*Amarita*'s hardest passage is such a report).
- **Two minds in one body:** the host's name in the vocative fixes who is who («اهرب يا موسى. موسى لا يراه.», *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*; `viewpoint-and-narration.md` §8).
- **Role before name, name when needed.** «المرأة», «الأم», «السيد» can carry a character until a scene needs the name. When a second holder of the same role enters, shift one label, so that the mother of a mother becomes «العجوز» (*Palace Walk*).
- **Labels mark viewpoint, and can mark a death.** The same woman is «امرأة أبيه» from her stepson's side and «امرأة أبيك» in his mother's mouth. A dead woman becomes «المرحومة» (*Palace Walk*). A changed label is a quiet second channel for a fact.
- **Kinship apposition anchors a new name** («عزان زوج سالمة»; *Celestial Bodies*). Where a family realistically reuses given names, add a generation marker or an epithet each time. Recycled names and an unexplained matronymic are that book's main reference failures.
- **Fixed epithets** bring the reader back to the present after a memory (*Men in the Sun*).
- **Arabic forms for tracking the unnamed:**
  - «أحد الثلاثة», «أقرب الحارسين», «الحارس الذي كان يتخذه درعًا» (*Arsis 2*);
  - «أحد الجنود… جنديّ آخر… هو الآخر», with the leader singled out by his gear (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*);
  - an ordinal («الفقير الأول») or a visible attribute («الرجل الجالس» / «الرجل الواقف») instead of a reused «العجوز» or «الرجل».

## 4. Dialogue layout
**Measured across the eight version 3 novels** (OCR counts per 1,000 words, approximate; `novel-studies.md` §17):
- Speaker tags placed **before** the line: about 7–14 per 1,000 words.
- *Khawf 2* and *Khawf 3* use parenthesised speaker labels instead: about 20 per 1,000 words.
- Tags placed **after** the line, English style («…»، قال): close to zero.
- Translations from English often carry English layout into Arabic: tags after the line and heavy guillemet dialogue.

**The conventions:**
1. **A tag line, then the speech on its own dash line:**

   قالت لأخيها:
   - لن ننتظره.

   The tag can be a speech verb with a manner word (فسأله لاهثًا:), or a reaction beat that marks the next speaker («فارتسمت الدهشة على وجه الشاب:», *Ard Zikola*).
2. **Speaker labels** for long exchanges: «(أمي) وهي تقبّل جبيني: …». The narrator takes a bare dash line and never a label. *Khawf 2* keeps every speaker clear through runs of eighty lines and four-speaker family scenes. The cost: manner adverbs in labels end up telling the emotions.
3. **Guillemets «»** for speech embedded in a narration paragraph, quoted thoughts, documents, messages, and quoted words.

**Rules** (attribution frequency, which is the same in every language, is in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §7):
- **Put the addressee in the tag** when it shifts (ثم أكمل محدّثًا صديقه:). Put the volume in the label when someone whispers.
- **Let staging travel in the label's manner slot:** where the speaker stands, a fading image, a seat in a crowded car («(أمي) من وراء الباب:», *Khawf 3*).
- **Unnamed speakers** speak through a narrative verb (رد أحد الحاضرين بتهكم:), never through an unexplained label.
- **A story told inside dialogue needs a frame:** guillemets, or «قال:» with the teller named. Restore the outer scene by re-naming the listener with an action beat.
- **A recurring exchange over weeks** is staged once with the habitual «كان + المضارع» («وكان يجيب… وكنت أقول»).
- **Introduce free direct thought** («وحدّث نفسه:»), or give it one consistent mark. Don't rely on a closing "هكذا حدّث نفسه". Choose one convention for documents and one for thoughts, and never nest the same bracket.
- **Don't split a line around a tag** («غدًا»، قالت. «في الصباح؟»). Use the action as the introduction.
- **Speech inside speech** takes inner quotation marks, closed visibly.
- **A speech that runs over several paragraphs** needs a mark at the start of each continuation: a dash, a vocative, a second-person verb, or a short «وتابع:». An unmarked paragraph reads as narration (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*).
- **Keep gestures and new tags outside the dash line.** Narration folded into a quoted line («- لن نعود من هنا، وأشار إلى الباب، ثم أكمل: …») hides where the speech stops (*Amarita*). End the line, narrate the gesture, then open a new dash line. A short stage direction in parentheses is the exception, because the brackets show where speech stops and resumes («- لست حزينًا (ثم باسمًا) …»; *Palace Walk*).
- **Unspoken thought between spoken lines** can be set as an undashed paragraph between dashed speech (*Palace of Desire*). Tag it when the page may lose its dashes, as a scan or a careless typesetter will.
- **An unspoken reply** can run as unmarked free direct speech after a short spoken submission. The next spoken line proves it stayed silent: a father's «مالك لا تتكلم؟» after a son's long inner tirade (*Palace Walk*). It is strong once or twice in a book, and it weakens as a habit.
- **A chorus of five or six voices stays clear** when every line is tagged and each voice keeps its idiom (*Palace Walk*'s coffee hour). Untagged crowd and whisper scenes work only where the idiom alone fixes the speaker.
- **Other tag forms:** a لـ-clause introduces a reply as a consequence («ليجيبها في غير اكتراث:», *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*); a voice heard from outside the focus can be printed in guillemets first and identified in the next sentence.
- **Choose one convention per book.** *Arsis* mixes dash and no-dash after a colon and loses some clarity. *Celestial Bodies* shifts between conventions, and *Men in the Sun* lets a four-person car scene run untagged and opens a letter with no mark.

## 5. Sentence and paragraph construction
The general rules for sentences at hinges and shocks, and for paragraph hand-offs, are in `prose.md` §5. In Arabic:
- **Events run verb-first; states and portraits can run noun-first.** A construct-adjective portrait is a model of economy ("بشماغٍ أحمر بلا عقال.. لحيته سوداء كثيفة.. مكحّل العينين", *Khawf 2*).
- **Chains with و / ف / ثمّ / لكنّ are native.** Juxtaposed short sentences are available for effect. Long chains with few commas are normal in popular fiction. Mark logic where the reader needs it: لذا، مما دفعني، على الرغم من… فإنّ.
- **Interrupted and immediate actions:** «ما إن… حتى», «ما كاد… حتى», «بينما… إذ». The reader always knows which came first and what caused what.
- **Action climaxes can switch to chains of present-tense verbs** while the background stays in the perfect (*Ard Zikola*'s chases).
- **A shock is one flat, verb-initial sentence,** with no ".." and no exclamation mark (*Khawf 1*).
- **Routine, then the day that broke it.** Tell routine in habitual imperfects and verbless pairs («نهار شاق بطيء. وليل تهرول ساعاته»), then pivot on a fixed hinge into the singular scene: «ثم جاء ذلك اليوم…» (*Amarita*).
- **A cognate accusative clinches an image** at a peak: «شاحبة شحوبًا لم أرَ مثيله في حياتي» (*Amarita*).
- **The لـ-consequence clause** carries a surprise at a turn («لنُفاجأ في اليوم الثامن بما لم يتوقعه أحد», *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). Four in two pages make it a tic, and so does «لم يكن إلا…» for every identification (*Amarita*), or an interrupter formula such as «وما يدري إلا و…» used about twenty-five times in one book (*Palace Walk*).
- **Reasoning and decisions in Arabic forms:** «إذا كان… وإذا كان… فـ…»; «جانبٌ منّي يرى… وجانبٌ أكبر يؤيّد…» (*Wadi al-Dhi'ab*; the general rule is in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §5).
- **A plot fact in a concessive or subordinate clause is easy to miss.** Long periodic sentences built on «على أنّ», «بيد أنّ», «ومع أنّ… إلا أنّ» suit analysis and reverie. A pregnancy, a divorce or a death placed inside one, in a flashback and with no one reacting, slips past (*Palace Walk* has to repeat each one later). When the reader will need the fact, give it its own main clause or a reaction.
- **Fragments at the extremity.** When a death is narrated from inside, the prose can break into short units and questions. The change of rhythm marks an event the words never name, so the fact needs another channel soon after (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §6.2).
- **Summaries of long spans:**
  - use concessive-then-consequence frames («على الرغم من… فإنّ… لذا»; popular fiction often writes «بالرغم من… إلا أنّ»);
  - skip to the next event with «لم… إلا عندما» (*Khawf 3*).

## 6. Orientation devices in Arabic
The general devices (first-sentence anchoring, clocks the characters live by, headers and slug lines, reintroductions, transitions and replays) are in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §3 and `scene-and-summary.md` §8. Their Arabic forms:
- **Prayer times and the calendar** (فجر، عصر، مغرب; Friday; Ramadan; Eid) are native clocks and timers (*Khawf*). Count years by Ramadans per character (*Khawf 3*).
- **«وجد نفسه…»** puts the body and the new place in one clause after a passage, a faint or a dream (*Ard Zikola*).
- **Flashbacks** enter with «كان قد» and a time anchor (وقتها، قبل عام), a framing verb (تذكّرت، عادت بها الذاكرة), or a dated header («قبل تسع سنوات»). Once a memory is framed, continue in the plain past.
- **A slug line ending in a colon** (the place, then the clock) marks a shift inside a chapter (*Arsis 2*). «في الوقت ذاته» marks simultaneity in a cross-cut.
- **State a skip at the skip:** «مرّ شهر…».
- **The pluperfect clock** sets the time before anyone moves: «كان الليل قد انتصف حين طرق أحدهم الباب» (invented; the pattern opens most scenes in the *Zikola* books).
- **Clocks in a long summary:** an age clock («في عامي الرابع عشر…») or a day count («في النهار العشرين من…», *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*).
- **Re-introduce a long-absent character** with a dash parenthesis giving epithet and role («صديقه العجوز - مجنون السرداب -», *Ard Zikola*).
- **Habit and sound before names,** when one consciousness holds the scene: *Palace Walk* opens at midnight on a habit, places the street by its sounds, and names its heroine twelve pages later. In a crowded scene the same opening would confuse.
- **Waking, shock or a threshold decision** can be set as a few short centred lines, each adding one coordinate, resolving within five lines (*Khawf 3*).

## 7. Names, homographs and selective vowelling
- **Names that are ordinary words** (سعيد، أمل، نور، خوف، وله، كريم) need a marker:
  - a consistent parenthesis convention;
  - a role noun at first mention («جارتهم أمل»);
  - or a sentence position where the name can't parse as a word.
- **The accusative trap.** A masculine name that is also an adjective (سعيد، كريم، منصور) in the indefinite accusative reads as the adjective: «رأيتُ سعيدًا» ("I saw a happy man"). A role noun doesn't help there, because «رأيتُ جارَنا سعيدًا» reads "I saw our neighbour, happy" (a ḥāl). Take the name out of the accusative: make it the subject («كان سعيد في العرس، ورآه الجميع»), put it after a preposition («جاؤوا بسعيد»), use parentheses, or restructure. Feminine names like أمل don't take tanween and escape the trap.
- **Homographs near plot words** (زوج husband or pair; الملك king or angel; قُبلة or قِبلة; a verb ambiguous between "you" and "she"): reword, or add the one diacritic that settles the reading.
- **Vowel selectively:**
  - passive forms that could read active (قُتِل);
  - second-person gender (كتبتِ);
  - a feminine past form that could read as first person (سافرَت).

  Full vowelling makes adult prose look like a primer.
- **Use vowelling as a dial.** Leave an ambiguous word bare where the doubt is the point, and vowel it at the moment certainty matters. *Khawf 1* leaves «دينك» (debt or religion) bare when the narrator "could not understand it", and vowels it «دَيْنُك» at the decisive repetition.
- **Foreign words and loans** go in guillemets or parentheses on first use. A footnote can gloss one period or technical term («قادوس», the hopper of a mill; *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). A dialect line or folk idiom inside فصحى narration is fenced in guillemets, so the reader sees the register shift. A dialect speaker is glossed on entry, or the فصحى reply carries the meaning (*Khawf 3*).
- **Unfamiliar terms without stopping the scene.** The v4 novels gloss local and period words in stride, and each method has a different cost (the general method is in `world-and-society.md`):
  - **use in the same sentence,** which defines a thing by its purpose or motion («بلابيع» by what they fatten; a «حنطور» by its approach; *Palace Walk*);
  - **a correction in dialogue** («مسكد» corrected to «مسقط»; *Celestial Bodies*);
  - **a naive question** («وما الفرق؟»; *Men in the Sun*) or a character's ignorance (a wife hears a prince's name for the first time; *Palace Walk*);
  - **apposition or the next clause,** best kept for status («أكبر تاجر قطن»);
  - **an office shown by what it does** (the quarter's sheikh carrying orders door to door);
  - **a rule shown through its exception,** or a purpose clause (*Celestial Bodies*);
  - **a naive description of a device** before its name (a "strange waterskin" wrapped round the arm and pumped with leather bellows, before the word "pressure"; *Palace of Desire*);
  - **quotation marks around another person's word,** which also shows how the speaker regards it («شباب "اليوم"»; *Palace of Desire*);
  - **a delayed or retroactive gloss,** which works when the delay is short (Zeppelin is explained eight pages after the joke; *Palace Walk*);
  - **a footnote** for proverbs and garments, which interrupts least in a book that uses footnotes throughout (*Celestial Bodies*);
  - **nothing,** where context makes the word usable («الفلج», «الزار»).

  These fail when a word the plot turns on is left unexplained: a status word glossed 160 pages late («البيدار»; *Celestial Bodies*). They also fail when a real name carries weight that outsiders cannot know, as with Dinshaway in *Palace Walk*. A character's reaction can carry that weight without a gloss.

## 8. Idiom, formulae and figurative language
The general uses of ritual language and of sentence-level fear are in `emotion.md` §5 and `horror.md` §6. In Arabic:
- **Formulae as social acts:**
  - rebuke («أستغفر الله»);
  - comfort («كان الله في عونها»);
  - deflection («هداكم الله»).

  Invert one only at a turning point. Change a formula's ending to turn a scene: «الحمد لله» after a safe birth becomes «الحمد لله على كل حال» when something has gone wrong (*Palace Walk*).
- **At a death, sort formulae and customs by what they tell the reader.** A ritual can speak without explanation when it tells *this* reader what happened (*Arsis*). The general choice of channel is in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §6.2.
  - **Rituals of dying** mark dying, not death. Prompting the dying to say the shahada (تلقين المحتضر) or a last recitation leaves open when, or whether, death came. The talqin at the grave after burial, where it is practised, belongs to after death.
  - **Acts and words that follow a death** are unmistakable to a reader who knows them: «إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون» (also said at other calamities, so it is clearest beside a noun), «الله يرحمه», «البقاء لله», «المرحوم/المرحومة», «فارق الحياة», closing the eyes, covering the face, the wail heard from outside, condolences, washing and the shroud (*Celestial Bodies*; *Palace Walk*).
  - **A formal announcement** («ننعى إليكم…»; «استشهد») is unmistakable to anyone (*Palace Walk*).
  - **A condolence that is itself the announcement** («العمر الطويل لك») states the fact only to readers who know the formula. *Palace Walk* prepares it with the illness and pairs it with a lowered head.
  - **A mercy formula dropped into other talk** («رحمه الله» inside a smuggler's pitch) is the only sign of a past death in *Men in the Sun*. A reader who skims it as filler misses the death. A formula carries a fact only for a reader who reads it as information.
  - **A heralding formula read by the listener** («ربنا يلهمك الصبر!» answered by «الصبر؟.. علامَ؟») lets the bereaved understand on the page, and the reader with them (*Palace Walk*).
  - **Euphemisms are ambiguous:** «راح», «رحل», «ذهب», «انطفأ». *Men in the Sun* uses «راح» of a brother who is alive. Pin a euphemism with a ritual noun or a mourning act in the same paragraph («العزاء والدموع»; *Celestial Bodies*).
  - **Consolation and fatalism** («اصبر»; «الأعمار بيد الله») mark feeling and frame, not fact.
  - **A noun can carry the change:** الجسد becomes الجثة once the narration knows (*Men in the Sun*). A name becomes «المرحوم».
  - **An honorific in passing** («المرحوم فلان») is enough for a minor figure. It is too little for a main character's parent, who needs at least one plain clause (*Palace of Desire*).
  - **Each survivor's word for the dead** can encode the relationship: «مصرع», «المرحوم», «المرحوم» in guillemets, «الشهيد» (*Palace of Desire*).
  - **Death-watch formulae** carry near-certainty while the outcome stays open: «ادع بالرحمة للكبار», said over the sick, is the prayer for the dead (*Palace of Desire*).
  - **Euphemisms of illness** («إن عدوك راقد»; a family's «حادث») show how danger is handled socially. The doctor's plain word can follow (*Palace of Desire*).
  - **An aposiopesis** (". .") placed exactly where the dead son's name would fall marks the wound without narration (*Palace of Desire*).
  - **The plain verb in a character's mouth** («مات… حقًا»; «ابننا قُتل») is often where acceptance happens. It can follow an implied moment within a page (*Palace Walk*).
  - **A formula is a weak sole carrier in a book that uses formulae in jest and courtesy** elsewhere. Add a sound, a gesture or a plain verb (*Palace Walk*).
  - **Ritual withheld or inverted at a death** (prayers turned to curses, a grave refused) reads as a verdict, but only when the book gave ritual to earlier deaths (*Men in the Sun*).
  - **Show mourning customs as practice and dispute,** not explanation: who may wail and who may not, men's rites against women's, nights of the wake, Friday visits to the grave, black clothing (*Palace Walk*). A custom stated as a rule that binds explains behaviour without a lecture: the dead must be buried first, so no one consoles the son at the hospital (*Celestial Bodies*).
- **Fear in Arabic sentence forms** (the techniques themselves are in `horror.md` §6):
  - the hedge «رأيت، أو خُيّل إليّ أنّي رأيت..»;
  - escalation by «بل» ("by the tens, rather the hundreds"), then a short sentence of absence;
  - progressive naming carried by the ".." pause.
- **Contracts and oaths** borrow the diction of customary law or scripture («الدية في رقبتي.. والعوض والسداد في ذمتي», *Khawf 3*), so the weight is felt without exposition.
- **In an invented world without the reader's faith,** give its people their own oaths and curses («أقسم» with no name of God; «اللعنة على…»), and keep God-language for the characters who bring it from our world (*Amarita*, *Wadi al-Dhi'ab*). The contrast marks the worlds with no exposition (`voice-and-dialogue.md` §9).
- **Scripture and sacred text:** an allusion that carries a hidden meaning works better than decorative quotation. Mark real quotations typographically. Attach an echo to the literal situation, as with an abandoned house that is «خاوية على عروشها» (*Amarita*), and never mix two images in one simile.
- **Puns and jokes** are built on native double senses (مسكون "inhabited/haunted") or sound play (الحدس / العدس). Rebuild an English joke this way, or drop it (`translation.md` §6.6).
- **Persuasion** is made visible with «لذلك» or «إذًا» before the conclusion (`voice-and-dialogue.md` §5).
- **Collocation:** Arabic metaphors need Arabic verbs. Check every image for an English calque (`translation.md` §6.6).

## 9. Punctuation and typography
- **Use Arabic marks:** «،» «؛» «؟». Use ؟! and !! sparingly; popular fiction uses them heavily, and literary fiction rarely.
- **".." is the popular-fiction pause.** Per 1,000 words it ranges from about 10 (*Arsis*) through about 40 (*Khawf 2–3*) to over 100 (*Ard Zikola*). The same author's later *Wadi al-Dhi'ab* uses almost none: it punctuates with commas and full stops and adds more literary connectives («حينذاك», «بيد أنّ», «وقتئذٍ»).
  - Choose by register. Overuse flattens rhythm and blurs sentence boundaries, since every beat becomes a pause.
  - Use full stops and commas for logic, and reserve ".." for trailing speech, suspension, hesitation and montage.
  - Never use it in a sentence that delivers a shock.
- **Arabic has no italics in normal typesetting.** Mark a mentioned word with quotation marks plus a noun (كلمة، حرف، صيغة).
- **Use parentheses for names** only if you apply them consistently throughout.
- **Choose Arabic-Indic or Western digits** once per book.
- **Keep layout honest.** A page-bottom blank can read as a section break.

## 10. Composing in Arabic: a procedure
1. **Design the story with the shared craft:** the story engine, information plan, cast and scenes (SKILL.md), plus the references the story needs. This step is the same in every language.
2. **Voice and register plan** (§2): the narrator's person, distance and height; each character's dialogue register; the gloss policy.
3. **Naming plan** (§3, §7): names, labels for the unnamed, names that need markers, epithets that won't collide.
4. **Dialogue convention** (§4): tags before the line, or labels; the dash or not; guillemet uses.
5. **Intended-uncertainty list** for the story's secrets (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §2).
6. **Draft scene by scene:**
   - a first sentence or two with time, place, cast and action;
   - verb-first events;
   - each hinge legible: decide when the reader should know and which channel carries it (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §6);
   - one enigmatic signal per beat.
7. **Passes,** in the canonical order of `clarity-and-orientation.md` §12:
   1. links at hinges;
   2. background;
   3. reference and attribution;
   4. mechanism language;
   5. names and homographs;
   6. collocation.

   Then register consistency, and punctuation (the ".." and "!" budget).
8. **Cold read** (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §12).

## 12. Arabic for the ear: documentary and narrated voiceover
The shared method for narration a listener follows on first hearing is in `documentary.md` §7–11. The documentaries studied for it were all in English, so this section carries their findings into Arabic through the rest of this layer. It was then tested on an original sample, with a cold listen and an independent audit, and revised where they found problems. It has not been tested on Arabic documentaries or with a native narrator reading aloud. Read every script aloud before trusting it. What changes in Arabic:
- **Register.** For viewers across the Arab world, narrate in contemporary فصحى. Narrating in a dialect is a deliberate choice for a local audience. Keep dialect for quoted voices: interviews, testimony, dramatised lines. When a dialect quotation carries a fact the story needs, state the fact in the narration's فصحى as well, before or after the quotation, so viewers from other regions keep it.
- **Events verb-first, one fact to a sentence at a turn.** «انطفأ ضوء المنارة قبل الفجر.» A shock is one flat sentence, as in §5.
- **Keep the verb near its subject, and break long construct chains.** A listener holds «دفتر الأجور» but loses «دفتر حساب أجور عمّال منجم الشركة». Split it: «كان للشركة دفترٌ تسجّل فيه أجور عمّال المنجم.»
- **The pause silences the case ending.** In writing, an ending can show which noun a final adjective belongs to. At a pause the ending is gone, and the ear attaches the adjective to the nearest noun. «جاء ابنُ عمّ الحارس الأكبر» is heard as "the elder keeper". Rebuild it: «جاء ابنُ عمّ الحارس، وهو أكبرُ منه سنًّا».
- **Relative clauses one deep.** «الرجل الذي اشترى الخريطة التي رسمها الدليل الذي…» cannot be followed by ear. Give each clause its own sentence.
- **Connectives that name the relation:** لأنّ، لذلك، لكنّ، ثمّ، حتى، فـ. «حيث» used as an all-purpose link blurs place, time and cause when heard, and so does a chain of «وقد». Use the connective that says what the relation is.
- **The nearest-person rule is stricter by ear** (§3). Where two people of the same gender are in play, repeat the role or the name instead of a pronoun. Reported speech is where this goes wrong most often: «قال الحارس إنّه نام» can be heard as either man sleeping. Write «إنّه هو نفسه نام», or use the name.
- **Names.**
  - Put the role before the name at first mention: «الحارس مبارك».
  - A name that is also a word (مبارك، سعيد، هلال، نور) is heard as the word unless something announces it. Audio has no quotation marks or parentheses to help. A role noun at first mention does not protect the later mentions. Wherever the bare name can be heard as the word, repeat the role or introduce the name with «واسمُه…». That includes inside a construct phrase («علامةُ X»), as a bare subject, and after «هو».
  - Avoid two names that sound alike in one stretch (سالم وسليم).
  - **General or particular.** A definite singular after a general plural is heard as the story's own man: «وإذا مات الرجل…» after «كان الغوّاصون…» sounds like the protagonist. For the general case use an indefinite noun: «وإذا مات غوّاصٌ…».
  - **A title is not a name.** «كان ربّان المركب يُسمّى النوخذة» can be heard as the captain's name. Introduce a title as a title: «وكان ربّان المركب يُعرف بالنوخذة».
- **Words with a rival meaning by ear.** «صاحب» is owner or companion. «السطح» is a deck, a roof or the sea's surface. «الدَّين» can be heard as «الدِّين». «يتبع» can be read as «يُتبَع», "to be continued". Choose the precise noun («مالك المركب»، «ظهر المركب»), or vowel the deciding letter.
- **Terms.**
  - Introduce one local term at a time, with what it does in the same breath, then keep that word: «العتّال، الرجل الذي يحمل البضائع على ظهره من السفينة إلى الرصيف». Don't alternate synonyms for the same thing.
  - A term known only from an English transliteration gives neither its Arabic spelling, nor its regional form, nor its vowels. Check local terms in Arabic sources, or enter them in the ledger as unverified.
- **Write numbers and dates out for the narrator.**
  - Number agreement is where readers stumble when they meet digits: 3–10 take the opposite gender, 11–99 take an accusative singular noun, and hundreds and compounds add more traps.
  - Write numbers in words, vowelled where the case matters.
  - Round them («نحو ثلاثين مركبًا») when exactness does not matter.
  - Keep to one calendar; if both are needed, give the second once.
- **Vowel for the performer, not for the page.** Vowel names, foreign words, passives that could be read as actives (قُتِل), duals (يدَي), and any word whose ending changes the meaning. Full vowelling slows the read.
- **Let the pause carry the weight.** A word that ends a sentence is read in pause. Put the word that carries the news where the pause falls: «وفي آخر الليل، انطفأ ضوء المنارة.» Commas mark the breaths.
- **Voices and sources.**
  - Introduce a quotation by its speaker and its kind, and name a source by what it is: «يقول تقرير المعتمد البريطاني:»، «يقول مقالٌ نُشر عام…».
  - Close each quotation audibly, with a change of voice or the narrator resuming with a plain line of consequence.
  - Mark claim and doubt with words the ear catches: «بحسب…»، «يُقال إنّ…»، «تذكر الروايات…»، «لا نعرف على وجه اليقين…»، «الأرجح أنّ…».
  - Keep the source's modality: «كان يُنتظَر منه أن…» is not «كان يفعل»; «كثيرًا ما» is not «في الغالب».
- **Fiction and history.**
  - Mark the strand with «في الرواية…»، «تتخيّل الرواية…»، «أمّا تاريخيًّا…»، «تقول السجلّات…» (`documentary.md` §15).
  - The habitual past («كان النوخذة يسجّل…») is heard as how the trade worked. Keep the story's own objects out of it, or mark them as the story's.
- **Rebuild English devices in Arabic terms.** Direct address, signposts and recaps carried over from an English draft sound translated: «احتفظوا بصورة هذا الحبل»، «ويحكي معها»، «أمّا هل بقي حيًّا». Find the Arabic way of doing the same job, or drop the device.
- **A formula is not the fact.** In narration, a religious formula marks feeling. It does not tell the listener that someone died (§8). Say the fact first, and let the formula belong to the people in the story.
- **Time the Arabic aloud.** The same section does not run the same length in Arabic and in English, and their word counts are not comparable. Time the Arabic script at the narrator's pace and re-cut the picture to it, or, if the picture is locked, shorten the lines that run long. When you cannot read it aloud, estimate it separately and say so.

## 13. Checklist
- [ ] The story was designed with the shared craft references; this layer was added, not substituted.
- [ ] Voice and register plan written: the narrator's person, distance and height; each character's dialogue register; the gloss policy.
- [ ] Naming plan: names that are words are marked, and none sits in the accusative trap; no epithet is shared by two people; labels change with knowledge, with a bridge.
- [ ] Every narrator switch marked the same way; every continuation paragraph of a long speech marked.
- [ ] One dialogue convention (tags before the line, or labels). No English-style tags after the line in group scenes. Attribution frequency per `clarity-and-orientation.md` §7.
- [ ] Every scene's first sentence or two give time, place, cast and a first action. Flashbacks are marked in and out.
- [ ] Every pronoun and omitted subject checked against the nearest-person rule.
- [ ] Each hinge is legible for this readership: a plain clause, or an act, ritual noun or reaction that settles the fact. Euphemisms and rituals of dying are pinned by a second channel, unless the doubt is designed and labelled. One enigmatic signal per beat.
- [ ] Homographs near plot words settled. Selective vowelling only.
- [ ] Punctuation budget chosen (".." and "!") to suit the register.
- [ ] Cold read logged, with every accidental stumble fixed.
- [ ] For narration heard rather than read (§12): the role repeated wherever a word-name could be heard as the word; no final adjective that the pause leaves without a noun; reported speech checked for two candidates; one term to a thing; numbers written out and vowelled for the narrator; no «حيث» chains or deep relative clauses; English devices rebuilt in Arabic terms; quotations closed audibly; the script read aloud and timed, or the timing marked as an estimate.
