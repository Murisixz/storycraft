# Viewpoint and narration: who tells, from when, and what they let the reader know

In prose, viewpoint is the main lever on information and emotion. It decides what the reader can perceive, what they
can infer, whom they feel *with*, and whom they watch *from above*. Choosing a narrator is choosing an information
strategy and a voice at the same time.

## 1. The main options and their costs
| Viewpoint | Gives | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **First person, retrospective** | a voice with hindsight, selection and judgment; intimacy; the narrator's present as a second story | the narrator knows the ending, so withholding must be motivated; suspense about survival is reduced |
| **First person, close to the action** | immediacy; the reader learns with the narrator | limited to what one person can perceive; exposition is hard |
| **Close third, single** | intimacy plus flexibility of distance | one consciousness limits irony |
| **Close third, rotating** | dramatic irony between minds; several lives lived from inside | each viewpoint needs its own want and voice; switching can dissipate tension |
| **Omniscient** | free movement; summary across lives and years; ironic commentary | intimacy and suspense are easy to lose; the narrator's personality must earn its presence |
| **Objective (camera)** | no access to thought; the reader infers everything | interiority must be carried by action and dialogue |
| **Second person / addressed** | intimacy, accusation, confession | wears thin at length; an addressee needs a reason |

Choose by effect: whose feelings the reader must live inside, what the reader must not know yet, and what irony the
story needs.

## 2. The narrating moment: who tells, from when, and why
- **Fix when the telling happens.** A narrator speaking years later knows how things ended, and that knowledge colours
  every sentence: hindsight remarks, regret, irony, foreshadowing. Decide what the narrator knows at the moment of
  telling and keep it consistent.
- **Give the telling an occasion.** Why is this person telling this now? (A confession before an ending, a
  justification, an attempt to understand, a duty, a last chance to remember.) An occasion makes the act of telling
  dramatic and can itself be revealed late.
- **The narrator's present can be a second story.** What is happening to the narrator *now*, while they remember, can
  carry its own pressure and change. Keep it light or let it build, but don't forget it.
- **Hindsight is a budget.** Each "I didn't know then that…" spends suspense to buy dread. A few, placed at turning
  points, are powerful. Many become a tic and leak the plot (information-design.md §10).

## 3. Narrative distance
- **Distance** runs from far (summary, historical overview, the narrator commenting from above) to close (inside one
  mind, moment by moment, in that person's vocabulary).
- **Move closer** for decisions, perceptions, blows and temptations. **Pull back** for transitions, years, irony and
  breadth.
- **Free indirect style** blends the narrator's voice with the character's: the narration takes on the character's
  words, judgments and errors without quotation marks. It's the most flexible tool in close third. Use it to
  characterize, to carry exposition coloured by attitude, and to create irony when the reader sees past the character's
  judgment.
- **Control the shifts.** A sudden jump in distance can work as a cut (from inside a mind to a cold summary of what
  happened next). Drift without intent blurs the scene and the information state.

## 4. Multiple viewpoints
- **Give every viewpoint character a want, a voice and a reason to be a viewpoint.** If a viewpoint adds only
  information, consider giving that information another way.
- **Rotation creates irony.** When the reader has been inside two minds, they know what each hides from the other.
  Place scenes so that this knowledge produces dread or tenderness.
- **The same event from two minds** can show how each misreads it. That is one of the strongest ways to dramatize a
  misunderstanding that drives the plot.
- **Switch at moments of maximum question,** not in the middle of a feeling. A switch is a cut; it should raise
  curiosity about the line just left.
- **Antagonist viewpoints** buy dread and understanding, but they risk giving away plans and identities. Decide what the
  antagonist chapters may reveal and hide (§9).
- **Balance the lines.** If one viewpoint is much weaker, readers skim it. Each line needs its own forward pull.

## 5. Disclosure control: how a narrator manages what the reader learns
A narrator can let the reader sense far more than is stated. These are the main instruments, and each has a cost:
- **Euphemism and inherited vocabulary.** A narrator using the words she was taught (official, polite, childish or
  institutional) names terrible things softly. The reader feels the gap between word and thing. This is the core of
  much dramatic irony in first person.
- **Deferral.** "I'll come to that." "But that was later." The narrator signals that something matters and postpones
  it. It creates curiosity cheaply, but repeated without payoff it becomes coy.
- **Digression and association.** Memory moves sideways, so the narrator follows one recollection into another. Order
  becomes a tool: a crucial fact can sit inside a digression, unemphasized.
- **Misreading.** A narrator who misinterprets what she sees (too young, too insecure, too invested) passes her error
  to the reader, who shares it until a reveal re-sees it. The misreading must come from character, and the true
  reading must be available on reread.
- **Selective memory and admitted uncertainty.** "I may be wrong about this." "I've thought about it since." These
  build trust and, used strategically, can hide what the narrator chooses not to examine.
- **Hindsight remarks** (§2). Dread at the price of suspense.
- **Withholding by choice.** If a narrator who knows withholds, the reader must accept the reason (shame, protection,
  a dramatic purpose the frame later explains), or the withholding reads as the author's trick.

## 6. Limited, unreliable and self-deceiving narrators
| Kind | What's wrong with the account | How the reader can tell | Main effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Naive** | doesn't understand what they report | details the reader understands better than the narrator does | irony, tenderness, dread |
| **Self-deceiving** | can't face something about themselves | contradictions, over-insistence, others' reactions | moral irony; a late self-recognition |
| **Partial** | wasn't present, or knows only one side | gaps, hearsay, conflicting testimony | mystery; Rashomon-style investigation |
| **Lying** | deliberately misleads | inconsistencies, later evidence | a twist; must be fair on reread |
| **Fabricating or authorial** | the narrator is shaping the story itself (rewriting, inventing, sparing someone) | a frame that exposes the act of telling | recontextualization of everything before |

- **Fairness rules:** an unreliable narrator may misjudge, omit or lie. The *text* must still contain the truth for a
  careful reader: details the narrator reports without understanding, other characters' reactions, physical facts.
- **Plan the moment of exposure.** Gradual (the reader slowly sees past the narrator), sudden (a late reveal), or never
  fully (the reader is left weighing). Each needs planting (information-design.md §6–7).
- **The narrator's motive is the deepest layer.** Why this version? What does it protect? The best unreliability
  comes from need, not from authorial cleverness.

## 7. Frames, embedded narratives and documents
- **Frames** (a present that contains the telling of a past) give the past a reason to be told and a listener to
  change. Close the frame with consequence: the telling should alter the present situation or the teller.
- **Embedded narratives** (a story within the story: a found manuscript, a confession, a book) can carry the backstory in
  another voice. Give the embedded text its own author, purpose, gaps and distortions. Long embedded texts suspend the
  main story; budget them, break them, or let the main story resume at moments of strain (scene-and-summary.md §6).
- **Documents** (letters, diaries, reports, transcripts, notes) give variety and evidence. They're strongest when the
  reader must read *between* their lines.
- **A reveal about authorship** ("who has been writing this?") recontextualizes the whole book. It must be planted (in
  the way the narrator knows things, in odd omniscience, in style) and must pay off emotionally, not just
  intellectually (endings.md §10).
- **Explanatory documents at the end** (a confession, a summation) resolve a puzzle at the cost of drama. Keep them as
  short as the puzzle allows and give the last beat to a person.

## 8. Unusual narrators
A child, a dead person, a collective "we", an animal, an institution, a future historian:
- **Define the limits precisely.** What can this narrator perceive, know, enter or affect? Keep the rules consistent.
  Breaking them for convenience destroys trust.
- **Use the displacement.** The unusual position should show what an ordinary narrator couldn't: the dead seeing the
  living grieve, a child seeing adults' lies without understanding them.
- **Give the narrator their own want and change.** A narrator who only observes becomes a camera. Even a dead narrator
  needs something to want, lose and let go of.

## 9. Viewpoint and fair play
- **In mysteries, the narration may not lie about objective facts** (mystery-and-clues.md §9). With multiple
  viewpoints, entering a culprit's mind while hiding their guilt is the classic risk. If you enter a culprit's mind,
  the thoughts shown must be true thoughts that can be read innocently. Ambiguous phrasing is fair; a false thought is
  not.
- **Head-hopping** (moving between many minds in one scene) can hide a culprit among many perspectives. Each mind
  shown must keep consistent with its hidden truth.
- **A narrator's ignorance must be consistent.** A narrator can't know at one point what they "learn" later unless the
  telling's hindsight is explicit.

## 10. Failure patterns
| Symptom | Cause | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| "I saw the twist coming" | hindsight remarks or a knowing second voice leaked it | budget hindsight; audit leaks (revision.md §6) |
| "Why didn't the narrator just tell us?" | withholding without a motive | give the withholding a reason in character, or drop it |
| Viewpoint characters blur | same voice in every head | voice dossiers (voice-and-dialogue.md §2) |
| Head-hopping confusion | uncontrolled shifts in a scene | one mind per scene, or deliberate, signalled omniscience |
| A frame that goes nowhere | the telling doesn't change the teller or the listener | let the present line pay off |
| Embedded document stalls the book | a long account without interruption or a voice of its own | break it up; give it gaps, an author and a purpose; resume the present at strain |
| An unusual narrator who only watches | no want of their own | give them a want and a cost |

## 11. Checklist
- [ ] Is the viewpoint chosen for the effect wanted (inside whom, hiding what, ironic where)?
- [ ] Is the narrating moment fixed, and is the narrator's knowledge consistent with it?
- [ ] Is there an occasion for the telling, and does the narrator's present change?
- [ ] Is hindsight budgeted?
- [ ] Is distance moved on purpose: close for decisions, far for transitions?
- [ ] Does each viewpoint character have a want, a voice and a reason to be a viewpoint?
- [ ] Does the narrator's disclosure (euphemism, deferral, digression, misreading) leave the truth available on reread?
- [ ] Are frames closed with consequence, and embedded texts budgeted and voiced?
- [ ] Is fair play kept in every mind the reader enters?
