# Sources

Storycraft's guidance is written in its own words and synthesizes the sources below. Short quotations are attributed.
Where a source was known only through a summary or secondary account, that is stated, so users can verify load-bearing
claims.

## Craft books read in full
- **Robert McKee, *Story: Substance, Structure, Style, and the Principles of Screenwriting*** (ReganBooks/HarperCollins,
  1997). Story events and value change; the gap between expectation and result; the risk test; empathy vs sympathy;
  the five-part design (inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution); scene design and
  analysis; composition, pacing, rhythm and tempo; progression (social, personal, symbolic, ironic); crisis as true
  dilemma; "Meaning Produces Emotion"; the principle of antagonism; exposition; curiosity and concern; mystery,
  suspense and dramatic irony as audience positions; true vs cheap surprise; false mystery; coincidence; point of view;
  character and cast design; dialogue and text; a writer's method. (Read from a scanned copy via OCR; the scan lacks
  the back matter, i.e. the suggested readings and filmography.)
- **David Howard & Edward Mabley, *The Tools of Screenwriting*** (St. Martin's Griffin, 1993/1995; introduction by
  Frank Daniel). "Somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it"; audience participation through
  hope vs fear; objective vs subjective drama; main tension, culmination and resolution; theme revealed in the
  resolution; unity; exposition rules; characterization and objectives; development through escalating attempts;
  dramatic irony and the revelation→recognition obligation; preparation and aftermath (including by contrast); planting
  and payoff; advertising and elements of the future; plausibility and inevitability; activity vs action; dialogue;
  visuals; the dramatic scene and its rewrite questions; sixteen film analyses.
- **Edwidge Danticat, *The Art of Death: Writing the Final Story*** (Graywolf Press, 2017; The Art of series), read
  for version 4. Complete. Her readings of how writers handle a death were compared with the novels, and her advice is
  cited where the novels confirm or complicate it (`clarity-and-orientation.md` §6; `novel-studies.md` §8).

## Arabic novels studied for version 3
Each was read in full, in order, in chunks of about 3,000–5,000 Arabic words, with a note per chunk, a whole-book
analysis and a separate analysis of its Arabic prose. Every quotation in the notes was machine-checked against the
text. The texts are OCR of the supplied PDFs (most were scans): Tesseract's Arabic model, checked against the page
images, with skipped lines recovered and dialogue layout verified on the images. Quotations are therefore verified
against the OCR, not against print. The skill draws principles and contrasts from these books
(`novel-studies.md`, `arabic-writing.md`). It reproduces no plots, characters or prose beyond short attributed
quotations.
- **Osamah Al Muslim (أسامة المسلم), *Khawf* (خوف) 1, 2 and 3**: a trilogy of occult-horror memoir (Markaz al-Adab
  al-Arabi, Dammam; volumes 1–2 carry King Fahd National Library cataloguing). Volumes 1 and 2 are complete,
  including their afterwords. **The scan of volume 3 lacks printed pages 306–307** (the end of the last scene with
  the devil and the narrator's return home). Those pages were not read, and nothing in the skill depends on them.
- **Ahmed Al Hamdan (أحمد آل حمدان), *Arsis* (آرسس) parts 1 and 2**: a Saudi thriller with a supernatural time
  gate. Both complete. Both are re-typeset e-book editions, so their page breaks differ from print. Part 2 is from
  Markaz al-Adab al-Arabi, first edition 2024.
- **Amr Abdel Hamid (عمرو عبد الحميد), *Ard Zikola* (أرض زيكولا), *Amarita* (أماريتا) and *Wadi al-Dhi'ab
  al-Mansiyya* (وادي الذئاب المنسية)**: an Egyptian fantasy series (Asir al-Kutub; *Ard Zikola* legal deposit
  2010/19834). All three complete. The scan of *Wadi al-Dhi'ab* drops thirteen blank pages before chapter openings and the front
  matter. Its reader checked every page-number jump against the page images and found no text missing.

Also used for version 3: an unpublished story and its Arabic edition, studied at the author's request. An
editor's critique of it was tested claim by claim, and a cold read of the Arabic edition located why a native reader
could not follow it. The lessons are generalized in `clarity-and-orientation.md`, `translation.md` and
`mystery-and-clues.md` §18–19. Nothing from the story is quoted, and every example in the skill is invented.

## Books studied for version 4
Version 4 read every supplied book with one central question: when is the reader meant to understand a decisive event, and does it have room to land? The books were also read for the whole craft (voice, dialogue, scene, pacing, suspense, clues, investigation, reveals, relationships, consequences, historical atmosphere and continuity). Each was read in full, in order and in chunks, with:
- a note per chunk;
- a ledger of decisive events;
- a whole-book analysis;
- for the Arabic books, a separate analysis of the prose.

Every quotation in the notes was machine-checked against the text, and all were found: 12,769 across the seven novels, Danticat's book and the *Hamnet* sample. Scans were read through OCR checked against the page images, as in version 3. The English titles of the Arabic novels are used for convenience: the Arabic originals were read, not the translations.

**In English:**
- **Wilkie Collins, *The Moonstone*** (1868; a Feedbooks PDF made from the Project Gutenberg text). Complete, from the Prologue to the Epilogue.
- **Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall*** (Henry Holt, first US edition, 2009; a scan). Complete, printed pages 3–532. Seven blank versos were not scanned and two pages were scanned twice. No text is missing.
- **Hilary Mantel, *Bring Up the Bodies*** (Henry Holt e-book, 2012). The complete text. The two family trees are absent from the file.

**In Arabic:**
- **Ghassan Kanafani (غسان كنفاني), *Rijal fi al-Shams* (رجال في الشمس; *Men in the Sun*)** (first published 1963; Rimal Publications, Cyprus, 2013 edition, 2015 printing). Complete, pages 7–109.
- **Jokha Alharthi (جوخة الحارثي), *Sayyidat al-Qamar* (سيدات القمر; *Celestial Bodies*)** (Dar al-Adab, Beirut). The copyright page says first edition 2010, but the half-title mentions the 2019 prize for the English translation, so this is a later printing. Complete, pages 7–223.
- **Naguib Mahfouz (نجيب محفوظ), *Bayn al-Qasrayn* (بين القصرين; *Palace Walk*)** (first published 1956; Dar al-Shorouk, 2006 edition, 10th printing 2015). Complete, pages 5–579.
- **Naguib Mahfouz, *Qasr al-Shawq* (قصر الشوق; *Palace of Desire*)** (dated 1957 in the publisher's list; Dar al-Shorouk, 2006 edition, 6th printing 2013). Complete, pages 5–542. The print itself repeats about five lines on page 291 and drops a word on page 528. A reader of the two volumes wrote a series analysis.

Also used for version 4: a reader's feedback on an unpublished story, about the moment a decisive event is understood. It is generalized in `clarity-and-orientation.md` §6. Nothing from the story is quoted, and every example in the skill is invented. Other books on the version 4 reading list were not available and were not used.

## Documentary transcripts studied for version 4.2
Version 4.2 adds `documentary.md`, a workflow for long-form narrated documentaries, from caption transcripts of
twenty-one videos on three YouTube channels. Each transcript was read in full and in order, in chunks of about 6,000
words, with:
- a note per chunk;
- a ledger;
- a map of the whole video: its opening, question ladder, sections, turns, ending, information placement, withholding,
  attribution, sentence-level craft, room to absorb, and what the transcript cannot show.

Each channel was then analysed on its own, counting each video once, before the three were compared. Every quotation in
the notes and analyses was machine-checked against the captions, and all were found within 45 seconds of the time cited.
The skill quotes none of them, and it reuses no creator's wording or recurring structures.

What captions cannot show is treated as unknown throughout: the picture, the music, the editing, and who voiced each
reading. Pause lengths come from caption gaps, so they are minimums.

- **Brick Immortar** (maritime and engineering disasters; six videos):
  - *INFLECTION POINT: The Tragedy of SS Marine Electric* (captions begin at 0:02:30);
  - *A COLLISION OF BLAME: The Total Collapse of Baltimore's Key Bridge*;
  - *FROZEN, DARK & OVERWHELMED: The Loss of FV Destination* (captions begin at 0:00:34);
  - *OFFSHORE NIGHTMARE: The Collapse of Texas Tower 4*;
  - *INSTANTLY UNDERWATER: The Sinking of Towboat Ricky Robinson* and *TRAGIC DISAPPEARANCE: The SS Marine Sulphur
    Queen*, the channel's shorter formats.

  Caption spans run from 28 minutes to 3 hours 14 minutes.
- **Fall of Civilizations** (narrated histories, also published as a podcast; five episodes):
  - *17. Carthage - Empire of the Phoenicians*;
  - *18. Egypt - Fall of the Pharaohs*;
  - *19. The Mongols - Terror of the Steppe*;
  - *20. Persia - An Empire in Ashes*;
  - *21. Majapahit - Empire of the Islands*.

  Caption spans run from 3 hours 39 minutes to 6 hours 47 minutes. *Majapahit* was supplied twice: one upload's track
  looked automatic, with hundreds of misspelled names. It was compared word for word with the human-made track, which
  was the one read, and no judgement about wording rests on it. The credits name readings in other languages that the
  English captions do not contain.
- **LEMMiNO** (investigations, unsolved puzzles and histories; ten files):

  - two Q&A videos (*4,000,000 - Q&A*, *5,000,000 - Q&A*), used only for what the creator says about his own process
    and kept apart from what the films show;
  - a two-minute charity promo (*Seas #TeamSeas*).

## Primary texts and essays (read directly)
- Aristotle, *Poetics* (S. H. Butcher trans.), MIT Internet Classics Archive: reversal and recognition; pity and fear;
  surprise that follows as cause and effect; unity.
- E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), ch. V "The Plot", Project Gutenberg: story vs plot; mystery as
  suspended time-sequence; "no loose ends."
- Edgar Allan Poe, review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* (1842) and "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846),
  eapoe.org: single effect; writing toward the dénouement.
- William Archer, *Play-Making: A Manual of Craftsmanship* (1912), Project Gutenberg: curiosity vs interest;
  foreshadowing without forestalling; tension and its suspension; the obligatory scene; peripety.
- S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories" (1928); Ronald Knox, "Detective Story Decalogue" (via a
  1939 excerpt): fair play (principles kept; dated rules discarded).
- Alexander Mackendrick, teaching handouts (The Sticking Place): exposition in conflict; cause and effect as falling
  dominoes; "Drama is expectation mingled with uncertainty."
- Lee Child, "A Simple Way to Create Suspense" (New York Times, 2012): ask a question and delay the answer.
- Craig Mazin, *Scriptnotes* ep. 403 "How to Write a Movie" (transcript, 2019): theme as the central dramatic argument;
  structure as a symptom of the character's relationship to it.
- Andrew Stanton, "The Clues to a Great Story" (TED 2012; third-party transcript): make me care; 2+2; promise.
- Emma Coats's Pixar story rules (as compiled by Aerogramme Writers' Studio).
- Henry Jenkins, "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" (2004): evoked, enacted, embedded, emergent narrative.
- Harvey Smith & Matthias Worch, "What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling" (GDC 2010 session description and
  Nieman Storyboard write-up).
- Pete Docter on *Up*'s opening (The Ringer, 2022).
- George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity" (Psychological Bulletin, 1994): information-gap account.
- Mary Beth Oliver & Anne Bartsch, "Appreciation as Audience Response" (Human Communication Research, 2010).
- Silvia Knobloch-Westerwick et al. on tragedy viewers (Ohio State University release on the Communication Research
  study, 2012).
- Brandon Sanderson's 2025 lecture guides (publisher staff write-ups): promises, progress, payoff; signposting.
- YouTube Official Blog, "Tall updates coming to Shorts" (Oct 2024): Shorts up to 3 minutes.
