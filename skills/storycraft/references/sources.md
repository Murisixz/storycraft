# Sources

Storycraft's guidance is written in its own words and synthesizes the sources below. Short quotations are attributed.
Where a source was known only through a summary or secondary account, that is stated, so users can verify load-bearing
claims.

## Books read in full
- **Robert McKee, *Story: Substance, Structure, Style, and the Principles of Screenwriting*** (ReganBooks/HarperCollins,
  1997). Story events and value change; the gap between expectation and result; the risk test; empathy vs sympathy;
  the five-part design (inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution); scene design and
  analysis; composition, pacing, rhythm and tempo; progression (social, personal, symbolic, ironic); crisis as true
  dilemma; "Meaning Produces Emotion"; the principle of antagonism; exposition; curiosity and concern; mystery,
  suspense and dramatic irony as audience positions; true vs cheap surprise; false mystery; coincidence; point of view;
  character and cast design; dialogue and text; a writer's method. (Read from a scanned copy via OCR; the scan lacks
  the back matter, i.e. the suggested readings and filmography.)
- **David Howard & Edward Mabley, *The Tools of Screenwriting*** (St. Martin's Griffin, 1993/1995; introduction by
  Frank Daniel). "Somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it"; audience participation through
  hope vs fear; objective vs subjective drama; main tension, culmination and resolution; theme revealed in the
  resolution; unity; exposition rules; characterization and objectives; development through escalating attempts;
  dramatic irony and the revelation→recognition obligation; preparation and aftermath (including by contrast); planting
  and payoff; advertising and elements of the future; plausibility and inevitability; activity vs action; dialogue;
  visuals; the dramatic scene and its rewrite questions; sixteen film analyses.

## Secondary summaries used (not the original books)
- John Fraim's outline of **John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*** (FSG, 2007): premise and designing principle; seven key
  steps; need vs desire; ghost; opponent competing for the same goal; character web; moral argument; the 22 steps,
  revelation sequence, apparent defeat, audience revelation, self-revelation and double reversal; scene construction.
- A Bookey app summary of **Donald Maass, *The Emotional Craft of Fiction*** (Writer's Digest Books, 2016). Thin; used
  only for direction.
- A Bookey app summary of **Larry Beinhart, *How to Write a Mystery*** (Ballantine, 1996).

## Primary texts and essays (read directly)
- Aristotle, *Poetics* (S. H. Butcher trans.), MIT Internet Classics Archive: reversal and recognition; pity and fear;
  surprise that follows as cause and effect; unity.
- E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), ch. V "The Plot", Project Gutenberg: story vs plot; mystery as
  suspended time-sequence; "no loose ends."
- Edgar Allan Poe, review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* (1842) and "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846),
  eapoe.org: single effect; writing toward the dénouement.
- William Archer, *Play-Making: A Manual of Craftsmanship* (1912), Project Gutenberg: curiosity vs interest;
  foreshadowing without forestalling; tension and its suspension; the obligatory scene; peripety.
- S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories" (1928); Ronald Knox, "Detective Story Decalogue" (via a
  1939 excerpt): fair play (principles kept; dated rules discarded).
- Alexander Mackendrick, teaching handouts (The Sticking Place): exposition in conflict; cause and effect as falling
  dominoes; "Drama is expectation mingled with uncertainty."
- Lee Child, "A Simple Way to Create Suspense" (New York Times, 2012): ask a question and delay the answer.
- Craig Mazin, *Scriptnotes* ep. 403 "How to Write a Movie" (transcript, 2019): theme as the central dramatic argument;
  structure as a symptom of the character's relationship to it.
- Andrew Stanton, "The Clues to a Great Story" (TED 2012; third-party transcript): make me care; 2+2; promise.
- Emma Coats's Pixar story rules (as compiled by Aerogramme Writers' Studio).
- Henry Jenkins, "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" (2004): evoked, enacted, embedded, emergent narrative.
- Harvey Smith & Matthias Worch, "What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling" (GDC 2010 session description and
  Nieman Storyboard write-up).
- Pete Docter on *Up*'s opening (The Ringer, 2022).
- George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity" (Psychological Bulletin, 1994): information-gap account.
- Mary Beth Oliver & Anne Bartsch, "Appreciation as Audience Response" (Human Communication Research, 2010).
- Silvia Knobloch-Westerwick et al. on tragedy viewers (Ohio State University release on the Communication Research
  study, 2012).
- Brandon Sanderson's 2025 lecture guides (publisher staff write-ups): promises, progress, payoff; signposting.
- YouTube Official Blog, "Tall updates coming to Shorts" (Oct 2024): Shorts up to 3 minutes.

## Known only through secondary accounts (verify before quoting)
- Michael Arndt, "Endings: The Good, the Bad, and the Insanely Great" (external/internal/philosophical stakes; via a No
  Film School article).
- Raymond Chandler, "Casual Notes on the Mystery Novel" ("The solution, once revealed, must seem to have been
  inevitable"; via secondary sources).
- Ernest Hemingway's iceberg theory (*Death in the Afternoon*, via Wikipedia's quotation).
- Brewer & Lichtenstein (1982) structural-affect theory; Meir Sternberg on suspense, curiosity and surprise; Dolf
  Zillmann's disposition theory of suspense; Noël Carroll on the paradox of suspense (via abstracts and summaries).
- Spoiler studies: Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011) and later replications with mixed results (via summaries).
- Scott McCloud on closure; Walter Murch on editing; Jane Alison on narrative shapes; kishōtenketsu (via reviews and
  summaries).
